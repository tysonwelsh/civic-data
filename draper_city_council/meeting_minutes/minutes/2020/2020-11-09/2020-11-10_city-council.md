1.1   LaRayne Day, Chair of the Historic Preservation Commission, thanked the Council for the
      opportunity to meet with them. She said that Draper City established the Draper City
      Historic Preservation Commission in 1985. The purpose of the commission was to work
      with and assist the City in identifying, preserving, protecting, and enhancing historic
      buildings, sites, and monuments within Draper City. She informed the Council about what
      the commission is currently doing. The commission received a certified preservation grant
      through the state and was in the process of funding three restoration projects: the
      Crossgrove home would get a new roof, restoration work on the brick of the side of Sherrie
      Smith’s home had been completed, and work on the porches on the Beck home. Also, the
      commission had been working with homeowners to identify homes that might be eligible
      to be placed on the national register of historic buildings.

      Ms. Day said that she wanted to talk tonight about saving or preserving some part of the
      current Utah State Prison site. Instead, she felt that she needed to focus the attention on
      saving the Draper Historic Preservation Commission. She said that she was taken by
      surprise over the weekend when she received an email from one of the commission
      members who told her that he had received a letter that two of the commission members
      would not have their terms on the commission renewed because the City was moving in a
      different direction. She noted that she had attended a meeting with Mayor Walker and
      David Dobbins, City Manager, on November 3, 2020. The meeting was focused on
      informing Ms. Day that the City had received feedback in a letter from the Point of the
      Mountain Project in response to a survey they had conducted. According to Ms. Day, at
      that meeting, she was instructed that sending such a letter without the blessing of the mayor
      and the Council was inappropriate. The commissioners’ names were not mentioned during
      the meeting and Ms. Day knew nothing of the intent to remove them from the commission.
      Since then, Ms. Day said that both Mayor Walker and Mr. Dobbins had called to apologize
      for the oversight.

      Ms. Day said that she realized that the Council had the authority to appoint or dismiss any
      member of the commission at will. However, she did not see any reason for the removal
      of the two commissioners in question. She said that they had served on the commission
      since 2016. During that time, they had only missed one meeting. She added that these
      commissioners had suggested having plaques made for the Draper Historical Park to let
      visitors know that the area was once a fort and that the first church in Draper had been
      located on that spot of the land.

      Ms. Day asked the Council if the commissioners were being removed from the commission
      because the City was going in a different direction. If so, she wondered what that direction
      would be. She wanted to know if the Council intended to do away with the Draper Historic
      Preservation Commission. She said that there currently were not enough members on the
      commission to hold a meeting. She said that her first instinct after receiving the email

       Scott McCombs, Corner Canyon School District IT Director, thanked the Council for their
       partnership and asked if it would be possible to show a 40-second video clip about
       Eduroam. He played the video that talked about how Eduroam provides secure Wi-Fi
       connections throughout different locations worldwide. Typically, in higher education,
       institutions share their bandwidth to create an Eduroam network. There are two types of
       Eduroam licenses. The first is a service provider that means an institution is not adding
       accounts or users, but rather is letting other users authenticate and use their network. The
       other type of license was for an organization like the Corner Canyon School District who
       was providing Eduroam to all its staff and students. They feel as though Eduroam was a
       tool that could bridge the digital divide. They hoped to put Eduroam in all of their locations
       and partner with the county and cities to ask if they would consider using some of their
       bandwidth to strengthen the network. If a city or community center were allowing
       Eduroam, Canyon’s students would be allowed to go to that network and use the district
       credentials and be able to sign in on the network to connect to the district resources. He
       said that it was a great thing because it worked for Canyon’s students and staff, but also

                                           Page 6 of 16
would work for most other K-12 and higher education students. He said that if they could
push Eduroam out into communities they would not only be helping marginalized students
who are part of the digital divide but would also be providing an opportunity to help all
students. He said that it was known that the digital divide was impacting college students
as well as K-12 students.

Mr. McCombs continued that one of the nice things about Eduroam was that there was not
a lot of costs associated with the service. He said that if an organization allows the district
to partner with them and puts Eduroam on their network, the authenticated user will use a
portion of bandwidth that came with a cost. The bandwidth, however, could be shaped to
be what an organization wanted it to be so that Eduroam did not consume all of the
bandwidth. However, organizations would be volunteering to allow students and staff to
use a portion of their bandwidth. There would be some small overhead in configuring
Eduroam. The University of Utah, Salt Lake Community College, UVU, BYU, and the
Front Runner all have Eduroam. However, it was not until now that it had made it into the
community. He saw this as groundbreaking. The Canyon School District would be the
first in the world to bring Eduroam into the community.

5.0   Public Hearing: Ordinance #1454, City Initiated 2020 Q3 Bulk Text Amendment
      (Legislative Action), on the request of Draper City for approval of a Text Amendment
      to various sections of Title 9 and 17 of the Draper City Municipal Code concerning
      development access points and reducing or eliminating the requirement for paper
      submissions with applications.

5.1   Jennifer Jastremsky, planner, explained that the City had been evaluating development
      access points and how to ensure that the proper number of ingress and egress points existed
      for development. She said it had been reviewed for most of the year since a notice of intent
      was posted to amend the code in March of 2020. Part of the solution that staff came up
      with was to clarify the specific access points that were required, including what constituted
      an access point, and the types of access points based on types of dwelling units. She said
      that she had both a summarized version of the text that outlined the changes, as well as the


                                         Page 13 of 16
detailed text that she could go through with the Council. The type and number of access
points were defined based on dwelling types:

Single-family units:
   a. One access point required for up to 30 units.
   b. Private rights-of-ways can serve up to 20 single-family units.
   c. Widen street width standards of private right-of-way to ensure a 20-foot minimum
       width asphalt.

Two-family units and townhome unit developments:
  a. One access point required for up to 30 units.
  b. Two access points are required for 31 units or more.

Multi-family unit developments other than townhome unit dwellings:

   a. One access point is required for projects with up to 100 dwelling units.
   b. Two access points are required for a project with 101 or more dwelling units.

The code also defined an access point as: A roadway access into a subdivision or
development that provides unrestricted access to residents, the public, and emergency
services. This definition does not prohibit a gated community entrance that is designed to
service residents, guests, and the general public.

She then displayed the more formal text. She said that three different width standards were
set for single-family rights-of-way based on the number of homes.

Single-family dwellings:

       a. 2-5 dwelling units: the current code requires 20 feet total. It is proposed to
          require 24 feet of width: 20 feet of asphalt and then the curb and gutter on either
          side.
       b. 6-10 family lots: the street width would go from 26 feet to 31 feet. That would
          require 26 feet of asphalt with a curb and gutter on either side. It would allow
          for parking on one side of the road and still allow for fire access.
       c. 11-20 family units: the street width would go from 36 feet to 37 feet. 26 feet
          of asphalt would be required with a sidewalk required for these larger
          subdivisions.

Regarding private rights-of-ways, the staff has clarified when parking would be allowed
and what type of parking in section F is based on asphalt width. Section G addressed how
dead-end streets were handled in subdivisions, as well as subdivisions with one access
point. If a subdivision does have a stub street; they would still be limited to the 30- or 100-
unit restriction based on the type of housing. Once the stub road was extended through
there would be potential for additional units. Any temporary turn arounds would have to
meet any engineering or fire standards.

      The second component to the proposed text amendments pertained to paperless
      applications. She said that as a result of COVID-19 and City Hall being closed they had
      to accept digital applications only. The code technically requires paper submittals. The
