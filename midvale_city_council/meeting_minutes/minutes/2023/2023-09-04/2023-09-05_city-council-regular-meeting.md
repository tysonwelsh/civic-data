       A.    DISCUSSION OF AN ORDINANCE TO PROHIBIT THE NEGLIGENT
             DISCHARGE OF FIREWORKS
Aaron McKnight said Midvale City Council members have expressed concern about the
use of fireworks in the City. Some interest was expressed in prohibiting the use of
                                            10
Proceedings of City Council Meeting
September 5, 2023

fireworks on City property. However, in 2018, the Utah State Legislature passed a law
significantly limiting what prohibitions a municipality can impose on the use of fireworks.
That law prevents the City from outright prohibiting the use of fireworks on City property.
However, state law allows a municipality to impose a prohibition against the negligent
discharge of fireworks, which allows the City to enact policy regarding firework safety.

Staff has drafted a proposed ordinance. The proposed ordinance would make it a class
B misdemeanor to negligently or improperly discharge fireworks in the City. While any
negligent use or discharge of fireworks would violate the ordinance, the proposed
amendment identifies several specific scenarios that would violate the ordinance. Those
specific scenarios are (1) using or discharging a firework in such a manner that the
firework projects over or onto someone else's property without their consent, (2) using or
discharging a firework from or into any motor vehicle, or at or near any person, and (3)
using or discharging a firework in such a manner as to cause any building, structure, or
other property to catch fire.

This proposed ordinance is intended to incentivize responsible and safe use of fireworks.
It also will help the City and law enforcement to hold people accountable who are
negligent or reckless in their use of fireworks and who start fires as a result of their use
of fireworks. The penalty of a class B misdemeanor gives prosecutors and judges
sufficient leverage to order and enforce restitution orders, which often becomes an issue
in fireworks cases.


Council Member Dustin Gettel asked Mr. McKnight if it was his opinion that the ordinance
West Valley City has prohibiting the discharge of fireworks on any city property or park
was illegal.
Aaron McKnight said yes, that was his opinion that it was an illegal ordinance.

The Council discussed this issue at length and agreed to move forward with the approval
process for the proposed ordinance.


       B. DISCUSS USE OF DEVELOPMENT AGREEMENTS
Garrett Wilcox said in March 2023, the Utah State Legislature passed HB 406 Land Use,
Development, and Management Act Modifications which amended the language in Utah
Code Ann. $ 10-9a-532. This law had two significant effects on development agreements
for the City. One impact will be addressed in the near future with a proposed code
amendment. The other impact will be discussed tonight.

First, HB 406 prohibited municipalities from requiring a development agreement as a
condition of developing land if a municipality's land use regulations establish all of the
applicable standards for development. A development agreement must address only
issues that are not found within a zone's standards. This will affect several of the City's
current zones. Our Planning Director, Wendelin Knobloch, has already prepared and
coordinated a proposed legislative amendment to address this prohibition. This proposed

                                             11.
Proceedings of City Council Meeting
September 5, 2023

amendment will be brought before the Planning Commission and City Council in the near
future for consideration.

Second, HB 406 requires a municipality to disclose to a developer if a development
agreement restricts any of the developer's rights under state law. lf the municipality fails
to disclose these rights, then any provision pertaining to the undisclosed rights is voided.
This provision is problematic for a number of reasons. One of these problems is the
vagueness of the municipality's obligation. A municipality is required to disclose whether
a development agreement restricts a developer's rights under clearly established state
law. This sounds straightforward. But if a municipality cannot require a development
agreement if its development standards are sufficient to develop a project, then when
would a development agreement that restricts a developer's rights be appropriate? The
developer is already entitled to a review of their application under a municipality's land
use regulations if they submit a complete application and pay their fees (See Utah Code
Ann. $ 10-9a-509(1)(a)). A municipality should not be entering into a development
agreement under these circumstances.

For development agreements linked to rezone applications-as many of ours are-the
developer is not entitled to any rights because the project does not meet the requirements
of the existing zone. ls a municipality supposed to identify the rights the developer has
under the existing zone before a rezone? ls a municipality supposed to identify what rights
the developer could have if the rezone is approved and is not linked to a development
agreement? Does a municipality need to do both? The law is not clear.

This problem is further compounded by the fact that all of the risk is forced upon the
municipality. lf the municipality does not identify the correct rights or all of the rights, then
affected provisions are voided. lf a court disagrees with a municipality's understanding of
the developer's rights, then the municipality loses the benefit of the deal it brokered with
the developer-a deal that the municipality probably would not have entered into
otherwise.

Another significant problem with the new disclosure requirement is the troubling conflict
with municipal attorneys' ethical obligations under Utah's Rules of Professional Conduct.
Rules 1.7(a), 4.2(a), 4.3(a) each prohibit representation of and communication with a
party that is either represented or where a conflict of interest exists. Where a conflict of
interest clearly exists-our attorneys represent the City's interests and not that of a
developer-and where many developers have their own legal counsel, our attorneys are
placed in a difficult ethical dilemma. Wll every developer understand that they are not our
client in this respect? \Mll every developer understand that our disclosure of their rights
is not legal advice? Can we even have these discussions directly with a developer in most
instances? The answers to these questions are unclear. Thus, the disclosure obligation
