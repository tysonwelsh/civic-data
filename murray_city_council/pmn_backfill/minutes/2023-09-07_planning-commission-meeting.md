Mr. Smallwood provided context about the conditional use section of the meeting. He referenced
relevant Utah State Code regarding conditional uses. The code states cities may adopt
ordinances with provisions for conditional uses requiring compliance with objective standards in
the applicable ordinance. Specifically, the code requires approval of a conditional use if
reasonable conditions can be imposed to mitigate reasonably anticipated detrimental effects, in
accordance with applicable standards. The requirement to reasonably mitigate detrimental
effects does not mean the effects must be completely eliminated, just reasonably mitigated. If
there is no way to reasonably condition a proposal to mitigate anticipated detrimental effects,
then denial of the conditional use would be warranted. Mr. Smallwood emphasized these state
code requirements around conditional use approvals and mitigating detriments apply to all three
upcoming agenda items. Essentially, conditional uses must be approved if detriments can be
reasonably mitigated through conditions but may be denied if detrimental effects cannot be
reasonably mitigated.

Craig Cook, President and general counsel for the Humane Society of Utah, has been involved
with the organization for 51 years. In 1989, he found the site the Humane Society facility now

yearly, provides low cost spay/neuter services, vaccinations, fostering, education, and more.
Hundreds of people come through daily to access services and walk dogs on the lawn area. In
contrast, Legacy Materials was recently formed in June 2022 as a limited liability company (LLC)

makes it difficult to hold the company accountable. Legacy has already violated city orders twice
by operating without permits. Yet they advertise themselves as open for business online,
ignoring licensing and permitting requirements. One requirement for a conditional use permit is
that the use cannot be detrimental to health, safety, or welfare of the surrounding area. Mr. Cook
argues a concrete batch plant is normally a toxic operation that has led to litigation elsewhere.

their small operation, conflicting with Legacy's statements. Before approving the permit, Mr. Cook
argues Legacy must conclusively prove their operation will not generate toxic dust that could
harm the Humane Society and surrounding residents. Simply stating it is safe is insufficient given

reasonable doubt there will be zero emissions from the proposed concrete batch plant.

that the Humane Society is essentially a medical facility for animals, with surgical facilities,
inoculations, and specialized air filtration. It is not just a facility that walks dogs - the animals

handling units are designed to filter highway particulates but cannot filter the tiny cement dust.

recently replaced a $50,000 air handler for the adoption area near the proposed batch plant. It
has MERV 8 filtration, but MERV 13 would be required to filter cement dust. He doubts Legacy
can fully mitigate the dust spreading from their open site. If the Humane Society has to upgrade
their equipment due to the batch plant, it will cost hundreds of thousands of dollars. Mr. Babcock
argues the batch plant operation is not clean and the cement dust poses a serious health risk.

Jeff Colemere clarified that a previous speaker misstated the applicable standard. The standard
is not that the applicant has to prove beyond a reasonable doubt there will be zero emissions
from the proposed concrete batch plant. The "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard is a criminal
law standard that does not apply here. Rather, the actual standard is that the applicant needs to
demonstrate to the satisfaction of the Planning Commission that they intend to maintain
equipment and comply with any permit conditions provided. Mr. Colemere stated they have done
that today through their submissions and statements to the Commission. Based on meeting the
correct standard of showing planned compliance, the speaker requested the conditional use
