   2.1. Discussion regarding the creation of two new Herriman City citizen committees - Tami
        Moody, Assistant City Manager & Lorin Palmer, Mayor

Assistant City Manager Tami Moody said the Council requested to discuss the possibility of creating
two additional committees, in order to increase community engagement. She looked to get a
consensus on the creation of the committees, the first being the Diversity Committee. She noted many
cities have a Diversity Committee, and each was unique. She observed the committee in Ogden, which
focused on poverty, homelessness, and inclusion, and the one in Draper, which was composed of
residents, and offered business, school, and public events. Assistant City Manager Moody explained
the other possible committee was the Reputation Management Committee, which would aim to
generate positive conversations about the City.

Mayor Lorin Palmer elaborated the Latino community had reached out to him to express interest in
getting involved in the City, and said he would love to see Spanish classes, as well as classes about
how residents of Herriman could get registered to vote. He said the LGBTQ community and the special
needs community had reached out to him as well to express interest in the creation of groups would
involve them in the community more. He said he wanted to create educational opportunities in the
City for those groups, and get them more engaged in Herriman. He was in favor of the creation of both
of the committees, and said he wanted to highlight the diversity in Herriman and celebrate everything
Herriman residents had to offer.

 Councilmember Teddy Hodges offered some insight as to how Herriman was perceived by other cities
and reported the reputation was changing for the better throughout the valley. He thought they were
turning the corner for the better. He said the programs already in existence could be utilized as a
partnership, rather than starting from nothing. He said this would show resources which were readily
available.

Councilmember Ohrn agreed with the concept of the committees but thought it should focus on
celebrating things people had in common, rather than celebrating differences. She opined differences
divided people, and worried committees were formed it would be used as a crutch. She thought the
community could be involved where committees were already established to not have too many
March 23, 2022 – City Council Minutes                                                       Page 3 of 15
committees formed. She wanted everyone to feel like they were a part of Herriman and did not think
additional committees were necessary.

                        Councilmember Jared Henderson arrived at 6:00 p.m.

Mayor Lorin Palmer replied he understood her perspective and explained he saw the committees as a
solution to get residents more involved. He pointed out that the committees would be led by volunteers
and did not feel this foundation would not be used as a crutch for residents. He said that City staff did
a lot for the residents and thought that the committees would help get residents to take the lead on
community involvement. Councilmember Ohrn thought their role could be to help facilitate finding
spaces to gather people together.

City Manager Cherpeski mentioned it was important to take a look at who would serve on the
committees and have the ability to restructure if necessary.

which the residents met in a local park to meet their neighbors, Council Members, and staff to discuss
ongoing issues in the City. He said this could be reinstated in order to raise awareness for the new
committees, and to get people involved. Assistant City Manager Moody agreed neighborhood meetings
would be a good idea.

Mayor Palmer summarized the Council was in favor of the creation of a committee but wanted the
focus to be more on community and connection, rather than targeting underrepresented groups in the

   2.3. Discussion pertaining to Court Services - Tami Moody, Assistant City Manager

Assistant City Manager Moody recalled the Council had agreed to hire an additional court clerk, since
the court had seen a huge increase in caseloads. Since that time, Judge Farr has offered his resignation,
effective April 14th. She reviewed options available, including hiring a part time judge, although she
noted it would be difficult to find a judge wanting a part time position. She offered another option to
look into entering into an interlocal agreement, in which the City would share a judge with another
municipality, or dissolve the court entirely, and contract with another City. She reported a discussion
with Riverton City who was interested in sharing a judge. She explained Riverton City would need a
judge for one to two days, and Herriman could utilize the judge for three days. She said if the Council
chose to dissolve the court, the City would still have to pay for services, and the revenues generated
March 23, 2022 – City Council Minutes                                                      Page 5 of 15
from the court would stay in the other City. She pointed out police officers for Herriman would have
to drive to the other City to go to court, which would mean officers would spend less time in
Herriman. She said staff was ready to submit an interlocal offer to Riverton City, but wanted from
Council before proceeding. She noted the significant increase in caseloads, DUI cases specifically, and
noted it would place a substantial burden on another City if Herriman chose to outsource court
services. Mayor Palmer noted it would also be a burden on residents to travel to an outside jurisdiction
for court services. City Manager Cherpeski explained the City would still need to pay for a prosecutor
event if the court services in Herriman were dissolved. He confirmed the additional time it would take
for officers to travel to court to testify.

Mayor Palmer thought it would be best to enter into interlocal agreement with a neighboring City and
keep court in Herriman. Assistant City Manager Moody commented if this option was selected,
revenues could increase as more cases could be processed making the services more efficient. City
Manager Cherpeski thought it would be better for the community. Councilmember Henderson agreed
it would be most cost-effective for them to keep court in Herriman and noted it would be expensive if
the City had to subsidize the police force driving to and from another City for court. Councilmember
Shields confirmed the judge would be on-site in Herriman for three days a week, and City Manager
Cherpeski confirmed. Assistant City Manager Moody added the model showed in about ten years,
Herriman would need a full-time judge, so using a shared judge would not be a permanent solution.


   2.4. Discussion regarding the addition of an Assistant City Attorney and Legal Assistant within
        the legal department - Chase Andrizzi, City Attorney

City Attorney Chase Andrizzi noted this item was related to the previous discussion for court
services. He said the legal department had four divisions: Human Resources, strategic services,
outside legal services, and prosecutor services. He explained the City contracted for prosecutor and
outside legal services. He said staff looked at the growth of the City and workload and reevaluated if
the City was getting the best level of service through those contracts. He suggested the City was
getting sufficient services; however, thought it was not the best service available. He referenced the
significant caseload increase, and the associated costs. He thought it would make sense to bring a full-
time prosecutor in-house, rather than utilizing a contract. He said the strategic services position had
been left unfilled and suggested the associated salary could be reallocated to bring in a full-time
prosecutor/Assistant City Attorney. He noted this would also reduce costs for outside legal counsel.

City Attorney Andrizzi explained law firms spent a lot of money managing their file systems and said
record retention required a lot of extra work. He pointed out it was ineffective to send those tasks to
an attorney and suggested to hire a legal assistant to take care of maintaining records. He noted it was
not a requirement, but it would make the department more efficient and more cost-effective to have
both a full time prosecutor as well as a legal assistant, to help with more clerical tasks.

The Council agreed to move forward with the suggestion to move the prosecutor services in-house
and hire a legal assistant as well.

   2.5. Discussion of proposed amendments to Title 10 of City Code for home occupation
        regulations (City File No. Z2021-121) - Michael Maloy, City Planner

City Planner Michael Maloy spoke about an email the Council had received from a resident and
provided context of the proposed amendments. He personally wanted to support local businesses and
said the draft amendment was driven by the Planning Commission, not from staff. He commented the
State of Utah was viewed as pro entrepreneurs and small business and noted there was no charge for
home occupation licenses throughout the state.

City Planner Maloy explained when they received an application for a home occupation license, the
neighbors are notified, and wait to make sure there are no concerns before moving forward with the
March 23, 2022 – City Council Minutes                                                     Page 7 of 15
application process. He offered a synopsis of a case in which a woman ran a daycare out of her home,
and had hired additional employees from outside the home, when the number of children she had to
look after increased. The current Code did not allow this, and the experience had gotten the Planning
Commission to reevaluate requirements. The Planning Commission understood it was hard to find
good childcare and did not want to discourage small daycare centers from expanding. He said the
Planning Commission requested staff to review the ordinance.

City Planner Maloy explained the most common complaint about home occupation businesses were
regarding parking issues. He said the objective was to find a way for small businesses to grow, while
keeping the residential feel of a neighborhood. Currently, the Code did not allow an accessory
structure to be used for a business and reported the Planning Commission had asked for this
requirement to be reviewed. The Planning Commission proposed the structures could be used for the
running of the business, and not just for storage relating to the business. The Planning Commission
had also proposed for up to three employees who do not reside in the home to be permitted, with one
off-street parking spot to be provided for each employee. The Planning Commission suggested the
driveway could be used to achieve the off-street parking requirement. City Planner Maloy added the
Planning Commission should keep a limit of two customers in the business at one time, to mitigate
parking issues.

City Planner Maloy suggested that no more than 12 children be allowed in the daycare, as this would
still conform to state statute, and if three employees were allowed, the ratio of employees to children
would be reasonable. The Planning Commission had proposed home daycares should only be
conducted between the hours of 6:00 AM to 7:00 PM. City Planner Maloy said there were other types
of uses which would be impacted in home group instructions, such as adult classes taught in someone’s
home. He noted the proposed change would only allow 12 students at a time. He commented in the
wake of COVID-19, there were a lot of entrepreneurs and at-home businesses, and acknowledged
changes were very pro-home occupation.

Councilmember Henderson wondered where the line would be drawn, and worried about infringing on
the rights of neighboring property owners if there were too many children in a home. It was clarified
a 12-child maximum, not 12 children per employee. Councilmember Ohrn elaborated on the ratio of
employees to children and thought it made sense to keep the maximum at 12, and not the State-
mandated maximum of 16. She pointed out if one employee called in sick, 16 kids might be too much
for two employees to handle. Councilmember Hodges wondered if it might be more cost-effective to
keep it at one person with eight kids, rather than hiring additional staff and expanding the number of
children. The Council debated about where the line between commercial and residential space would
be drawn. Councilmember Henderson thought if 16 kids were in a daycare should be constituted a
commercial space.

Councilmember Shields spoke about the rule which stated that only 20-percent of floor space in a
residential unit could be used for business purposes. He did not think this was a sufficient amount of
space to run a daycare in an average size home, and he worried about the rights of the surrounding
neighbors. City Planner Maloy said Council could consider a modification to the 20-percent rule, even
though the Planning Commission had not suggested the change. Councilmember Shields asked if there
were requirements about things like amenities, safety features, or number of bathrooms. City Planner
March 23, 2022 – City Council Minutes                                                    Page 8 of 15
Maloy commented while most at-home businesses had to be entirely inside, they did allow home
daycares to include a yard, as long as it was fenced in. Mayor Palmer did not want to create a double
standard and pointed out many people relied on daycare services. Councilmember Shields thought it
was a double standard while most home occupation businesses were only allowed two customers at a
time, a daycare could have 12 kids.

Councilmember Henderson worried about unintended consequences if exceptions were made for
home daycares, and Councilmember Ohrn agreed it was a nuanced issue which raised
questions. Councilmember Henderson had a hard time with the rule about allowing three employees
to work at a home business. He said the Code was written the way it was for a reason and felt he
needed more justification to make exceptions. He also felt 12 kids were too many for a home business
and thought the size of the group should go in a commercial space. Councilmember Shields agreed
three or more employees was a full business, and at that point, they should move to a commercial
space from a residential home. The Council discussed there was also a huge range in the size of homes
and wondered how to codify a home had to be a certain size and have a certain size home in order to
operate a home daycare. Councilmember Shields pointed out they had to consider things like HOA
rules and street width as well. He asked if home size would be a better standard to determine the
maximum number of kids allowed, rather than setting it at 12 children for all homes.

City Planner Maloy said he could prepare a document about state requirements on home daycares to

did, and imagined Herriman was something of an outlier in regulations for home occupation

residents, while still supporting small businesses.

6. City Council Board and Committee Reports

Councilmember Ohrn reported she had attended the Youth Council’s Leadership Conference, held at
Utah State University. She said she had been reminded of the amazing youth in the community, and
saw their potential for leadership. She said applications for the Youth Council were due on May 15th,
and said it was a great opportunity for high school students. She said it was about training to be leaders

Conference she attended, and said the ground was more saturated than it had been the year before,
although they were still facing many of the same issues from last year. She said water was usually
studied in 30 year intervals, and the last 30 year period had just ended. She discussed Herriman had
essentially doubled their population recently, and said currently, the average person used about 237
gallons of water per day, which was higher than the average amount of water a person used 30 years
ago, and cautioned people to be careful with their water usage. She said there were online classes and
other free resources available for people to learn about ways to mitigate their water use, specifically
in landscaping. She said it was important to conserve both culinary and agricultural water uses.

Councilmember Ohrn also discussed garbage and said Salt Lake County had voted to change them
from a special district to a local district, which she explained removed an unnecessary level of
bureaucracy. She said the change had been several years in the making.

Councilmember Hodges thanked Camp Williams for a tour of their facility. He said the Sewer Board
had met, and they had been able to tour this facility as well. He reported it was a state-of-the-art
facility, and the smell of sewage was not a problem in the facility. He observed the research and work
on how to process waste more efficiently. He explained the process, and said they first extricated
water from solid waste, so the water could go back into the water system. He reported currently, 15
million gallons of water went through the facility per day. He spoke about the reduction of impact fees
in the Draper and South Mountain area, due to some local agreements which would soon be
changing. He said they had also spoken about the possibility of a boundary adjustment to the sewage
service area once certain areas got more developed.

Councilmember Shields spoke about the Mosquito Abatement District, which he said was a fascinating
board that had incredible technology. He reported on tests with genetically modified mosquitoes
which were unable to reproduce, which might help curb the population. He stated there were new
machines which put out x-ray radiation which would sterilize males. He explained traps were set up
around the area, and as mosquitos were collected, would be sorted out by breeds in order to predict
trends and then control different populations. He said that they had gotten much better data recently
due to these methods, and the results of the data had indicated West Nile virus could be a big problem
the next summer. However, he said there was potentially less need for insecticides, as the mosquitoes
changed genetically. He then gave a legislative update about House Bill 22, which was in regard to
electronic meetings. He lastly noted Mr. Gene Drake had served 25 years of service on the Mosquito
Abatement Board and said he appreciated all the work that he and others on the board did.

Mayor Palmer gave an update on the Regional Growth Committee, and reported the TLC grant was

   8.1. Discussion and consideration of a recommendation to amend Chapter 10-16 of City Code
        to allow medical services as a permitted use in C-2 (Commercial), and Chapter 10-24 of City
        Code regarding minimum parking requirements for medical services (City File No. Z2021-
        151) - Michael Maloy, City Planner

City Planner Maloy explained the item had originally come before Council at a January work meeting
and gave an update on the concerns noted in the meeting. He said the current Code had medical uses
listed as a conditional use in the C-2 zone and said there had been challenges with limited parking
spaces in one medical building that was in the C-2 zone. He said the Planning Commission and City
staff had proposed to change medical services from a conditional use to a permitted use and added the
definition of “medical services” was expanded in the ordinance. He explained the mandate of one
parking space for 200 square feet of office building, which was on par with what other cities
required. He said in this case, given the size of the building in question, which would yield six parking
spaces for the building. He said the Planning Commission had recommended approval. He clarified
this might bring someone out of compliance but would be grandfathered in as legal non-conforming.

Councilmember Shields moved to approve Ordinance No 2022-11 amending Title 10 of the Herriman
City Code to permit medical services in the C-2 Zone, modify the parking schedule and amend the
definition of medical services. Councilmember Hodges seconded the motion.

The vote was recorded as follows:
Councilmember Jared Henderson Aye

   8.2. Discussion and consideration of Reimbursement Agreement with Midas Crossing for
        Storm Drain Trunkline and Secondary Waterline - Jonathan Bowers, City Engineer

City Engineer Jonathan Bowers explained this was a reimbursement agreement for installed
infrastructure for Midas Crossing phase one and two. He listed the improvements, which included a
secondary water line and a storm drain line, which he noted were both system improvements, and thus
not impact fee eligible. He briefly explained the benefit of the project and said it would allow water to
discharge into the pond. He said the City had required the developer to make both improvements. He
said if the City had not needed these improvements to be made, the developer could have installed a
smaller storm drain, so the reimbursement would be for the increased size. He stated the
reimbursement amount would be $80,000 for the water line, and clarified it was a not to exceed
number, and the money would come from the Water Enterprise Fund. He continued it would be
$176,000 for the storm drain line, and he said since the drain solved an existing deficiency, it would
not be impact fee eligible, and could be paid for with the Storm Drain Enterprise fund. He summarized
the total cost for the project would be $260,000 in total and offered his recommendation to move
forward as part of next year’s budget. He looked to the Council for direction, and the Council agreed
to move forward.

   8.3. Discussion and consideration of a proposed amendment to the Official Zoning Map for
        property located generally at 12823 S 6300 West from 11.75 acres of A-1 Agricultural Zone
        to R-1-10 Residential Zone. (City File No. Z2022-004) - Michael Maloy, City Planner

City Planner Maloy said this was a privately initiated application and observed the Planning
Commission recommendation of approval for the rezone. He indicated it was in alignment with the
General Plan. He said the General Plan stated the density for that zone was 2.5 units per acre and
noted the land in question was located close to the Olympia Hills development. He said the Planning
Commission had suggested that a zoning condition be attached to the amendment that would limit the
density to 2.5 units per acre.

Applicant Lovell said he was excited about the project and was available for questions. Councilmember
Henderson reported a resident in the neighborhood had emailed the Council with concerns about
increased traffic in the area and had noted the cross streets connected to the old
town. Councilmember Henderson said he had consistently voted against density, and against the
Olympia Hills development. He stated they only had two roads which went in and out of town and
thought this would lead to congested traffic on those roads. Community Development Director
Thomas acknowledged this would lead to an increase in traffic and said that the biggest impact would
be at the intersection. He noted in their Master Plan, it was intended for 6000 West to eventually be
widened, as well as Silver Sky road, which would alleviate the traffic in that area. He also pointed out
