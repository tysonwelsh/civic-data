38          Architectural Standards Discussion
39
40   Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, discussed the City’s architectural standards
41   and its application process. He explained that the City Council is responsible for setting the
42   commercial architectural standards, while the Planning Commission administers and approves
43   projects based on these guidelines. The Planning Commission also holds public hearings and
44   reviews project proposals to ensure compliance with the established standards.
45
46   Mr. Lethbridge highlighted two types of architectural standards: "soft" and "hard." Soft standards
47   offer flexibility, such as using an earth-tone color palette, which can vary depending on the
48   context. Hard standards, such as building height limits and roofline variations, provide clear and
49   rigid guidelines for developers. He presented various examples from other cities ranging from
50   cities that use broad, general guidelines allowing maximum discretion, to those with detailed
51   design manuals specifying exact requirements.

 2   Mr. Lethbridge said that the Planning Commission's feedback emphasized the need for more
 3   flexibility and cohesiveness, especially in the City's downtown area, where there are established
 4   architectural tones.
 5
 6   Councilmember McCay noted that some standards seemed outdated or challenging to interpret,
 7   and shared examples of recent developments where frustration arose due to these issues. She
 8   also proposed that final approval for larger commercial developments (over 2,000 square feet)
 9   or larger residential developments (over 10-20 units) should return to the City Council for
10   review, rather than being solely decided by the Planning Commission. The reasoning was that
11   the City Council has more training and oversight responsibilities, making it more appropriate for
12   them to handle larger-scale projects, while the Planning Commission could continue to oversee
13   smaller developments.
14
15   Ryan Carter, City Attorney, explained the role and function of the Planning Commission under
16   Utah's Land Use Development and Management Act (LUDMA). The Planning Commission is a
17   required body with specific duties, including serving as an advisory entity for legislative
18   functions, such as general plan amendments, zoning amendments, and code amendments. The
19   Commission is intended to develop expertise over time, focusing on land use approvals and
20   acting as the authority for certain administrative decisions, including commercial site plans.
21
22   Mr. Carter acknowledged that while the City Council has the legal option to act as the land use
23   authority for site plan approvals, many cities choose to delegate this role to the Planning
24   Commission. This is because the Planning Commission often becomes more familiar with land
25   use codes and the technical aspects of project approvals, making them better suited to handle
26   these decisions efficiently. Historically, Riverton City had the City Council as the land use
27   authority until about 2010-2012, when it was decided that having the Planning Commission
28   manage these approvals would be more efficient for both the City and the development
29   community.
30
31   Mr. Carter also discussed the difference between legislative functions (where decisions are
32   made based on policy and preferences) and administrative functions (where the focus is on
33   whether an application meets the City's existing standards). Once a project reaches the site
34   plan approval phase, the decision is no longer about preferences but about whether the project
35   complies with the ordinances. This creates a situation where, even if the Council or community
36   dislikes a project, they cannot reject it if it meets the legal standards. The Council may face
37   political pressure to reject projects or change ordinances, but if a project is filed before any
38   ordinance changes, it may still proceed regardless of opposition.
39
40   Mr. Lethbridge explained that the changes to architectural standards would first go to the
41   Planning Commission for approval and then to the City Council. He related an example where a
42   commercial project caused issues due to its elevation being significantly different from nearby
43   homes, which eventually escalated from the Planning Commission to the City Council. He
44   reminded the Council that one of the most effective ways to manage these concerns is through
45   setting clear standards that the Planning Commission can apply uniformly across projects.
46
47   Councilmember McDougal said he does not believe the Council should play a larger role in
48   approving architectural standards for commercial developments because the current process
49   has been effective. He supported staff’s recommendation to update the architectural standards
50   which would give the Planning Commission the tools to make decisions without confusion.

24   Currently, the property's general plan designation is estate density residential, which requires a
25   minimum lot size of half an acre, and it is zoned as RR-22 (Residential Rural 22,000 square feet

29   The applicant seeks to rezone the property to RM-18, which would allow for multifamily
30   development at a density of 18 units per acre. However, the Planning Commission has reviewed
31   the application and recommended denial, as indicated in the motion presented to the Council. If

34   zoning classification.
