      Mayor Kristie Overson reported on two recent ribbon cuttings—one at the Patty Shack and
the other at Spill the Tea. Both openings were fun and festive, and she encouraged everyone to eat
and shop locally.

A beautiful, snowy tree-lighting ceremony was held on November 28th. The Mayor thanked those
who attended, including the Taylorsville High School Madrigal Choir, and the Taylorsville Youth
Council. She thanked those groups as well as the facilities crew who spent the day moving snow
and preparing for the event. She also thanked the City Council for providing the budget for what
she hoped might become an annual event.

Earlier in the day, local artist Jeannine Young had donated an additional sculpture to the Plaza +
Art program. (She currently had a sculpture displayed in Centennial Plaza which was there on a
rotation basis.) The new piece would be part of the City’s permanent collection and would be
displayed in city hall until an outdoor pedestal could be procured. Mayor Overson appreciated Ms.
Young’s generosity and looked forward to seeing the Plaza + Art program grow.

The Youth Council had been very busy with a variety of service opportunities. They had decorated
the Heritage Museum and city hall for the holidays and were now job-shadowing several
employees to learn what each did for the city.

Another program the Youth Council was involved with was the Wreaths Across America project
sponsored by the Honor 365 non-profit organization. The goal for the Taylorsville Cemetery was
to lay a holiday wreath at each of the 255 veterans’ graves, and donations had far exceeded
expectations. The additional funds would be used for more wreaths the following year. The
wreaths would be placed following a brief ceremony at city hall on December 17th which would
begin at 10:00 a.m.

The Taylorsville Exchange Club had met the previous week and chosen City Council and Youth
Council Coordinator Kris Heineman to receive the Book of Golden Deeds award. This was a
national award geared towards volunteers who strived to make their communities better, and Ms.
Heineman certainly did that. The Exchange Club was very happy to honor Ms. Heineman for her
hard work.

Speaking of community spirit and good deeds, they Mayor wished to share two examples of kind-
hearted Taylorsville businesses. Wes and Suzie who were the proprietors of Speed Kleen
Laundromat here in town had donated eight full baskets of food and a gift card to Taylorsville
residents in need. They had delivered the food and cards to the police department who then made
sure that they went to local families who needed a Thanksgiving meal. Texas Roadhouse would
also be donating meals to needy families through the police department during the holiday season.
She was grateful for their giving spirits.

        1.3      Citizen Comments

      Chair Barbieri invited members of the audience to step forward and address the council on
the topic of their choice.

Lawrence Horman explained that he was one of many homeless people and wished to advocate on
the part of the homeless. He explained that homelessness was not a problem that could be solved
by one person or one organization. Funding might come from the federal, state or county level but
he felt the real work had to be done at the city level. He wished to encourage Taylorsville to work
with the homeless, not just for them. He felt a solution must begin with housing and that continuing
supportive services needed to last beyond 90 days. He explained that he had provided packets to
each elected official and that he had distributed the same packets to many local mayors in 2017
and received almost no response. He said he was on the Salt Lake Valley Coalition to End
Homelessness, providing that group with a ‘client perspective.’ He ended his comments by stating
that he would be happy to discuss the subject with anyone interested in hearing more.

Mark Petrie explained he was present on behalf of himself and several other residents who lived
along Atherton Drive. He showed an aerial map of about 1175 West Atherton. He reminded the
Council that he had addressed them previously on his safety concerns regarding the number of
serious accidents in the area. He then played video and audio of several traffic accidents that had
taken place in front of his home dating as far back as 2019, resulting in at least one fatality. The
footage ended after showing an accident that had taken place just three days earlier. While Mr.
Petrie appreciated the signage that had been put in place since his last appearance, he did not feel
it was sufficient since people who were impaired or fleeing from police did not pay attention to
signs. He requested low profile speed bumps prior to the location of a sharp corner where most of
the accidents took place. He asked the council again to do more before additional injury and
property damage took place.

Councilmember Harker asked city engineer Ben White if the city had ever tried having speed
bumps at the corner in question. He responded that it had not.

Dennis Sanok mentioned that the location referenced by Mr. Petrie had been on his bike route for
quite some time and he agreed that drivers took that corner too fast even during the best driving
conditions. He then said he wished to congratulate the Mayor and City Council on not making the
news. He had attended the meetings of other cities regularly in the past and he appreciated the
decorum shown in Taylorsville when the Council, Administration and Staff interacted. He also
appreciated the respect shown towards the citizens.

3.      REPORTS

       3.1       Audit Report – Scott Harrington/Rob Wood

      Scott Harrington explained that the name of the Comprehensive Annual Financial Report
(CAFR) had been changed to the Annual Comprehensive Financial Report (ACFR). The year
fiscal year had ended on June 30th, after which bills and revenues were received. City
administration had then met with the budget/audit committee before holding a kick-off meeting
with the auditor. Field work was conducted in September and October. The report was compiled
beginning in November and the administration met again with the budget/audit committee earlier
that week and had then presented it to the council for their review.

     Mr. Harrington then turned the time over to Rob Wood of HBME, LLC who highlighted the
report for the city council. He explained what state law required, and that banks, bond holders and
grantors might also request copies of the audit report. Mr. Wood was pleased to report that
Taylorsville had received the highest audit opinion he could give as an independent auditor —an
Taylorsville City Council Minutes

unmodified opinion, meaning that he did not believe there were any material misstatements. He
stated that Taylorsville was in a very strong financial position.

Taylorsville had received nearly nine million dollars in federal funding which helped to fund many
recent projects throughout the city.

The general fund was the city’s operating account and had total assets of $13 million with $2.8
million in liabilities. Mr. Wood explained that the State Auditor liked to look at unassigned balance
numbers and Taylorsville had $5 million in unassigned funds. That percentage needed to remain
between 5% and 35% of the general fund balance and was currently at 15.5%. This was lower than
in recent years partially because $11.3 million had been transferred from the general fund to the
capital projects and parks funds. In short, future park and road projects had already been funded
in the past year.

Mr. Wood pointed out that one of the council’s responsibilities was setting the budget. It had
appropriated $27 million for FY2022-2023, and the administration had only spent $25 million, so
$2 million in tax payer funds had been saved which was particularly noteworthy given the current
economy. Each of the city’s funds was under budget. The only potential concern was the storm
drain fund which had borrowed about $832,000 from the general fund the previous year. That had
created a negative unrestricted net-position in that fund which would take a couple of years to pay
off.

     Mr. Wood had reviewed the city’s internal controls over financial reporting, cash receipts,
payrolls, journal entries and looked for any weaknesses. He and his firm had found no deficiencies.

The State Auditor published a State Compliance Audit Guide each year which sought to ensure
that the City maintained appropriate records of enrollment in Utah Retirement Systems, annual
training for elected officials on Utah’s Open and Public Meetings Act, a standardized Fraud Risk
Assessment, and conducted public hearings when required, including appropriate notice.

In the last twenty years, the state had implemented sixty new accounting standards that each
governmental entity needed to meet. Mr. Wood said he was pleased to present another excellent
report for the City.

Councilmember Burgess expressed his appreciation to Mr. Wood, Mr. Harrington, and the citizen
budget/audit committee for their hard work.

Mr. Wood pointed out that Taylorsville was the only city he could think of that had more than two
citizens review the budget. In fact, there were 10 residents sitting on the committee who reviewed
the budget at least bi-annually.

        3.2     Taylorsville Bennion Improvement District Report – Mark Chalk

      Taylorsville Bennion Improvement District General Manager and Chief Executive Officer
Mark Chalk greeted the councilmembers and said that the District was celebrating 65 years as a
public entity. It handled all the water distribution and waste water collection services for the
majority of Taylorsville as well as some of West Jordan, Murray, Kearns, and West Valley City.
The District was governed by a three-person Board of Trustees, had 34 employees and was
responsible for 17,500 connections.

Water Distribution:
  • 11 active wells
  • 16 reservoirs
  • 2,139 fire hydrants
  • Over 6,000 valves and blowoffs
  • 4 booster sites
  • 3 chemical addition sites
  • Maintained over 246 miles of water pipe
  • Deliver, on average, over 4 billion gallons of water annually

Wastewater Collection and Treatment:
  • 4,274 manholes
  • Over 188 miles of wastewater pipe
  • About 5 million gallons of wastewater collected daily
  • 2 Lift Stations
  • 11% owner of Central Valley Water Reclamation Facility (CVWRF)

The Jordan River Syphon Project would involve doing away with a 46-year-old lift station and
replacing it with a syphon which would be a significant improvement to the system.

Councilmember Harker inquired about the wells and pointed out how good Taylorsville water
tasted. Mr. Chalk explained that was due to water quality.

Mr. Chalk explained that the federal government had mandated that the phosphorous limit in water
be decreased from 10 parts per billion (ppb) to 1 ppb. That required the entire plant’s processes to
be changed and among other things, it turned what had been a $250 million project into a $460
million project.

Regarding the current drought in the region, Councilmember Harker asked what Mr. Chalk wished
residents to know. He responded that there had been an amazing response to drought messaging

7.      OTHER MATTERS

        7.1      Resolution No. 22-35 – A Resolution of the City of Taylorsville declaring
                 certain real property located at approximately 1611 West Dowry Court as
                 surplus property – Ben White

     City Engineer Ben White explained that UDOT had been in the process of designing an
additional onramp to I-215 from Redwood Road. They had met with nearby residents who would
be affected by the project including the Traditions Homeowners Association which involved
between 75 – 100 homes off of 6020 South. There was a school bus stop in a travel lane on
Redwood Road to pick up and drop off children who lived in the Traditions development and there
was only one access road to Traditions and no way of moving the bus stop off of Redwood. There
was a desire to increase access to the neighborhood and find an alternative location for the bus
stop. UDOT thought they might be able to provide some funding if a solution to those two
problems could be identified. The City had recently purchased the Dowry Court property with the
goal of then selling it to the HOA. A sidewalk had been constructed to allow for more ingress and
egress of pedestrian traffic to Traditions. Additionally, the HOA would agree to maintain the
sidewalk in perpetuity, and it would allow the bus stop to be moved away from Redwood to a
neighborhood street. To sell the property to the HOA, it needed to be declared as surplus which
was why the matter had been brought to the City Council.

Chair Barbieri appreciated everyone working together to address the situation so that student safety
could be increased.

      Jay Ziolkowski explained that he had made a presentation to city staff earlier in the year
regarding emergency preparedness at home. The City’s overall emergency management plan had
