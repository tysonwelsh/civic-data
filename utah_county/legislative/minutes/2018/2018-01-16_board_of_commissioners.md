4.     ADOPT (OR DENY) AN ORDINANCE TO AMEND THE UTAH COUNTY GENERAL PLAN, 2014
       AND ADOPT SECTION 5-18 OF THE UTAH COUNTY LAND USE ORDINANCE TO ADOPT
       GRAZING (G-1) ZONE (Continued from the January 9, 2018 Commission meeting)
(Track A, 19:25)
Commissioner Ivie reminded the audience of the continuing discussion on this item. Commissioner Lee mentioned
he is currently inclined to adopt the ordinance and therefore information shared last week in support of the change
does not need to be repeated. He added he is open to hearing any new comments in opposition to the ordinance.
Commissioner Ivie opened the microphone to the public and requested comments be limited to two minutes.

(Track A, 21:45)
Lake Shore resident Bryson Hinkins was the first to come forward, stating he is opposed to concrete and asphalt plants
being removed from the proposed grazing zone. Mr. Hinkins pointed out operations can be within 500 feet of
residences within city limits, but the closest mining to a home in West Mountain is 1,800 feet. He contended this
amendment is not to protect grazing as much as it is to stop mining, listing several permitted uses within the zone that
would be detrimental for agriculture: racing tracks which cause noise and dust, shooting ranges which need buffer
zones, commercial dog kennels, electrical generator plants which leave huge footprints, and a myriad of potential
development issues related to landfills, explosion facilities, nursing homes, and correctional institutions.

(Track A, 26:00)
Debi Brozovich of Payson explained the Utah Division of Oil, Gas and Mining (DOGM) will not approve a mining
permit which is not in compliance with Utah County Code. She commented on the blaring early morning alarms from
the Kilgore asphalt plant and the local agricultural production being jeopardized. Ms. Brozovich noted the need for
stricter ordinances, and the excessive amount of water the Kilgore operators would need to truck into the area for
appropriate dust suppression. She pointed out the community depended on the legislators to protect them from these
issues, stating “While we appreciate your working with us for over four years, if the zoning change on West Mountain
is not approved to allow protection of our resources, and the management of the growth of mining operations, you
have failed us.”

(Track A, 30:30)
West Mountain resident Mary Hope commented on the beauty of the area. She feels her family’s health and safety
are threatened by the mining situation, and is in favor of the proposed ordinance. CMC Rock representative and Provo
resident Todd Kusick presented a salt-dough clay model of property (pictures of which are attached to these minutes)
as viewed from Utah Lake when looking directly due East. He explained the pink dots are CMC-owned property,
black dots specify property owned by the United States Bureau of Land Management (BLM), and orange dots belong
to the Utah School and Institutional Trust Lands Administration (SITLA). Mr. Kusick mentioned the BLM owns
approximately eighty percent of the ground affected by this proposed ordinance, and they have submitted a letter of
opposition to the Utah County Board of Commissioners. Mr. Kusick read the letter which specified the BLM may
UTAH COUNTY BOARD OF COMMISSIONERS

still consider mineral extractions for the land registered to them. Commissioner Lee clarified which mining
applications would need to be authorized by the Utah Division of Oil, Gas and Mining versus the BLM based on
extracted materials. The Board discussed Mr. Kusick’s clay model with him and potential long-term impacts of the
proposed ordinance, including higher construction costs.

(Track A, 43:35)
Nita Burch with the South Utah County Community Voice read prepared comments (a copy of which can be found
attached to these minutes) in favor of rezoning West Mountain for grazing. She mentioned her Mohawk Indian
ancestry, and the amazing petroglyphs unique to this area. Ms. Burch also spoke of the gravel trucks causing damage
to homes near their routes similar to registered 3.5 magnitude earthquakes. West Mountain resident Julie Sainsbury
is also part of South Utah County Community Voice. Ms. Sainsbury is opposed to possible language that could allow
mining as a conditional use within the grazing zone. She also mentioned the Bureau of Land Management has
autonomy whether or not the county updates the zone. Ms. Sainsbury talked about deficiencies and lack of personnel
to address problems between state and federal departments. She gave examples of past issues expanding mines with
conditional use, and suggested the Board consider creating two distinct levels of grazing zones like what is done with
Critical Environment areas (CE-1 and CE-2 zones). She thanked the Board for their involvement thus far.

(Track A, 54:00)
Benjamin resident Shayla Hawkins stated South Utah County Community Voice does not speak for everyone.
Referencing a community meeting at the Benjamin Park four years ago, Ms. Hawkins explained the goal was to
address traffic safety concerns – not stop mining, as the resources from earth extraction are needed. She pointed out
the potential for lawsuits once the county begins deciding who can do what with their land. Ms. Hawkins talked about
the need to fix the roads, especially county road 8000 South. She gave examples of being hit by overspray from
county snow plows on the front porch which is 25 feet from the road, and addressed speeding, mentioning a recent
vehicular wreck on 3200 West and 10000 South. Provo resident Robert Stevens equated the situation to fictional
character ‘Ebenezer Scrooge’ (A Christmas Carol by Charles Dickens) and monopolies, speaking on the annual
increase of gravel rates and anti-trust laws. Commissioner Lee disagreed with Mr. Stevens’ use of the word
‘monopoly’ and countered Mr. Stevens’ interpretations of the Utah State Constitution. Commissioner Ivie supported
Commissioner Lee’s statements. Commissioner Graves stated he would not respond to the claims as he does not
support this proposed ordinance; the best option for West Mountain would be to incorporate to address these issues.
The Board debated their points with Mr. Stevens.

(Track A, 1:04:55)
Shauna Phelps of West Mountain came forward to define ‘monopoly’: “the exclusive possession or control of the
supply or trade of a commodity or service; a control that makes possible the manipulation of prices.” Ms. Phelps
explained this is not about money or financial benefits. The residents are concerned with property rights and multiple
families and businesses are being affected detrimentally by mining. She talked about personal losses and the need for
concessions to be made on both sides. Aaron Schell also lives in West Mountain and works in construction – he
understands the need for materials, but the fruit farms were there first. He spoke of greenbelt and agriculture
production, and suggested reclamation be part of the mining process. Lana Creer-Harris talked about mitigation, water
shortages, water shares, poor air quality, damaged roads, and conflicting land use.

(Track A, 1:19:05)
Sharla Hawkins emphasized her concern over traffic safety, pointing out 8000 South is one of the few county roads
being used for the mining operations. This zoning change will not fix the roads. She suggested the mining companies
or the State of Utah be required to install a haul-only road. Jeannie Lewis is in favor of the proposed ordinance. She
read a letter from Cynthia Chamberlain (a copy of which can be found attached to these minutes), who is also in
support of the new grazing zone. Debbie Farrer is a newer resident to West Mountain and expressed concern that
the Bureau of Land Management can override decisions made by the Board of Commissioners. She commented on
air quality and reminded the audience bees and trees are natural resources just as important as minerals. Ms. Farrer
suggested an additional year to review and study consequences of mining in the area, and perhaps impose impact fees
onto the mining companies.

(Track A, 1:28:15)
Susan Chapman grew up in Benjamin and is worried about air pollution. She explained the national average for
asthma between 2014 and 2016 is eight percent, but the Utah Department of Health puts the Benjamin/Palmyra rural
area at 13.9%. This may not be caused by mining but does show this is a high-risk population. Ms. Chapman asked
the Board to consider the people struggling to breathe when making their decisions. West Mountain resident Tamara
Atkin talked about her background from Bakersfield, California and expounded on the causes of air pollution. She
commented on needing zoning to reduce risk for neighbors, and reminded the audience the people of West Mountain
did not move there for sand and mining. Frank Kanig of Spanish Fork referenced an email from the Utah Division of
Oil, Gas and Mining (DOGM) which he had forwarded on to the Board the previous week. The email outlined DOGM
suggestions for the Kilgore operation. Mr. Kanig asked the Board to consider implementing some of the suggestions
for all of the mining operations. He also pointed out the potential to accommodate increasing population in the
southern end of the county.

(Track A, 1:37:20)
Commissioners Ivie and Lee thanked the public for their input. The discussion was returned to the bench, and the
Board discussed the history of this issue and the importance of protecting natural resources. Commissioner Ivie
provided statistics about food production, noting eighty acres of agricultural land has been lost nationwide during the
course of today’s meeting. Commissioner Graves talked about the ramifications of Board decisions, stating he wants
his children to look back in twenty years and be able to ask whether their father made the county a better place. The
proposed ordinance makes sense for some areas of the county but not all. Commissioner Graves talked about his
family’s agricultural history and farming roots and condemned the Board for not unifying the community to find a
solution for the current practices.

(Track A, 1:49:15)
Commissioner Lee talked about meeting with a representative of the Bureau of Land Management, and a mutual
willingness to coordinate efforts. Commissioner Lee views this as a ‘time-out’ while working through the best
practices for all parties. Commissioner Graves pointed out there is frightening potential in adopting this proposed
ordinance; if a zone can be created to stop mining, what is to stop the Board from creating a zone specifying “you
must farm if on this land?” Or no more building will be allowed? He and Commissioner Lee discussed parcel lot
sizes, with Commissioner Graves noting less acreage is better for water conservation. They debated their views and
possible alternative solutions. Commissioner Lee commented on the federal government taking action to protect sage
grouse, prairie dogs, and certain plants but something should be in the law to protect the American farmer.

(Track A, 1:57:00)
Commissioner Lee said he was bothered by the removal of fish hatcheries from the proposed ordinance, and motioned
to adopt the ordinance with an update to include fish hatcheries.

6.     APPROVE AGREEMENT WITH OPERATION UNDERGROUND RAILROAD AND ACCEPT
       GRANT FOR LAW ENFORCEMENT PURPOSES
(Track A, 2:03:40)
Sergeant Jason Randall of the Utah County Sheriff’s Office came forward; he supervises the Special Victims Unit for
the Sheriff’s Office as well as the Special Victims Task Force for Utah County. They are very aggressive in fighting
‘travellers’: a person who sexually preys on children under the age of thirteen through the internet. Utah County
arrested more than 70 travellers last year, amongst the highest numbers in the nation. Operation Underground Railroad
(OUR) is a local entity that conducts international investigations to save children, and also works to find corporate
sponsorships for local law enforcement to assist in these efforts. The Utah County Sheriff’s Office works closely with
the State Attorney General’s Office through the Internet Crimes Against Children (ICAC) Task Force and Homeland
Security Investigations (HSI). Adobe, a software company based in Lehi, made a donation to OUR with a specific
request for the funds to serve. The Sheriff’s Office is being offered this opportunity as an agency which has produced
impressive outcomes as results of their investigations. Sergeant Randall talked about how the $20,000 grant will be
utilized to help protect youth in our community.

(Track A, 2:31:45)
Utah County Surveyor Gary Ratcliffe serves on the Utah County Historic Preservation Committee. The recent focus
of the committee has been orchards and gravel pits. Long before those industries existed, Native Americans lived
here. Some of their culture is shared on West Mountain. Mr. Ratcliffe noted historic artifacts can co-exist with mining
operations if restrictions are in place. As a land surveyor, he is a proponent of property rights and people being able
to use the land they own as they see fit; however, this Board can have influence in protecting irreplaceable relics.
Commissioner Lee mentioned it is against federal law to destroy precious rock art. The Utah Division of Oil, Gas and
Mining (DOGM) has been asked to re-open studies of the area based on newly discovered sites and the goal of
preventing damage. Mr. Ratcliffe also asked the Commissioners to take appropriate steps and make the financial
investments to minimize vandalism of the petroglyphs and art near the shooting range currently under construction on
the west side of Utah Lake.

(Track A, 2:38:05)
CMC Rock representative Todd Cusick stated there is an open invitation for anyone wanting to research rock art on
his company’s property. Mr. Cusick stated he started the largest EIS (Environmental Impact Study) in the state of
Utah regarding preservation of these types of artifacts in 1999; he spent eleven years working on projects such as
routing the road around ‘The Hunter Panel’ in 2002, one of the most famous pieces of Native rock art in the entire
country. Mr. Cusick noted his sensitivity to the issue and willingness for compatibility.
