 SR 224 Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) Update - Presented by Caroline Rodriguez, High 
 Valley Transit (HVT) Executive Director: 
 Rodriguez reviewed the history of BRT along SR-224. She worked with Park City 
 Transportation staff to find a way to get riders from the valley to the mountain. BRT 
 reduced traffic congestion. Buses traveled from Kimball Junction to the Old Town Transit 
 Center. 
  
 Council Member Dickey asked if transit technology included changing signals to allow 
 the buses to move through intersections without stopping, to which Rodriguez affirmed. 
She reviewed the impacts from the BRT program, including a small noise increase, but 
a large increase in ridership. She stated they were getting ready to work on final design 
and estimated the process would take two years. She hoped the project would be 
completed by 2027. Council Member Dickey asked why the project had been delayed, 
to which Rodriguez stated HVT did not have enough staff to keep up with the workload. 
 
Council Member Rubell asked who would be allowed to use the BRT lane. Rodriguez 
stated the BRT lane would be for public transit vehicles only, including microtransit, 
emergency vehicles, and school buses. Council Member Rubell referred to wildlife 
mitigation efforts along SR-224, and asked if there were other solutions that wouldn’t 
require large capital outlays. Rodriguez stated there were many agencies that met on a 
regular basis to discuss solutions, but they were told by the Division of Wildlife 
Resources (DWR) there was no one solution that would mitigate impacts because the 
issue was along the entire corridor. The solutions in place now included lower speed 
 limits and the wildlife silhouettes to remind drivers to be alert.

 1. Discuss Ranked Choice Voting as a Possible Method for the 2025 Mayoral and 
 Council Election: 
 Michelle Kellogg, City Recorder, Eve Furse, Summit County Clerk, and Kelleen Potter 
 and Josh Daniels, Utah RCV, were present for this item. Kellogg reviewed the City took 
 elections seriously since voter confidence in elections was a top priority. There would be 
 several future discussions before a decision was made if the Council wanted to proceed 
 after hearing the pros and cons tonight.

 Potter noted there was contention in the country with regard to politics and voters were 
 exhausted. She conducted a sample ranked choice voting on National Parks and the 
 Republican Presidential Primary. The audience in the room used their phones to rank 
 the options. Daniels explained the option with the least votes was eliminated and the 
 second choice of the voters for that option get redistributed. The same process would 
 be used multiple times until there was an option that received over 50%. The point of 
 RCV was to achieve consensus by having an instant runoff. He displayed a map of the 
 country to show the states that used RCV in one form or another. 
  
 Daniels noted RCV ballots would be mailed out and processed the same as was 
 currently done. In 2023, 12 Utah cities participated in RCV. Potter reviewed some cities 
 used RCV to save money. Some other pros include the voters could more fully express 
 their preferences, the winner achieved 50% or more consensus, and RCV eliminated 
 the spoiler effect. She indicated processing the ballots didn’t take any additional time so 
 the results would be announced timely. She felt the process incentivized more civil 
 discourse during the election cycle. 
  
 Daniels stated several cities in Salt Lake and Utah County used RCV and a majority of 
 voters indicated they were satisfied using RCV. He noted 60% of survey respondents 
 ranked their preferred candidate first, which proved strategic voting was minimalized. 
 Kellogg commented on strategic voting and stated no matter the election method, 
 strategic voting would be used by some voters. Some voters might not like all 
 candidates and only vote for one or a few. It was observed that social media groups had 
 been formed specifically to advise voters on the order they should rank their 
 preferences in order to get their favorite candidate(s) elected. 
  
 Kellogg expressed concern over changing election methods and the confusion it could 
 cause some voters. She noted the Heber City Recorder stated there were many spoiled 
 ballots from voters who listened to social media groups and subsequently filled out the 
 RCV ballot wrong, thus disqualifying the ballot from being counted. Another concern 
 was eliminating the candidates who received the fewest votes, with the second choices 
 of those voters being redistributed amongst the remaining candidates. Kellogg asserted 
 the top vote getters in the first round were mainstream candidates and sometimes the 
 lowest vote getters were outliers in the public’s opinion. Using the second choices from 
 voters who voted for outliers would most likely be for other outliers. There could be a 
 chance that in those scenarios, an outlier candidate would then receive a majority of 
 votes and become elected. Kellogg’s final concern was that all votes would not be 
 counted in certain scenarios. In the Heber City election, three candidates were elected 
 in a multi-seat election. Mike Johnston won the first seat and his name was then 
 eliminated. For Johnston to reach 50%, RCV used voters’ first and second preferences. 
 Voters who selected Johnston as their third choice in the three-seat election, had their 
 votes tossed out, meaning one of their three votes for elected officials did not count in 
 the final tally of votes for each winner. RCV proponents argue it shouldn’t matter to the 
 voter that their vote didn’t count because their candidate was still elected. They state

 2. Discuss the Main Street Water Line Replacement Project: 
 Griffin Lloyd and Harrison Holley, Utilities Engineers, presented this item. Lloyd 
 reviewed the corrosion of the iron pipelines in the Main Street area. The replacement of 
 waterlines, water meters, and fire lines would be a multi-year project. The work would 
 be done on the shoulder seasons over three years. There would be minimum impact to 
 events. They would have a full road closure to maximize efficiency. He explained the 
 detour route during the construction. Sidewalk closures would be on one side of the 
 street at a time. The trolley would be replaced with a van during construction and no 
 parking would be allowed in the construction zone. Vehicles could be parked at China 
 Bridge parking lot. Lloyd stated they would have weekly/biweekly outreach for the 
 public.

 3. Open and Public Meetings Act (OPMA), Government Records Access and 
 Management Act (GRAMA), and Ethics Act Updates: 
 Margaret Plane, City Attorney, and Michelle Kellogg, City Recorder, presented this item. 
 Plane played a video from the Utah State Auditor regarding the regulations surrounding 
 OPMA rules. She explained three members of the Council must be present to hold a 
 meeting and it would take three Yes votes to approve any item. She also counseled the 
 members to refrain from texting during public meetings since others watching wouldn’t 
 know who they were texting or the subject of the texts. 
  
 Kellogg reviewed the Utah GRAMA laws. She indicated she had to weigh the right of the

 definition of “record” and noted personal items or drafts were not considered records.

 what would be released. Kellogg stated there were four types of records: public, private, 
 controlled, and protected. Those who request documents might receive a denial 
 response and they would have 30 days to appeal the denial. At that point the City 
 Manager would have 10 days to review the request and determine if the denial should

Council Member A is friends with a consultant who is seeking a 
contract with the City. The contract is before City Council for 
approval. 

Does A need to disclose the relationship before contract approval?

Without other facts, there is no legal requirement 
to disclose the friendship.             ETHICS SCENARIO

Council Member B is the sole owner of a business in Park City that is 
regulated by a PCMC business license. 

Does B need to provide a written disclosure about B’s ownership 
interest?

Yes, B owns a substantial interest in a business entity subject to 
City regulation and must provide this in B’s written disclosure 
when taking office (or when the interest is acquired).             ETHICS SCENARIO
Council Member C owns more than 10% of a local consulting firm 
that is seeking a contract with the City. Because of C’s ownership 
interest, C will financially benefit if the contract is approved. The 
contract is up for City Council approval. 

Does Council Member C need to disclose C’s ownership interest?
Yes

Does Council Member C need to recuse from the discussion 
and vote?

Yes             ETHICS SCENARIO

Council Member D has an open building permit to construct a new 
business space in a commercial building. The Planning Commission 
is recommending a proposed ordinance containing a zoning 
amendment to one of the LMC provisions at issue in the approved 
building permit. 

Does D need to disclose the open building permit? 

No, for several reasons.             ETHICS SCENARIO

The building permit is active and the proposed LMC amendment 
will not impact it.

Utah’s disclosure requirements are about individual financial 
conflicts and D does not stand to benefit directly the LMC 
amendment.

This legislative action does not benefit D in a way different from 
other similarly situated persons or property.            ETHICS SCENARIO

              ETHICS SCENARIO

Council Member F gets an email from a City employee announcing 
that an event sponsor has provided the City with tickets to the event. 
The City has a contract with the sponsor to regulate and subsidize 
the event. The tickets are provided to the City and offered to Council 
Members generally, then they are offered to employees. 

May Council Member F accept the tickets? 

Yes             ETHICS SCENARIO

Because the City supports the business entity through a contract that 
provides fee waivers or other subsidies, and because the tickets are 
offered to the City, not individuals, these tickets may be accepted 
without concern about violating the ethics codes.

Keep public perception in mind.
