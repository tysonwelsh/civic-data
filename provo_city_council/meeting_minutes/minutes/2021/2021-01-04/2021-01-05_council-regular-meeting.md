Javin Weaver, Provo City Planner, presented. Hannah Peterson was the applicant for this item. The
initial proposal included the entire Slate Canyon corridor from the round-about at 300 South 1350 East
to the intersection of Slate Canyon Drive and State Street (1860 South). The proposal was to have
unrestricted parking between the hours of 6 a.m. to 1 a.m. Parking restrictions would be imposed
between the hours of 1 a.m. and 6 a.m. The number of permits for residents would be based on street
frontage. The applicant said the goals were to reduce parking congestion and reduce the need for
zoning enforcement.

Staff analyzed the parking permit area and found the following.
     A total of 179 legal parking spaces were found from 690 South to 1400 South.
     During a 24-hour period, vehicles occupied 51 percent of the streets. At no time was there an
        area with 100 percent parking.
     No parking signs needed to be installed near intersections and fire hydrants.
     Speeding was observed due to long stretches of unimpeded roadway. More traffic enforcement
        was needed.
     Out of 101 single-family dwellings along the corridor, seventeen had an active rental dwelling
        license.
     Between 1997 and 2019, there were 266 code enforcement cases with 95 of those for
        occupancy violations.

Data collected illustrated there was plenty of on-street parking along the corridor. Staff did not believe
that the applicant’s proposal would accomplish the desired outcome. They recommended a two-hour
parking restriction be implemented from 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., Monday through Friday. The two-hour limit
would be applied to Slate Canyon Drive from Slate Canyon Park Street (about 650 South) down to 1450
South. Staff said they could make further modifications later, if needed.

Councilor Fillmore’s primary concern was safety. He had observed the 25 mile-per-hour speed limit was
not usually followed. He suggested looking into speed bumps or other remedies to control the speed of
traffic in the neighborhood.

Councilor Handley invited public comment.

Lisa Knapp, Provo, had lived in the neighborhood for more than 20 years and was opposed to a parking
program or time restricted parking. It was unfair and made it difficult for residents to have visitors. If
approved, she would consider selling her home and leaving the area.

Hannah Petersen, applicant, said they had worked with Matt Taylor and Gary McGinn to come up with
the first plan. The details of the proposal were at their recommendations. She expressed concern about
having recommendations from someone that was not involved from the beginning. The main goal, and
the reason for Mr. McGinn’s support, was to correct some of the zoning issues. There were hundreds of
residents involved in this process. She had all of the data and comment cards from residents. Of those
that responded, there was 70 percent approval for this parking permit program on Slate Canyon Drive.
While there were 17 legal dwelling licenses in the area, there were at least 400 illegal rentals in their
neighborhood. She wanted the opportunity to present their information to the new council and parking
staff.

Cody Cutler, Provo, did not think a permit program or additional enforcement was necessary. He rarely
had issues with parking on Slate Canyon Drive. He felt this was an attempt to bully renters. He was a

young single adult renter, whose stomping grounds was in the area of Canyon Meadows. He did not
know of any over occupied rentals in the area.

Terry Fife, Provo, had lived in the neighborhood for four years and this was the first time he heard about

There were no more public comments.

Brian Jones, Council Attorney, explained the procedures that were followed for this request. Citizens
submitted a petition asking for a permit program. On October 29, 2019, the council held a public
hearing to determine public interest and forwarded the proposal to the city parking coordinator for
review. On November 10, 2020, the parking coordinator submitted a report to the council for review.
The council was required to hold a public meeting within 90 days of receiving the report (current step
for this request). During that meeting, a resolution of intent to create a parking permit program would
be voted on. If the resolution of intent were approved that night, notice would be delivered to all
affected persons of a public hearing that would happen later. During that public hearing, council would
consider formal adoption of the parking permit program.

Mr. Weaver noted that the data collected for this study was prior to the pandemic. Councilor Handley
said more people were working at home during the pandemic. Measuring the success of a new program
right now seemed difficult.

Councilor Harding was not prepared to vote against the motion, but also did not want to vote in favor of
the proposal. A lot of effort had gone into this, but the process had been disjointed. It might be helpful
to revisit the discussions from years ago when the proposal was first suggested.

Councilor Shipley agreed with Mr. Harding. He wanted to know if this was a parking issue or an
occupancy issue. If it was a safety issue, other things may need to be addressed. He did not know if this
plan would solve any of the issues.

Councilor Fillmore agreed, saying he wanted to look into resolving safety issues in this neighborhood.
He wanted to ensure the applicant shared her ideas with the appropriate audience.

Councilor Ellsworth asked Chair Handley if she could read a text from a resident, Lee McCready, who
was unable to call in.

         parking issue. I like your proposal. Sounds like the motion is a solution looking for

         Karen’s complaining because they have no bigger problems than to make

Councilor Ellsworth said there were a couple of other citizens that did not get a chance to call in or did
not know about this ahead of time. One woman, a single mother of three autistic children, felt like this
program would impact her financially. It would not be a net positive for safety.

Councilor Ellsworth understood that citizens were frustrated. She lived about 100 yards from Slate
Canyon Drive and noticed cars that did not move for sometimes weeks or months at a time. However,
there was no consensus on the problem or the solution. She thought there were two remedies that
would solve 90 percent of the headaches.

The first remedy would be for the city to allocate more resources to code enforcement and clean up
Slate Canyon Drive. The second remedy was for neighbors to be better neighbors and not speed, block
each other’s driveways, and just be neighborly. She would vote against this proposal but was okay with
a continuance. She agreed that speeding was a tremendous issue.

Gary McGinn, Community and Neighborhood Services Director, said they had worked with the neighbors
in the past and talked about parking permit programs. Permit programs could be a tool to deal with
over occupancy, but you had to be careful about determining exactly the problem you were defining.
When staff analyzed the problem, it was parking on Slate Canyon Drive, and not a neighborhood wide
occupancy problem. Rather than going to the cost and expense of a parking permit program, staff
proposed implementing two-hour parking along Slate Canyon Drive as the first phase of an on-street
parking management approach.

As for safety concerns, Mr. McGinn said this was a street where the speed limit was 25 mph but frankly,
it was designed for much greater speeds. A parking permit program would not solve that problem.
Increasing enforcement of code violations and parking violations would require additional resources and
would be a budget impact.

Councilor Handley said that 1450 East had the same problem with speeding as Slate Canyon Drive. Both
of them were designed to be faster streets than the residents would like. Engineers have told residents
of 1450 East that they could not put speed bumps on a road where the risk of speeding was high. Speed
bumps created other kinds of safety issues.

It was unfortunate that Ms. Peterson had to work with three different parking coordinators since she
started this process. Because of the length of the process, there was probably a very different set of
circumstances than when the process began. The rental dwelling license legislation, created a number
of years ago, had helped reduce over occupancy. He did not feel good about creating a law because
other laws were not being enforced. We need to make sure that occupancy laws, parking laws, and
speeding laws were enforced. This issue needed more discussion.

Ms. Ellsworth asked to read another text from a citizen.

         “We live on Slate Canyon Drive and believe parking should be enforced. If it is not
         enforced now, why should we have to pay for a parking permit? I would be much more
         comfortable with our efforts going towards slowing down drivers on the road.”

Councilor Hoban said when he went to BYU for his Masters, he would park in the free one-hour parking
and between classes, and he would move his car or wipe off the chalk. People can find ways around a
time limit for parking.

Councilor Harding said we need to either increase the resources for parking enforcement, or find
another place where we could cut back. One benefit of having paid on-street parking was it could pay
for enforcement. He was hopeful that they could find a way to pay for enforcement without pulling
from taxpayer money.

He said not all laws were created equal or as easy to enforce. Over occupancy is one of the more
difficult things to enforce. Likewise, a 72-hour parking limit was quite difficult to enforce. We had a law
that said every car operated from a property should have an off-street parking stall. We could easily
enforce that law by not allowing parking on the street between the hours of 2 a.m. to 4 a.m. or midnight
to 6 a.m. We could require residents to pay a fee to park overnight during those hours. The proposed
parking permit program did not have restrictions during the day. People could have visitors or receive
services throughout the day. There were several ways we could enforce the laws that already exist.

Councilor Harding said it might be interesting to see if Community and Neighborhood Services had an
estimate on the number of rental dwelling licenses (RDL’S) in that area. What compliance rates do we
have with RDL’s?

Councilor Hoban said enforcing the 72-hour time limit would not do anything. People would just move
their car another 20 feet or to another spot every couple of days. If the most parking problems were in
the evening or at night, he did not see how a two-hour limit during the day would do anything to help
solve the problem. He liked the idea of no parking on the street at night. People might clean out their
garages, or cram their cars in the driveway to avoid being ticketed, booted, or towed.

He had lived in some big and medium sized cities and, at some point, they had to use parking permit
programs to deal with over occupancy or parking problems. It was not practical to just start enforcing
things and expect all the problems to be solved. There needed to be some sort of program limiting
parking overnight before there would be any impact to Slate Canyon Drive. The council needed to do
something to improve the quality of life for residents in that area.

Councilor Hoban said he was okay with continuing the item but at some point they had to do something.

Councilor Ellsworth encouraged councilors to listen to, and read the comments of people who lived on
Slate Canyon Drive. This program was not a panacea for everything that ailed the neighborhood. They
needed to pay attention to the people that lived there because it was important for their quality of life.

       Motion:      Councilor Fillmore made a motion to continue this item and urged more staff study
                    and input. Councilor Shipley seconded the motion.

Councilor Handley asked if there was any council discussion on the motion.

Councilor Ellsworth wanted to be clear about what data the staff would provide.

Councilor Fillmore said staff had indicated they would look at the speed bump possibility. He also asked
staff to look at creative ways to slow down traffic on Slate Canyon Drive. He would like a larger
discussion with the administration with respect to the parking permit program. By continuing the item,
it would give staff time to prepare more options and give the residents more time to study the issues.

Councilor Ellsworth said that occupancy was a separate issue from parking and speeding. Whatever
happened that night, she intended to have a conversation with public works and the administration to
find some solutions to resolve the speeding problem.

Mr. Parker said that Public Works and Engineering staff would be attending the work meeting in two
weeks. They were prepared to present some analysis and data concerning Slate Canyon Drive. There
may be some way to restructure Slate Canyon in some fashion. It might be good to have this discussion
before reaching any conclusions about a parking program.

Chair Sewell asked Ms. Ellsworth to quantify what enforcement improvements could be made.

Ms. Ellsworth gave examples of several problems. They included:

        Vehicles parked on the street for months at a time.
        A boat parked on the street for eight months and only moved once or twice.
        A van and SUV that did not move.
        Three driveways were broken down cars were parked.
        Garages being used as attic space; which pushed cars out of the garage and onto the driveway
         or onto the streets.
        Cars from Slate Canyon Drive that park in open HOA parking stalls throughout the
         neighborhood.
        HOA residents parking out on Slate Canyon Drive.

She said residents would call 311 to report problems but things did not seem to be changing.

Chair Sewell asked Mr. McGinn if he had the resources to properly address the issues Councilor
Ellsworth raised.

Mr. McGinn said that, while they go down Slate Canyon routinely, they had all of Provo City to enforce.
Without additional resources, they would have to cut back in other areas in order to go down Slate
Canyon several times per day. If they were to take on a new residential parking permit area, they would
definitely need more help. That help would need to come from the general fund or have the permit
program pay for itself.
