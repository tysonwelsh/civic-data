Proposed Ordinance 2020-48 amending the Municipal Code by revising
provisions regarding group dwellings primarily in the Community
Commercial (C-2/CP-2) and Regional Commercial (C-3/CP-3) Zones
          A memo from the Community and Economic Development Department regarding proposed ordinances amending
apartment design standards in commercial zones came before the Council for consideration. The memo stated Ogden City
Planning is proposing amendments to the regulations for group dwellings and apartments in the City's C-2 and C-3 commercial
zones. The proposed amendments are the result of months of study with regard to multi-family dwellings in commercial zones. A
notice of pending ordinance outlining potential changes was issued in April of 2020. The notice included the option to eliminate
multi-family dwellings in the C zones; however, after study and review, the proposal would not eliminate the use but rather
introduce improved design standards and other regulations to help promote higher quality multi-family projects in commercial
zones.
          Planning Manager Montgomery summarized the memo and used the aid of a PowerPoint presentation to discuss the
proposed amendments. According to State Law, a pending ordinance is a tool to place a 180-day halt on a defmed action to
evaluate and adjust ordinances to address defmed possible changes. The pending ordinance regarding this matter was adopted in
April 21, 2020 and will expire October 18, 2020. The Planning Commission held a public hearing on the subject matter during
their September 2, 2020 meeting. The pending ordinance halted the acceptance ofnew applications for permitting in the C-2/CP-2
and C-3/CP-3 commercial zones for apartment and multi-family (three or more dwelling units in one building) and group
dwellings (two or more dwellings in two or more buildings). Staff recommended the adoption of the pending ordinance in order to
evaluate the appropriateness of the apartment and group dwelling uses in the C-2 and C-3 zones. They also wanted to evaluate
possible site design, open space, parking, screening, building design, and building materials for the uses. The pending ordinance
removed apartments and group dwellings as an allowed land use in commercial zones in order to give staff and the Planning
Commission maximum flexibility in determining the results of this discussion and ultimate action on the ordinance. He presented
a map of the City to orient the Council to the area thatthe pending ordinance applies to; this encompasses 6.5 percent of the City.
The area the pending ordinance does not apply to 10.2 percent of the City where group dwellings and multi-family apartments are
allowed. The catalyst for this proposed amendment was the dramatic increase in the amount of applications for multi-family uses
in the C-2 and C-3 zones from 2018-2020. The ratio of the number of dwellings per acre spiked drastically in that time. In the 50
years before 2018, the City has had 510 multi-family units located in the C-2 and C-3 zones; in the last two years, 893 units have
been proposed and there are another 792 units in the pre-application phase with applicants waiting for the outcome of this
ordinance. He discussed the surge of the multi-family use in the different zones in the City; 54 percent of the new units have been
located in the C-2 and C-3 zones and consumed 33 .61 acres of commercial land. The average project size is 1.9 acre and each
project has an average number ofunits of 52. Potential future projects would add another 1,033 units City-wide with 694 of them
located in the C-2 and C-3 zones. This would consume an additional 49 acres of commercial land with the average project size
being 6.1 acres. This led staff to wonder if housing is the major land use of commercial zones in the City; 52 percent of general
fund revenue comes from property tax and sales tax. It is important to preserve commercial space for commercial activity for this
reason as well as to provide services to the community and facilitate job creation. He added that for those residential uses that
have been constructed in commercial areas as of late, there has been a lack of site and design consideration and developers are
simply building what the ordinance allows. Land is set aside by zoning for permitted uses; 51 percent of the City is zoned for
residential use, while manufacturing zoned property accounts for 27 percent of the City's land, open space zoning for 11.6
percent, and commercial zoning for 10.4 percent. The non-residential zones also include permitted and nonconforming residential
uses that provide housing opportunities for the community. Projections indicate that Ogden's population will grow from 87,773 at
present day to over 116,000 in 2050; this would indicate 280 to 320 new units per year. According to the 2018 American
Communities Survey, Ogden has 33,346 dwelling units, which is 36 percent of the total dwelling units in Weber county and 3.1
percent of all dwellings in Utah. This includes many different housing types, such as single-family detached units, single-family
attached, single-family manufactured, duplexes, multi-family housing units, apartments, and high-rise buildings. He then
presented a chart offering a comparison of design standards and density allowances for the various residential zones in the City,
noting that standards for residential development in commercial areas are minimal while the density is much higher than allowed
in typical residential zones. Staff has carefully examined the review processes for different types of development applications and
found that the process for apartments in commercial zones is much simpler and quicker than for other types of projects.
Considering these factors, it is easy to see why there has been such a push for higher-density residential development in
commercial zones. He then presented a graph providing an assessment of recent projects, noting many of the projects share similar
characteristics relating to setbacks, design features, and building materials. He then discussed parking trends; parking dictates a
project's density and failure to provide adequate parking can hinder the owner's ability to lease units. However, developers must
strike a balance relative to parking because over-parking a project increase the costs of the project. In 2018, parking requirements
were adjusted to require 1.5 stalls per housing unit. Mr. Montgomery then discussed underutilized vacant commercial zoned lands
in the City; there are 160 parcels totaling 74.8 acres - the average size of each parcel is half-acre. There are 277 single-family
zoned commercial parcels for a total of 45.6 acres with the average size being 7,000 square feet. There are seven parcels over an
acre for a total of 40 acres, with the average size 5. 7 acres. General observations about property taxation indicate that multi-family
uses have higher valuations, but those uses are only taxed at 55 percent of their value. He then stated that if the Council is going to
proceed with an ordinance amendment, it is important to have a clear understanding of the potential implications; developers are

key in implementing a new plan, yet there are two types of developers - short tenn and long tenn. Marketability of a project will
draw someone to want to live in it and attention to detail will ensure that the project is durable. The influx of residential
development on commercial property means that barriers to securing approval of a project are low and 'the worst thing in the
world is not an empty lot but wishing that lot was empty'. A long-range perspective is impmiant; housing structures have a much
longer life than commercial structures, but they have less flexibility to adopt to changes. Additionally, everyone should be able to
reside in a safe, livable, and durable dwelling. The proposed ordinance has been evaluated for its consistency with the General
Plan, which has the following principles:
               • Higher densities along major transportation corridors
               • Utilize design and mixed use along corridors
               •    Create site and building design guidelines
               • Expand the variety of housing types
               • Balance mix of services, retail and tourism industries.
          The criteria for the revision include creating common ground between group dwellings and multi-unit apartments;
ensuring appropriate use of cmmnercial lands and mixes ofuses; and setting standards that will create an Ogden product, simplify
processes, and have clarify and flexibility. The first revision relates to use of cmmnercial zones and calls for appropriate balance
between job creation and services. Both uses compete for major corridors and there are 74 acres of additional undeveloped or
underutilized commercial property. He presented a map that identified future development centers and districts throughout the
City to ensure appropriate cmmnercial use of commercial property. There is allowance for consideration of multi-unit dwellings in
commercial areas, so long as four acres or more than 25 percent of the land area is used for a cmmnercial component. This could
be accomplished using free standing single use buildings, mixed-use buildings, or a combination ofboth. In key village centers, no
more than 30 percent ofland use can be multi-family. The ordinance also includes parking revisions to ensure right size parking
by giving developers a range to meet target occupants. Parking ratios are proposed at 1. 75 to 2 .1 per m1it in cmmnercial zones, 1.3
to 1.75 stalls per unit for projects within a defmed proximity of public transit, or .8 to 1.75 stalls per unit for senior or low to
moderate income housing. Setbacks for residential development in the C-3 zone will be increased from zero to 15 feet with
options for front reductions for entrance projections or floors raised three feet above the sidewalk. Setbacks are important to
ensure privacy of residents living on the ground floor and to accommodate any future road widening projects. Open space
requirements for multi-family projects include the following:
               •    Open Space 20% of lot area excluding required setbacks for useable open space amenity area
               •    Decrease C-2 from 30% to 20%, C-3 retain at 20%
               • Exclude setbacks from open space calculation
               •    Includes indoor areas for more than 16 units
               • 20% includes total of indoor and outdoor
               •     Indoor space starts at 400 square feet and increase with unit count
               • Types of indoor and outdoor amenities options based on market seeking to attract.
          Open space requirements could be reduced to 10 percent if the project is located within a quarter-mile walking distance
of a public park. The ordinance also includes architectural standards for multi-family projects, namely for the entry design,
orientation of the project, difference between individual entrances and main entrances of multi-family units, and the canopy or
recess as the defining feature of a building. Mr. Montgomery offered photographs of different types of entryways to illustrate the
importance of architectural standards, after which he discussed the proposals relating to building materials for multi-family
projects; he emphasized at least two different building materials will be required. He added that utilities must also be screened
from public view and dumpster enclosure locations and designs will require approval from the City. For group dwellings, the
ordinance includes revisions to ensure that density matches the same density for a zone where multi-family development can take
place. Difficult equations to secure bonus density will be removed and replaced with a simpler percentage-based formula. The
maximum density increase in residential zones will be 10 percent, or 20 percent in commercial zones. Building material,
architecture standards, and other design criteria will be imposed on group dwellings similar to those for multi-family residential
development. Parking requirements for group dwellings will mirror those for commercial zones, with additions for bicycle
parking, electric charging stations, and covered stalls. Landscaping minimmns for group dwellings include a 400 square foot plaza
with seating and shade as well as three amenities chosen from a list of optional amenities in the ordinance. Connectivity of
sidewalks from parking and landscape amenities will also be required. The bonus density allowances for group dwellings mirror
those that are recommended for multi-family projects.
          Mr. Montgomery concluded the Planning Commission reviewed the proposal at its September 3, 2020 meeting and
forwarded a recmmnendation of approval to the Council with a vote of 7-1. The Commission made its recommendation based on
the finding that the amendments are consistent with the General Plan and the purpose of the commercial zones.
          Council member Lopez inquired as to the reason one commissioner voted in opposition. Mr. Montgomery explained that
Commissioner Boykin voted against the recommendation citing concern that the amendments may have unintended consequences
or implications beyond the scope of the proposal. Council member Lopez asked how Planning staff responded to that concern. Mr.
Montgomery stated that Planning staff has tried to examine and evaluate present standards to determine co1Tective measures that
are aimed at protecting the City against continued development. While staff does not believe the ordinances are perfect or that

the City will reve1t to the previous standards.
           Chair Choberka asked what other efforts Planning staff would have been expended in evaluating this matter if they had a
greater budget or more time to consider the issue. Mr. Montgomery stated that staff would have possibly pursued a fonn-based
code including improved graphics or calibrations. However, he stands behind the ordinances that have been proposed and feels
they are sufficient for addressing concerns that have been raised regarding the recent spike in multi-family development in
commercial areas.
           Council member Hyer agreed; he likened the proposed ordinances to 'getting the barn door shut before all the cows were
able to escape'; he does not believe this should be the end of the discussion of this issue, but he was pleasantly surprised by the
comprehensive amendments after just six months of work. His greatest concern has been the loss of all remaining commercial
ground to multi-family residential projects and the ordinances that have been proposed address those concerns.
           Council member Stephens added he also appreciates the work that has been done by Planning staff and the Planning
Commission. He believes the ordinances will improve standards for residential development in commercial areas. He then asked if
projects will be limited to two or three colors. Mr. Montgomery stated that the ordinance provides multiple options relative to
coloration of a project; these options are intended to create variation in a project. The Planning Commission will be the approval
authority for any request for variation from the coloring regulations.
           Council member White stated she is hopeful that any process to consider coloring variations will be expedited so that a
project is not delayed while an applicant waits for a decision on that matter. Mr. Montgomery indicated the Planning Commission
meets monthly and any application for adjustments to color requirements could be heard and considered in a single meeting.
           Council member Lopez referred again to unintended consequences; he is concerned that the ordinances do not address
housing affordability; this was discussed briefly during the September 22 work session. While he believes that the ordinances that
have been presented are appropriate, he is concerned that increasing design standards will make the housing units in a project less
affordable. He stated he does not want to create a situation where blue-collar workers are not able to afford quality housing in
Ogden and, instead, they are forced to live in sub-standard housing.
           Chair Choberka agreed and inquired as to whether Planning staff considers housing affordability when working to
address design standards. Mr. Montgomery stated that staff focuses on opportunities forreducing land costs for developers, such
as lowering parking standards to make more area available for unit development, which increases affordability. Additionally,
reducing or eliminating minimum square footage of a unit aids developers in creating more affordable units. Finally, consideration
of increased density can help to offset costs. All developers are paying market rates for land, which contributes to unit costs;
unless developers receive subsidies, they are unable to lower costs artificially in order to achieve housing affordability. Council
member Lopez and Chair Choberka thanked Mr. Montgomery for that response; Council member Lopez stated he feels City
Administration and the City Council need to work together closely to develop a strategy for addressing housing affordability.

more Council members, one fmancial expert, and citizen representatives. Ms. Eller-Smith then noted staff would recommend the
Committee meet a minimum of twice a year and consider compensating the financial expert and citizen members. Responsibilities
of the Committee would include recommending the appointment of an independent auditor for the City; review of fmancial
policies, procedures, internal controls, and use of government assets; and review the annual fmancial audit and make
recommendations to the Council based on the fmdings of an audit. Based upon discussion during the September 22, 2020 work
session meeting and continued discussion with Council leadership, Council staff recommends that Council members Hyer and
White as well as the City's Financial Advisory, Laura Lewis, be appointed to the Committee as voting members. Citizen
representatives will not be appointed at this time, but the Committee can consider whether it is appropriate to appoint citizen
