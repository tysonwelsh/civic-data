2.1. Water Department Updates - Justun Edwards, Public Works Director
Public Works Director Justun Edwards offered an update of the summer water demands and usage compared
to prior years, as well as commented on various water pressure issues which have developed as Herriman’s
water system grew. He started with the 2020 summer water consumption compared to previous years. He
provided numbers both on the number of gallons of water used and on the number of meters. This summer,
Herriman used over a billion gallons of water. The City saw a 27-percent increase in water usage since 2017.
This matched the 27-percent increase in meter growth since 2017. This indicated that Herriman is in fact not
using more water per household as Jordan Valley alluded to in a previous presentation. This indicates water
conservation has actually been occurring. Director Edwards noted this summer has been a higher water
demand, but water usage has not exceeded the growth in individuals being added to the system. Conservation
has helped. However, the numbers indicate this summer Herriman has used drastically larger volumes of water
than in previous years, something that causes the water system to struggle. The water tanks have not had the
opportunity to recover because of the usage along with the lack of rain events this year.

Public Works Director Justun Edwards presented on the water pressure issues going on in The Cove. He
highlighted the peak day demands were staying above the 40-psi threshold. During peak hour demands,
typically at 6:00 a.m. when the majority of people have their sprinklers on, the state minimum is 30 psi. The
City had not dropped below that level. Director Edwards explained the effects of increasing demands. The
August 26, 2020 – City Council Minutes                                             Page 4 of 20
master planned water infrastructure was designed for buildout conditions. During early development, system
pressures were higher due to lower demands and as the water demand increase the overall system pressure
would gradually decrease. Director Edwards reviewed noted during the winter, or low demand days, the
system’s pressure would typically be in the 50-psi range.

Public Works Director Edwards talked about source and storage sites within the system. He said storage and
conveyance were installed in strategic places in the system where they would be most efficient and can ensure
there will be good coverage in the system as a whole as the City grows. There are often gaps in the system
which is why it is important to build new connections to ensure access and certain water routes are not overly
congested.

Recognizing the need for system adjustments, staff had been looking at the City’s hydraulic model and
performing various scenarios to try and figure out what was causing the decrease in pressures the City had not
seen in the past. In addition, staff implemented the installation of pressure monitoring devices to be able to
combine actual field data with hydraulic model data to make more informed system adjustments. Currently,
the City has some instruments attached to the outside of hydrants; however, they have to be removed in the
winter. Staff had looked into more advanced instruments which can be installed within the hydrant to have
more comprehensive data.

He transitioned to talking about the infrastructure improvements currently in design. These included the East
Herriman Route and the Mountain View Corridor. He said staff looked to build a zone two and zone three
tank, a pump station near the City’s existing one-million-gallon tank and then making connections to existing
developments. He noted these would not be installed for the high demands of the summer of 2021, but would
be completed by the fall. However, staff had been working with developers to ensure they were installing the
infrastructure to be able to make connections to the new tanks and pumps.

Another project discussed was the Jordan Valley Connection at the zone two-million-gallon tank to connect
to the line installed for bullfrog and run it down to through the future 64000 West alignment and make a
connection into the zone three water pressure zone. Zones three and four were the areas being hit the hardest
and adding to their source would help elevate their pressures. Because of this, staff intend for this to be a
priority project to occur prior to the City’s peak watering season in 2021.

2.3. Discussion of the Parks, Recreation and Open Space Impact Fee Enactment - Chase Andrizzi,
City Attorney
City Attorney Chase Andrizzi talked about the process for imposing impact fees. He explained under state
statute in order to charge impact fees, a City has to pass an impact fee enactment ordinance. State code requires
the City hold a public hearing and provide the public ten-days to review the enactment ordinance before being
approved by the Council. The enactment was really nothing more than an ordinance to adopt the impact fees
and sets the rate. The impact fee analysis and the impact fee facilities plan were the documents which describe
what the highest rate and the method for achieving the rate, but the enactment the Council used to impose the
impact fee establishes the rate. State statute prohibits the enactment from establishing a rate any higher than
what was outlined in the impact fee analysis; however, the Council could choose to impose a lower rate.

He noted although staff had already held a public hearing for the impact fee analysis and impact fee facilities
plan, a separate public hearing needed to be held for the impact fee enactment. This was scheduled for
September 9, 2020. After the Council had heard public feedback, they could either approve the impact fee
enactment or make recommended changes which could then be approved at a later meeting. Unlike other
ordinances that were effective the day of their approval, the impact fee enactment would not be effective for
90 days.

It was noted impact fees only apply to new developments and not to existing homes or residents. New
construction would pay impact fees to help support new infrastructure and parks based on the impact they
have on the system.

7.1. Economic Development Strategic Plan Presentation - Bonneville Research
Bonneville Research representatives Bob Springmeyer and Tom Burdett provided an update on Herriman’s
economic development strategic plan. Mr. Springmeyer said Herriman was growing fast. The City’s population
had nearly doubled since the last census and would be likely to add an addition 7,200 households in the next
seven years. He was impressed by the fact that Herriman was a city of young families. There was almost an
equal number of school aged individuals as those who were in the prime earning age from 25-44. Mr.
Springmeyer stated Herriman was the center of the rapidly growing southwest Salt Lake County region.
Herriman could no longer be described as being out here, but rather was here: an integrated part of Salt Lake
County. Herriman’s household growth occurred 76-percent faster than the average household growth across
the state and 400-percent faster than household growth in the United States. In addition, Herriman was the
transportation hub for the Bangerter and Mountain View corridor. However, Herriman was unique in that the
City still had 22-percent of developable land. The city could reach a build out population of 115,000 people.

Mr. Springmeyer continued to offer findings of the strategic plan. Herriman residents were well educated with
50-percent of residents 25 or older having a college degree. Residents are also well paid with the average
household income about 50-percent higher than the Salt Lake County median. 79.2-percent of residents were
homeowners with a high median home value just over $382,000. These residents work as professionals in
areas such as business, sales, and administrative functions, with a low number of residents in trade jobs such

Based on business license reports, Bonneville Research found only 17.5-percent of Herriman businesses have

Herriman lacked a strong city core.

Herriman, however, was the center of the rapidly growing southwest region of the valley. Statewide there was

10.1. Discussion and consideration of a Resolution to initiate the process by which Herriman City
may consider withdrawal of the municipal territory of Herriman City from the Unified Fire Service
Area - Brett Wood, City Manager
City Manager Brett Wood said there have been many conversations about the Unified Fire Service Area. He
wanted to reiterate the motion to withdraw from the UFSA had nothing to do with service with Unified Fire
Authority, but rather to take control of the financial aspect of the services. By withdrawing, if Herriman
decided to increase the fire service the Mayor and Council would have total control of fees paid by residents.
After numerous conversations with the board Unified Fire will honor Herriman’s separation come January 1,
2021. He said staff had already contacted Zion’s Bank to start the fire Impact Fee Study by the beginning of
October and to get the new tax district formed and approved before next year. The resolution was just to
solidify providing the Unified Fire Service Area Herriman’s letter of intent to leave.

Councilmember Henderson said that the Council had talked about this extensively but wanted to reiterate for
the public that the decision to withdraw from UFSA was not because of problems with services, but so that
the Council and staff could better control funding. In fact, UFSA would still be the service provider, but
Herriman would have better control over the tax funds for the service.
August 26, 2020 – City Council Minutes                                            Page 18 of 20
Councilmember Smith said the dialogue with the UFSA board had been going on for several months with
Councilmember Henderson representing the city. He said Riverton was the first city to separate from the
district and that he appreciated their support of Herriman doing the same. Councilmember Smith also
commented on the openness and transparency of UFSA staff during the process. Despite some bumps in the
road along the way he was grateful to arrive at a place where Herriman and UFSA were on amendable terms.

Councilmember Shields said that he had been a part of some recent conversations where people have compared
the withdraw from UFSA to actions such has when the City previously divorced themselves with the Unified
Police Department of Greater Salt Lake (UPD). He noted that leaving the UFSA was distinctly different
because the City was not separating their service from the UFSA but had determined that it was in the City’s
best interest to contract directly for those services rather than using the UFSA as the middle man for those
services. This meant that Herriman was only responsible for the City’s services as opposed to when being a
part of the UFSA Herriman might have been subsidizing other cities that were not growing as fast as Herriman.
He said given the current climate individuals have claimed the Council was not trying to save taxpayers money,
but to defund fire service providers. He wanted to unequivocally say that the Council would stand behind first
responders 100-percent and would offer whatever support first responders needed despite some ridiculous
statements from residents claiming otherwise. He said this measures to most effectively utilize taxpayer’s
money had nothing to do with the Council’s support of fireman and police officers.

Councilmember Smith agreed and said the primary reason for the move was to make sure that tax funds were
protected for residents.

Councilmember Ohrn stated often times when people make comments such as those addressed by
Councilmember s Shields and Smith it is because they do not understand the situation. She was confident if
residents took the time to educate themselves they would understand that Herriman appreciates its first
responders and their relationship with UFSA.

Mayor Watts said the Council might not have been aware that there were several fire personnel in attendance
at the meeting in support of the functions that they provide for the City. He also wanted to echo what was
