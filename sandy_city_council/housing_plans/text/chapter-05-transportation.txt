1.0    EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Sandy City is a vibrant community located in the southeastern portion of the
greater Salt Lake City metropolitan area. Sandy City’       s vision for a more
centralized business core while preserving and maintaining quality residential
neighborhoods and employment opportunities is becoming a reality. A forward
thinking, comprehensive transportation system that addresses all modes of travel
is crucial to the long-term health of the City. Sandy’ s location requires that its
transportation system must not only serve the transportation needs of the City,
but, also the demands placed upon it by traffic from the adjacent communities.

The City has undertaken this update to its Master Transportation Plan (MTP)
after recognizing the rapid growth and development that has occurred and is
expected to continue in the City. The previous MTP was prepared in 1996. For
this updated version a special focus was placed on the downtown area or the
central business district (CBD) due to the extensive growth that has occurred
since the release of the previous plan. The primary intent of this plan is to
examine the existing transportation network as of 2008, evaluate the changes
from the previous MTP, and propose improvements to accommodate existing
and future transportation demands. Included in the proposed improvements is
the identification of funding sources and planning level cost estimates.

The Sandy City Transportation Committee, which is composed of citizens
throughout the community, elected officials, and city staff, was a major
contributor to the development of the Sandy City MTP. A primary role of the
Transportation Committee and an important part of Sandy’          s MTP was the
development of the goals and policies section which outlines the general
transportation desires of the community. The purpose of this section is to assist
planners and engineers in the development of guidelines, standards, and
solutions that are in accordance with the unique demands of the community. City
staff and officials can use this section to evaluate transportation alternatives not
specifically identified in the City’
                                   s MTP, and recommend alternatives that are in
harmony with the transportation needs and desires of the City.

Existing conditions data was collected in order to accurately evaluate Sandy
City’s transportation system and develop a MTP that would address the City’        s
current and future needs. In addition to the data contained in the existing MTP,
data collected included socioeconomic, land use and zoning, roadway
classifications, key roadway and intersection traffic volumes, signal locations,
public transit routes, and bicycle/pedestrian/equestrian trails. A special focus was
placed on the downtown central business district (CBD), which, for the purposes
of this MTP, was identified as the area between I-15 and State Street and from
9000 South to 11400 South. This focus included a detailed analysis of 23 CBD
intersections which were selected with input from the City Transportation
Engineer.

The existing traffic volumes and infrastructure were used to evaluate existing

starting point to forecast future transportation conditions. A travel demand model

was obtained from Wasatch Front Regional Council (WFRC) and supplemented

High Land Use scenario included additional development that City staff felt might
occur in Sandy. Similarly the Low Build scenario includes all the 2030
transportation improvements currently identified by Sandy City and included in
WFRC’   s Long Range Plan. The High Build scenario added a northbound on and
a southbound off ramp on I-15 between the existing freeway interchanges at
9000 South and 10600 South, plus some additional improvements.

potential funding sources were combined to create the recommended
transportation improvement program (TIP). The TIP provides a planning-level
cost estimate for each improvement and identifies potential funding sources. The
Capital Facilities Plan that was prepared by Horrocks Engineers includes the cost

and implemented, the TIP can be a valuable tool for City Officials in the
budgeting and planning process because it presents anticipated time lines, costs,
and potential funding sources for the transportation improvements identified in
this MTP.

2.0    INTRODUCTION
Nestled against the beautiful Wasatch Mountains, Sandy City is a vibrant
community located in the southeastern portion of the Salt Lake Valley, as shown
in Figure 2. Sandy is bounded by Midvale City and Salt Lake County to its north,
Draper City and Salt Lake County to its south, and West Jordan City and South
Jordan City to its west. Along the western edge of the City runs Interstate-15
which is the primary north-south travel route through the Salt Lake Valley and the
State of Utah. Due to its location, Sandy City should plan to not only serve its
own transportation needs, but the traffic demands placed on its transportation
network from adjacent communities. Therefore, a comprehensive transportation
system is vital to the long-term health of Sandy City.

Recognizing that rapid growth and development have occurred over the past
several years, and expecting that growth will continue for the foreseeable future,
Sandy City has undertaken this update to its Master Transportation Plan (MTP).
A special focus has been placed on the downtown area, or the Central Business
District (CBD), due to the extensive growth that has occurred in this area since
the release of the previous plan in 1996 and the additional development that is
anticipated in the future.

The primary intent of this plan is to examine the existing transportation network
as of 2008, evaluate the changes from the previous MTP and propose
improvements to accommodate future demands. Significant changes from the
previous MTP include the addition of light rail and commuter rail within the City.
Proposed improvements are identified along with potential funding sources and
planning level cost estimates. The MTP serves as an element of the City’         s
general plan that focuses on transportation issues throughout the City.

3.0    TRANSPORTATION GOALS AND POLICIES
The goals and policies outlined in this section provide general direction for City
staff and officials as they evaluate decisions that are not specifically addressed in
the MTP. The goals and policies from the 1996 MTP were used as a starting
point to develop this section of the report. These goals and policies have been
reviewed and approved by the City’    s Transportation Committee.

3.1    Streets
GOAL: Design, maintain, and operate streets that provide safe and effective
movement of people and goods.

POLICIES:

   1. Street Improvements – Minimize the negative impacts of traffic through the
      on-going monitoring and improvement of the overall transportation system.

   2. Design Standards – Require all roads and roadway features to meet
      minimum design standards established by the American Association of
      State Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO); require all signs,
      pavement markings, and traffic control signals to meet standards
      established by the Manual of Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD),
      and related roadway standards established by State, Federal, or local law.
      Exceptions to applicable standards may be granted by the City Traffic
      Engineer on a case-by-case basis and shall demonstrate innovative
      superiority or other advantages over existing standards.

   3. Multi-Modal Streets – Design roadways to maximize use for all modes of
      transportation including: personal vehicles, trucks, transit, bicycles, and
      pedestrians. Encourage a practical balance between multiple modes while
      realizing that private passenger vehicles will continue to be an important
      means of transportation.

   4. Coordination – Coordinate the development and improvement of streets
      with adjacent jurisdictions, Salt Lake County, Wasatch Front Regional
      Council (WFRC) the Metropolitan Planning Organization, Utah Transit
      Authority (UTA), and the Utah Department of Transportation (UDOT).

   5. Access Management – Implement access management practices that will
      promote safety and improve mobility on major roadways while providing
      an overall system that will support adjacent land uses. Discourage local
      street access directly onto arterials.

   6. Cut-Through Traffic – Discourage cut through and high speed travel on
      local roads by maintaining a high level of mobility on regional (collector



Draft Sandy Master Transportation Plan   9                                  July 2009
       and arterial) roadways and controlling roadway and vehicle traffic
       conditions on local residential streets.

   7. Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) – Consider solutions that will
      increase capacity without necessarily increasing the number of roadways
      or travel lanes. Improve the efficiency of the transportation system by
      optimizing traffic signal timing and using ITS technologies.

   8. Emergency Response – Promote a street system that prioritizes
      accessibility and mobility for emergency response services.

   9. Maintenance – Consider maintenance aspects in the planning and design
      of the street system. Minimize life cycle costs through preventative
      maintenance.

   10. Utilities – Coordinate the location of utilities and street improvements to
       reduce long-term costs and minimize disruptions to traffic mobility.

3.2    Safety
GOAL: Build and maintain a safe transportation system.

POLICIES:

   1. Safety Improvements – Improve safety through the use of signals, signs,
      street markings, and street lighting.

   2. Intersection Conflicts – Decrease intersection conflicts to reduce the
      frequency and severity of transportation-related collisions.

   3. Traffic Calming – Implement traffic calming practices to achieve
      appropriate vehicular speeds and pedestrian safety for residential, mixed-
      use, and multi-modal areas.

   4. School Routes – Work with schools to provide safe pedestrian routes to
      schools.

   5. Pedestrians – Provide a safe pedestrian system. Enhance safety by
      reducing vehicle-pedestrian conflicts. Consider alternative intersection
      designs that reduce the street crossing distances for pedestrians.

   6. Bicycles – Provide a safe bicycle system. Enforce state laws and local
      ordinances concerning the use of bicycles to promote bicycle safety.

   7. Railroad Crossings – Work with State and railroad officials to identify ways
      to alleviate existing railroad crossing safety hazards. Consider flashing
      lights, mechanical arms, and/or grade separation at railroad crossings to
      reduce auto-train conflicts.

   8. Speed Limits – Establish speed limits based on traffic engineering
      analysis. Enforce speed limits, especially near schools, residential
      neighborhoods, and other areas with considerable pedestrian activity.

   9. Bridges – Improve or replace bridges that create safety hazards.

3.3    Transit
GOAL: Increase transportation mode share and convenience of transit service
within city.

POLICIES:

   1. Coordination – Increase coordination and collaboration with the UTA.
      Continue to foster regular multi-level contact with UTA to keep lines of
      communications open. Review land use plans and infrastructure
      improvement plans with UTA to coordinate future transit services in the
      city.

   2. Rapid Transit – Encourage UTA to develop future rapid transit services,
      including light rail, bus rapid transit, and commuter rail stops and facilities
      within the city.

   3. Downtown – Encourage regional transit access into Sandy’  s downtown,
      along with local transit service within downtown, and connecting to the
      regional system.

   4. Transit Stops – Provide good auto, bus, bicycle, and pedestrian access to
      existing and future transit stations and facilities. Encourage UTA to
      provide shelters and other amenities that improve the safety and comfort
      of transit passengers.

   5. Special Events – Encourage use of transit for special events to relieve
      vehicular traffic and parking demand.

   6. Transit Oriented Development – Encourage Transit Oriented Development
      (TOD). Require commercial development to consider integrating transit
      and alternative modes into site plans by accommodating/building transit
      stops and pedestrian connections.

3.4    Bicycles and Pedestrians
GOAL: Make walking and bicycling a viable, convenient, and safe mode of
transportation.

POLICIES:

   1. Parks, Recreation, and Trails – Support the goals and recommendations
      of the Parks, Recreation, and Trails Element of the Sandy City General
      Plan. Coordinate street improvement projects with construction of trails
      (bicycle, walking, equestrian, and multipurpose).

   2. Connectivity – Improve the connectivity of sidewalks, trails, and paths.
      Provide and maintain a continuous and direct network of trails and on-
      street bicycle lanes and paths. Require new development and
      redevelopment plans to provide pedestrian connections to adjacent uses,
      transit facilities, and roadways.

   3. Design Criteria – Adopt and follow design and alignment criteria
      recommended by the Parks, Recreation, and Trails Element of the Sandy
      City General Plan, including trail widths.

   4. Accessibility – Provide facilities that will support the elderly and
      handicapped and comply with ADA accessibility requirements.

   5. Sidewalks – Require sidewalks on both sides of roadways.

   6. Regional Facilities – Coordinate with neighboring jurisdictions, UDOT, and
      the MPO to develop future bicycle facilities. Coordinate street
      improvements projects with construction of bicycle trails.

   7. Maintenance – Consider maintenance and aesthetic aspects in the
      planning and design of sidewalks, trails, and recreational facilities.

3.5    Parking
GOAL: Provide parking facilities that meet the needs and functional requirements
of surrounding land uses and streets.

POLICIES:

   1. School Areas – Work with schools to improve circulation at pick-up/drop-
      off areas and resolve parking issues as well as pick-up/drop-off issues.

   2. Downtown – Encourage drivers to “park once”and get around using other
      modes (walking, transit, etc.) in downtown and other activity centers where
      multiple trip purposes can be accomplished with a single automobile trip.

   3. Special Events – Develop a special event parking and circulation plan that
      considers available off-street and on-street parking and will minimize
      congestion during event peak traffic times.



Draft Sandy Master Transportation Plan   12                             July 2009
   4. Park-and-ride – Encourage the construction and public use of park-and-
      ride facilities along I-15, within or near major commercial centers, and at
      key transit stop locations.

   5. Bike Racks – Encourage the installation of bicycle racks or lockers within
      commercial / retail centers and transit stop locations.

3.6    Travel Demand Management
GOAL: Relieve congestion by reducing vehicle miles traveled per capita.

POLICIES:

   1. Land Use Planning – Integrate transportation and land use planning to
      reduce the length and frequency of vehicle trips and ensure that existing
      and planned transportation facilities meet the travel demands generated
      by surrounding land uses.

   2. Mode Choice Awareness – Increase awareness of transportation mode
      choices and their consequences. Support UTA’   s ride share and carpool
      efforts. Support UDOT’s park-and-ride lot program and the Travel Wise
      initiative.

   3. Alternatives to Travel – Support infrastructure and technology
      improvements that better enable telecommuting, remote shopping, and
      other alternatives to travel.

The goals and policies described above should be used by City planners,
engineers and community leaders on a regular basis as they evaluate
transportation needs and alternatives. Additionally, this section along with the
rest of the MTP should be updated as development and transportation
alternatives change in the City.

4.3    Roadway Functional Classification
A balance must exist in transportation planning to provide adequate roadways
that serve the transportation needs of the City and provide connectivity to the
region, while maintaining the overall quality of life in neighborhoods throughout
the City. This task is accomplished primarily by the establishment of a roadway
hierarchy. This hierarchy is called the functional classification of roadways. The
classification of roadways allows planners to plan for major corridors that serve
regional movements, and smaller roads intended to serve primarily local traffic
movements and provide direct access to homes. Further balance can be
achieved by adequately planning for major corridors and coordinating land use
with transportation planning. This will minimize through traffic in neighborhoods,
and allow dense development and high traffic generators to be located near high
capacity roadways and transit facilities to facilitate the safe and efficient
movement of people and goods throughout the City.

         4.7       Alternative Transportation Modes
         Car pooling, UTA light rail (TRAX), and UTA bus transit are the most widely used
         forms of alternative transportation in Sandy City. Approximately 11 percent of
         Sandy City residents car pool each day according to the 2000 US Census
         Commuting to Work information. This is a slight decrease from the 13 percent
         that was reported in the 1990 census. Car pool is the second highest mode of
         transportation next to private automobile.

         There are a variety of local bus routes serving important commercial,
         employment, and residential areas within the City. There are also a few express
         routes which provide more rapid service, although on a more limited basis, to
         regional destinations in the downtown central business districts of Salt Lake City,
         Orem, Provo, and Ogden. UTA bus routes are updated regularly and the most
         current transit information includes bus routes, light rail, and commuter rail
         schedules and fares can be found on UTA’  s website at www.rideuta.com.

         Currently, there are three light rail stations located within the Sandy City limits,
         which include Historic Sandy just north of 9000 South, Sandy Expo just north of
         9400 South, and Sandy Civic Center, the current southern terminus of the north/
         south TRAX line at approximately 10000 South. The use of car pools, buses and
         light rail is facilitated by the availability of park-and-ride locations in Sandy City.

4.9    Traffic Conditions
Due to the rapid growth of Sandy and the surrounding areas, infrastructure
improvements have not been able to keep pace with the increase in traffic in
Sandy and throughout the region. This has led to an increase in congestion and
a decline in traffic operations. A PM peak hour level of service (LOS) analysis
was completed using Synchro 7 software at each of the 23 intersections listed
previously in order to determine what, if any, improvements were needed based
on existing conditions.

LOS is a term used to describe and quantify traffic conditions and their
perception by motorists. A LOS definition generally describes these conditions in
terms of such factors as speed and travel time, freedom to maneuver, traffic
interruptions, and delay. There are six levels of service describing these
conditions, ranging from A to F, which have been standardized by the
Transportation Research Board. LOS A represents a free-flowing traffic condition
where motorists are affected very little by other motorists, and the level of
comfort and convenience to the motorist is excellent. LOS F is characterized by
congested conditions. Motorists have little if any freedom to choose speeds or
lanes of travel, and experience discomfort, inconvenience, and long delays.

2008 PM peak hour existing conditions. The LOS for all the study intersections in

5.0    FUTURE CONDITIONS
Projecting future travel and associated travel conditions is a function of projected
land use and socioeconomic conditions. Travel is not restricted to municipal
boundaries and must be considered on a region-wide basis. Future projections of
land use and socioeconomic data were obtained from WFRC and supplemented
by data from Sandy City.

Four future scenarios were considered including Low Land Use and High Land
Use scenarios under Low Build and High Build conditions. The main difference
between Low Build and High Build model runs was the addition of a Northbound
I-15 On-Ramp and a Southbound I-15 Off-Ramp at 10000 South. Detailed
analysis of the CBD includes the evaluation of 23 intersections for each of the
four scenarios.

5.1    Projected Land Use and Socioeconomic Conditions
As previously stated, a detailed analysis of land use and socioeconomic data is
beyond the scope of this MTP update. However, since future transportation
needs are interconnected with future land use and socioeconomic conditions,
some documentation is required. The land use and socioeconomic projections
used in this transportation analysis were obtained from WFRC with additional
input from Sandy City including data from traffic impact studies that have already
been completed and submitted to Sandy City.

The evaluation of existing conditions was focused on the CBD; similarly, the
projection and evaluation of future socioeconomic conditions were also focused
on the CBD. Socioeconomic projections included Low Land Use and High Land
Use scenarios. The Low Land Use scenario consists mainly of growth already
included in the WFRC model while the High Land Use scenario includes
additional possible growth within the CBD. Major developments within the CBD
include developments around the ReAL soccer stadium and the construction of a
large mixed-use project, which is planned as three 30-story buildings located
near City Hall. The expected increase in density within the CBD required a
redefinition of land uses within the CBD. Projected land use changes were
developed using data from Sandy City.

Each of the four alternatives were analyzed to develop recommendations for

       •       Roadways

recommendations for the preferred alternative to be incorporated into the MTP.

6.1    General Recommendations
Advances in technology have allowed for the development of intelligent
transportation systems (ITS). ITS is a low cost way to improve traffic flow.
According to the Research and Innovative Technology Administration (RITA), ITS
can be beneficial in the following ways:

       •       Reduced Environmental Impacts
       •       Increased Safety
       •       Improved Traffic Flow
       •       Reduced Impact of Incidents
       •       Improved Transit Efficiency

One of the most cost effective ways to implement ITS is by maintaining signal
coordination and optimization. Sandy City should work with UDOT to coordinate
traffic signals on state routes and, if the City takes control of the signals currently
controlled by Salt Lake County, they should prepare a plan to update signal
timing at these locations annually to maintain traffic signal coordination and
minimize travel delays.

6.2    Central Business District (CBD)
Most of the improvements included in the high build scenario affect primarily the
CBD. The effects of each alternative on the CBD were analyzed in detail and the
recommended improvements are summarized below. Generally, during the peak
periods higher congestion levels are expected along roadways within the CBD
and LOS E is acceptable for these intersections during peak periods.

6.2.1 Roadways

10600 South

10600 South experiences congestion today; however the construction of the
11400 South interchange is expected to relieve some of that congestion. By 2030
congestion levels along this corridor are estimated to be similar to those
experienced today.

South Towne Mall is a major traffic generator with access to 10600 South. The
development projected near Sandy Civic Center is also expected to contribute to
traffic growth on 10600 South. Primary access from 10600 South to these
locations is provided at the 10600 South/Automall Drive intersection. A critical
movement at this intersection is the eastbound left-turn. Direct access to the Mall
Ring Road from 10600 South could decrease the heavy left-turn volume and
improve the operation of the overall corridor. A fly over structure would be
required to provide free, direct access onto Mall Ring Road. This type of
improvement would require additional study and would be a costly improvement.

10000 South

Much of the growth expected within the CBD is expected to occur near 10000
South. The high build scenarios also include a half-interchange with I-15 on
10000 South, which is expected to cause additional growth on 10000 South. The
growth projected on 10000 South requires that 10000 South be widened from
South Jordan Parkway to State Street to accommodate the increase in traffic.

6.2.2 Traffic Signals and Intersections

As shown in Table 6, five intersections within the CBD are expected to operate at
LOS F under both the Low Land Use - High Build and High Land Use - High
Build scenarios. The following paragraphs identify these intersections and the
potential mitigation measures to improve their level of service. It is important to
note that the need for these improvements could occur before 2030, and each
intersection should be monitored to determine when and what specific
improvements are necessary.

9000 South & Monroe Street

The intersection at 9000 South and Monroe Street is currently signalized and has
dual left-turn lanes northbound with single left-turn lanes on all other approaches.
Monroe Street has a through lane and a right-turn lane both northbound and
southbound. 9000 South has three through lanes in each direction with the third
through lane being a shared through-right westbound and a separate right
eastbound. With the existing configuration this intersection is expected to operate
at LOS F for all future scenarios. The addition of a separate westbound right-turn
lane under the Low Land Use – High Build scenario would result in LOS E. The
High Land Use – High Build scenario would require additional improvements

Draft Sandy Master Transportation Plan   45                                July 2009
including an additional through lane in the each direction on 9000 South, a
separate westbound right-turn lane, and dual left-turn lanes eastbound.

9400 South & Monroe Street

The stop-controlled intersection at 9400 South and Monroe Street currently has a
separate right- and left-turn lanes westbound, a shared through-right lane
northbound and a single through lane and a left-turn lane southbound. As a stop-
controlled intersection it is forecast to operate at LOS F for all future scenarios.
Signalizing this intersection improves it to LOS C for both High Build scenarios.
This intersection should be monitored and signalized when MUTCD warrants are
met.

Mall Ring Road & Centennial Parkway (SB)

The southbound side of the intersection of Mall Ring Road and Centennial
Parkway is currently unsignalized. The northbound and eastbound approaches
have a single lane from which all movements are made. A two-way left-turn lane
exists along Mall Ring Road and is used by the westbound approach as well as a
single through lane. The southbound approach has a through-left lane and a
separate right-turn lane. With the existing configuration, this intersection is
forecast to operate at LOS F for all future scenarios except the Low Land Use –
Low Build scenario. Signalizing this intersection is expected to result in LOS C for
both High Build scenarios. To avoid having to split phase this intersection a
separate left-turn lane would be required northbound and the right-turn lane
southbound would need to be shared by the through movements to allow a
separate left-turn lane. This intersection should be monitored and signalized
when it meets MUTCD warrants.

10600 South & Automall Drive

The signalized intersection at 10600 South and Automall Drive experiences high
traffic volumes on all approaches largely due to its proximity to the 10600
South/I-15 interchange and the high volume of traffic entering and exiting the
mall. All approaches currently have dual left-turn lanes. 10600 South has two
through lanes and a shared through-right lane in each direction. Automall Drive
has a separate right-turn lane and a through lane northbound and a separate
right-turn lane and a shared through-right lane southbound. Even under existing
conditions this intersection operates at LOS E and is expected to operate at LOS
F for all future scenarios.

This intersection would require the most improvements to reach an acceptable
LOS. One of the major issues with this intersection is the high eastbound left-turn
volume onto Mall Ring Road. It is proposed that a fly-over be considered from
eastbound 10600 South onto Mall Ring Road before the 10600 South/Automall
Drive intersection. It was assumed that this flyover could reduce the left-turn
volume at the intersection by approximately 50 percent. Assuming the fly-over is


Draft Sandy Master Transportation Plan   46                                July 2009
constructed the Low Land Use – High Build scenario would require: a separate
right-turn lane on the eastbound approach, an additional westbound through
lane, and shifting the southbound through movements to the outside left-turn
lane, creating separate dual right-turn lanes, a shared through-left and a left-turn
lane on the southbound approach. This reconfiguration would require split
phasing north and southbound and is expected to operate at LOS E. The High
Land Use – High Build scenario would require all the improvements mentioned
plus a southbound right overlap with the eastbound left-turn phase.

The construction of a fly-over would be an expensive alternative with an
estimated cost of 15 million dollars as shown in the TIP. Because of the high cost
associated with this alternative, it is recommended that the other, less expensive
improvements be applied first. This would give a more accurate representation of
what actually is happening at the intersection and further engineering
investigation could be undertaken to determine the cost-benefit of such a major
improvement.

11400 South & State Street

Improvements are expected at the 11400 South/State Street intersection in
conjunction with the 11400 South/I-15 interchange project which is currently
underway; however, further improvements may still be necessary. The
intersection at 11400 South and State Street is currently signalized. By 2030 it is
expected to have dual left-turn and separate right-turn lanes on all approaches.
All approaches are expected to have two through lanes except the eastbound
approach, which is expected to have three. With this projected configuration the
intersection is expected to operate at LOS F for all future scenarios. Adding a
through lane to the westbound and southbound approaches and triple left-turn
lanes to the eastbound approach is expected to improve the LOS to LOS E under
the Low Land Use – High Build scenario and allowing free southbound right-turns
in addition to these improvements is expected to improve the LOS to LOS E
under the High Land Use – High Build scenario.

Base Transit Plan

The base transit service plan was developed based on the WRFC 2030 Plan.
WRFC’   s current plan includes extended TRAX service and a new FrontRunner
station. Additionally, bus service in the plan includes lines on State, 9000 South
and 10600 South, with direct connections to the TRAX Sandy Civic Center
station on 10000 South. No transit service is currently planned to directly serve
the core CBD and Civic Center area on Sego Lily Drive/10000 South.

The travel demand model was run for 2030 with the High Land Use assumptions,
with transit supply and demand included in the analysis. The results indicated an
increased future transit use in the CBD area with a total of 5,000 total daily transit
trips; however, transit shares could be further increased with improved service in
the CBD core area.

Optimized Transit Plan

The optimized transit plan was created to determine the potential increase in
transit share within the CBD area. The additional connection and modes provided
in the optimized transit plan are projected (by the travel demand model) to
increase the total daily transit trips about 10% (to 5,400).

In addition to the transit service included in the base transit plan, the optimized
transit plan included:

6.3    Non-Central Business District
Although most of Sandy’   s growth is expected to occur within the CBD, some
growth is also expected along roadways and at intersections outside the CBD.
Outside the CBD, LOS D is typically acceptable, but LOS E can be acceptable if
delays are minimal. Congestion levels are typically lower outside the CBD and
tend to decrease the farther away a roadway is from the CBD. The following
sections present the recommended improvements to roadways, intersections,
and alternative transportation outside the CBD.

6.3.1 Roadways

The recommended improvements to roadways outside of the CBD are presented
in the Transportation Improvement Program (TIP) in the Capital Facilities Plan
(CFP) located in the appendix. Along with the recommended roadway
improvements are recommendations for changes in roadway classification.
Figure 13 presents the recommended roadway classification, and the paragraphs
that follow briefly describe the proposed changes.

Highland Drive

Highland Drive is a major north-south corridor that currently runs from 9800
South, north and continues into Salt Lake City. Sandy City currently owns right-
of-way to extend this roadway to Sego Lily Drive. The portion of Highland Drive
that extends south of 9800 South to Sego Lily Drive is proposed to be
constructed as a minor collector and we recommend that a trail also be
constructed in this segment. The trail should be added to the City’s current trails
map. The extension of this roadway beyond Sego Lily is expected to relieve
congestion on other north-south corridors in the City; however this extension is
not expected to occur before 2030. If this extension is not completed, other
improvements are expected to be necessary to maintain north-south connectivity
in the City. With the Highland Drive extension, the cost for all roadway and
intersection improvements is expected to be approximately $65 billion versus $58
billion without the Highland Drive extension. The TIP located in the appendix
presents detailed cost estimates with and without this extension.

11400 South

major collector with an 82-foot right-of-way east of 1300 East. It is proposed that
this portion of 11400 South be upgraded to a minor arterial with an 86-foot right-
of-way. Additional right-of-way for a multi-use trail may also be required as
shown in the Parks and Recreation element of the City’  s General Plan.

6.3.2 Traffic Signals and Intersections

Intersection evaluation was focused on the 23 intersections located in the CBD;
however, the City Transportation Engineer expressed specific interest in the
operation of the following intersections:

       •       Creek Road/Highland Drive

       •       9400 South/Highland Drive

       •       11400 South/1700 East

These intersections were analyzed, and the following paragraphs summarize the
improvements recommended at these intersections. Each of these intersections
should be monitored to determine when the specified improvement is needed. All
improvements are expected to be necessary by 2030.

Creek Road and Highland Drive

The Creek Road Highland Drive intersection is signalized and currently operates
at LOS E and is expected to operate at LOS F in 2030 for all scenarios. This
intersection currently has two through lanes, a right and a left-turn lane in both
directions on Highland Drive, and a single through lane and a right and left-turn
lane in both directions on Creek Road. The projected LOS could be improved to
LOS E by adding an additional through lane in each direction on Highland Drive.


Draft Sandy Master Transportation Plan   53                              July 2009
9400 South and Highland Drive

The 9400 South/Highland Drive intersection is signalized and currently operates
at LOS C and is expected to operate at LOS E in 2030 for all scenarios. The
widths of the northbound and southbound legs are wide enough to accommodate
dual left-turn lanes. To decrease the delay at this intersection, it could be
restriped and the permitted left-turn signal heads switched to protected left-turn
signal heads. This is a minor expense and could be completed as needed.

11400 South and 1700 East

The existing distribution of traffic at the 11400 South/1700 East intersection
requires a four-way stop-controlled intersection. By 2030, the traffic distribution is
expected to change enough that the intersection could be a two-way stop-
controlled intersection with the stop signs on 1700 East. As a two-way stop-
controlled intersection, this intersection is expected to operate at LOS C.

There are no changes recommended to the existing network of alternative modes

transit service from the existing TRAX line east to the existing UTA park-and-ride
lot at 9400 South and Highland, and potentially further east up the canyon.

6.4    Potential Funding Sources
The implementation of the recommended Sandy MTP will require the allocation
of funds for the individual projects. Various funding sources are available to
Sandy City. The following paragraphs identify the various transportation funding
sources available to the City and provide descriptions of each.

6.4.1 Federal Funding

Federal funding is available primarily on a competitive basis and is often
segregated into distinct purposes and/or road groups to advance federal
objectives (i.e. the Interstate Highway System). Federal funding has been a
significant component in the advancement of major transportation projects in
Sandy, especially in the last decade. Many of these projects might have been too
large to advance with local or state money alone. Sandy City has been
successful in obtaining funding through Federal earmarks, and the City will
continue to look at the opportunity to obtain this funding.

The National Highway System (NHS) was envisioned as part of the 1991
Intermodal Surface Transportation Efficiency Act (ISTEA), created in 1995 and
amended in the 2005 Safe, Accountable, Flexible, Efficient Transportation Equity
Act: A Legacy for Users (SAFETEA-LU). The NHS is designated by a limited
number of road miles in each urbanized area which serve a national interest. In

Draft Sandy Master Transportation Plan   54                                  July 2009
Sandy City, only I-15, 9000/9400 South, and the existing and proposed sections
of Highland Drive are on the NHS and eligible for NHS funding.

Historically, a large source of federal funding in Sandy City has been the Surface
Transportation Program (STP), created as part of ISTEA. The STP program was
amended by the SAFETEA-LU and includes both urban and non-urban
components as well as state-wide discretionary components. In Sandy City, most
Major Collector streets and all Major and Minor Arterial Streets are eligible for
STP funding. This funding is provided on a competitive basis both within the Salt
Lake Urban Area and within the entire state, based on perceived transportation
needs.

Additional sources of federal funding, created as part of the ISTEA legislation
and continued with the SAFETEA-LU legislation, have been designated to
encourage lower emission transportation and/or less overall single occupancy
vehicle travel. The Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality Improvement Program
(CMAQ) funding was made available for projects and programs in air quality
nonattainment and maintenance areas that encourage less polluting and less
congesting forms of transportation. Transportation Enhancement (TE) funds are
discretionary funds available state wide to enhance the use and/or appearance of
transportation systems. In the past few years, CMAQ funds have funded bicycle
routes, park-and-ride lots, and transit centers, with a significant portion devoted
to traffic signal coordination projects. TE funds have funded bicycle trails, historic
preservation of train stations, roadside landscaping, and other similar projects.

Other sources of federal funding, such as federal demonstration money, are not
expected to play a role in Sandy City’     s long term transportation financial
planning. However, all federal funding sources should be examined and applied
for during budget cycles in order to maximize the City's return on federal
contributions. The point of this analysis is to identify the largest federal
transportation funding programs, not to present an exhaustive list.

6.4.2 State Funding

Historically, state funding has come from the State-wide Gas Tax and has been
restricted to state highways. The state gas tax has increased from 19 cents per
gallon to its present level of 24.5 cents per gallon over the past thirteen years.
Wasatch Front Regional Council (WRFC) and Mountainland Association of
Governments (MAG), the transportation planning agencies for the Wasatch
Front, recognize the increased need for transportation funding and the need to
develop additional sources of funding. They have resolved to support increased
transportation funding by diversifying funding sources to possibly include sales
and property taxes, user fees, and additional local option resources.

In Sandy, state highways currently include I-15, State Street, 700 East, and
9000/9400 South. There is discussion regarding inclusion of Highland Drive and
possibly portions of 10600 South. Many of the major arterial street widening

Draft Sandy Master Transportation Plan   55                                  July 2009
projects presently funded in Sandy are on state highways through either state
sponsored federal projects or state-funded projects. It is likely that State funding
can play a significant role in completing the network of state highways presently
in Sandy City.

6.4.3 Local Funding

Local funding includes the portion of the Statewide Gas Tax distributed to Cities
in the form of Class C road funds, as well as an amount that many cities
contribute from there own general fund revenue, from road impact fees, or other
local sources of funding. Class C road funds are distributed at an existing rate of
25 percent of (net) gas tax revenue (since transfers to other purposes are
diverted from the gross total) based on a ratio of population, road mileage, and
land area. Local general funds and other local contributions to transportation
have been added at an inconsistent rate based on need and community support.

Another potential source for the City to fund the proposed improvements locally
is the implementation of impact fees. Impact fees are contributions to the
transportation system provided by private developers as a condition of
development approval. The general idea behind impact fees is that if no growth
occurs then the existing infrastructure is adequate. Growth occurs as
development occurs and therefore the new development should pay for the
portion of improvements that are required due to the new growth. Impact fees
provide many types of infrastructure and facilities to the community including
roadway facilities. According to state law, impact fees can only be used to fund
growth-related system improvements.

Impact fees could be established to fund the needed improvements. These fees
would be collected by the City, from new developments, to help pay for the
necessary roadway improvements caused by growth.

Development exactions, contributions to the transportation system provided by
private developers as a condition of development approval, have been significant
in Sandy in the past. Since development exactions are not determined based on
need, but rather based on the location of development (or redevelopment), this
contribution to transportation funding has not been anticipated. Development
contributions, while not included in the list of expected Sandy City Transportation
Revenue, can be expected to add significantly to in-fill transportation
improvements and often lessen the cost that other funding sources may have to
contribute. One advantage of extractions over impact fees is that the City does
not have to complete the engineering and procurement for improvements, which
can result in more improvements. As development slows improvements from
both impact fees and extractions would decrease.
