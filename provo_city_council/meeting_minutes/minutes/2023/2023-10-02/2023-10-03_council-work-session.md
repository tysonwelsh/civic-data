3.   A discussion regarding Chapter 17 Code Changes for Hillside Excavation
     Administrative Citations (23-071) (00:47:20)
     Presented by: Gordon Haight, City Engineer

     Mr. Haight talked about the mayor setting up a hillside task force a year ago to address
     the concerns that were arising from developers carving away hillsides to make land
     more developable and leaving scars or other deformities that were not addressed as

     a $25 citation which their group feels won’t really change any behaviors as it’s so low.
     They would like to raise the penalty to $1,000 which they believe will get developer’s
     attention and drive the desired activity change in the future. It is also the maximum
     allowed under state statute. Vice-Chair Whipple asked what the fine is used for. Mr.
     Haight talked about using some of it to restore the area, and that sometimes it does take
     a couple of years, but their group keeps working on it until it is as close to how it

     pull the money from, but many do not. Vice-Chair Whipple asked if they could just fix

4.   A discussion regarding Gateway Standards. PLGPA20230190 (00:56:20)
     Presented by: Jantsen Teuscher, City Planner

     Mr. Teuscher gave a brief history of the topic, from the beginning of the discussion in
     2021 to the present day. The council had asked for a gateway plan and that was what he
     was there to report. The team had two goals in mind: first, to identify gateway priorities
     (Tiers), any themes and current conditions. Second, was to develop and recommend a
     “toolkit” of standards that future gateways could be designed from. Mr. Teuscher went
     on to talk about the 12 existing gateways that are identified in the general plan and how
     they developed the tiers from those. One of the unique things that can be done with
     these tiers is to incorporate some of the cultural and historical impacts of a particular
     area to help the city retain its heritage. Mr. Teuscher went on to display and talk about
     the look of each tier and why they picked the designs they did and what current design
     corridors would fall within them. Vice Chair Whipple asked about who would pay for
     and pick out the design for these. Bill Peperone, Director of Planning Services stated
     that it would be a joint effort between the city and the developers. In areas that already
     exist, the city would design and raise the signs. In new developments, the developers
     would shoulder the cost as well as have flexibility from the “toolkit” to choose a design
     that reflected the areas heritage – as already outlined in the master plan. Councilor
     Ellsworth would like more definition as to the size and scope of a design corridor as
     that term still feels a bit ambiguous. Mr. Teuscher said that there will be some
     flexibility for each one based on the size of the area. Some council members had a
     concern that initially three different areas were brought to them, gateway(s), mountain
     & hillsides, rivers and lakeshore(s) and that the feeling was whether the money was
     being spent and not accounted for due to lack of communication. Mr. Peperone said
     that it was his records that his group had been asked before the council on three
     occasions and they came and provided the updates as requested. All the originally
     approved money is still sitting in the designated area and has not been spent yet. There
     are corridors that are already a part of the ordinance that are being incorporated and
     then all future ones. There are no standards for the existing corridors.

     A motion was made by Councilor Dave Shipley and seconded by Councilor George
     Handley to move the State St. at Provo/Springville gateway to the Tier One category.

     Passed 5-0, with Councilors Fillmore and MacKay excused.

6.   A discussion regarding zoning code (23-042) (02:19:15)
     Presented by: Michael Sanders, Policy Analyst

     Mr. Sanders stated that this was a follow-up presentation from the August meeting
     where the council staff was directed to work with the planner group to bring back a
     more defined plan. Mr. Sanders went on to talk about how the scope of work stayed
     mostly the same, that it was his understanding the council was looking to see how
     much of the work the staff could do versus needing to bring in a contractor. Aaron
     Ardmore, Planning Supervisor, had already completed the first step. He reviewed each
     of the zones and titles and reduced them from 85 zones to 32 zones. In Title 14 there
     originally were 55 sections and he reduced them to 19 sections. Mr. Sanders
     appreciated this because it was ready to be handed off to a consultant. It is the staff’s
     recommendation that the consultant perform the following work:

           A. Move to a table format (includes permitted uses, standards)
Approved 11/14/2023

           B. Code Clean Up (Gaps, Ambiguity, Inconsistencies, Conflicts)
           C. Recommendations for Code Amendments and General Plan Goals (28 zoning
              related general plan goals identified)

     In addition to these three objectives, Mr. Sanders explained that it was his hope that the
     consultant would also work with them to achieve the 28 zoning goals – these are
     related to chapters on community identity land use and housing, economic
     development transportation and organization, and recreation. As it stands currently,
     many of the goals are achievable by the current zoning regulations – however the
     rewrite will empower the City to start to achieve the vision set forth by the General
     Plan. Mr. Sanders said that the planning group received some cost estimates from
     several consultants, and the increased difference really came from how much public
     participation the council wanted to have. It was his recommendation that the council go
     with the second nominated tier, which includes:

                  Development Community
                  City Personnel
                  Public Engagement
                  Planning Commission
                  City Council

     The approximate total cost would be around $29,440. That is for the zoning rewrites
     and condensing. In addition, it is the staff’s recommendation that they also use the
     consultant to update Title 14, which would cost approximately $153,600. Both together
     are a total if $183.040. Mr. Sanders went on to say there were three funds that could be
     used to pay for this: Legacy CIP, General Fund, FY 25 Budget. Vice Chair Whipple
     asked if this would only be for the ITOD zone or are there others too? Bill Peperone,
     Director of Planning Services said that since this concept is new, they thought they
     would test it on the ITOD zone first to see how it goes and if it works for Provo.

     A motion was made by Councilor George Hadley and seconded by Vice-Chair
     Rachel Whipple to request Council and Development Services staff to proceed with a

                  Form based code in the ITOD zone

7.   A discussion regarding "the dog problem" (23-070) (02:43:15)
     Presented by: Abi Maccabee, Council Intern
     Ms. Maccabee presented a follow-up discussion as asked by the council, to describe
     what constitutes a service animal, what staffs’ recommendation would be about non-
Approved 11/14/2023

     picked up dog waste, and off leash concerns and penalties. Currently, in the city code it
     says that a service animal is what is defined in 28 C.F.R. 35.104 as amended. The
     federal law states that “service animal means any dog that is individually trained to do
     work or perform tasks for the benefit of an individual with a disability, including a
     physical, sensory, psychiatric, intellectual, or other mental disability. Other species of
     animals, whether wild or domestic, trained, or untrained are not service animals for the
     purposes of this definition.” The next item the staff were asked to investigate and bring
     back information about penalties that may currently be in the city code or what staff
     thinks is appropriate if penalties were to be initiated. The current code does state that
     the Justice Court can deny a pet owner the right to own a pet if they have too many
     violations within a 12-month period. Animal control officers do have the authority to
     issue a citation at any time and/or impound the animal if the officer feels it’s warranted.
     Ms. Maccabee presented a slide with an example of several cities’ penalty amounts
     from Salt Lake City and around the country. Many seemed to carry “tiered or
     weighted” amounts as the number of violations increased. It was introduced to the
     council that for off-leash penalties, it was recommended for the council to explore a
     three-tiered system of “Soft, Medium, and Hard.” The three tiers started at $25, $75,
     and $300 respectively and went up from there, with the fifth violation on each tier
     included seizure of the animal. Councilor Hoban asked if there are any criminal
     penalties currently, and was told that in the code, “an animal is considered vicious if it
     acts aggressively towards or bites another animal or person – which could then incur a
     criminal charge.” For dog waste penalties, a similar tiered system was recommended of
     “Soft, Medium, and Hard” with the amounts of $50, $100, and $250 respectively and
     the amounts doubling during each subsequent citation with the fifth violation also
     being seizure of the animal.

     A motion was made by Councilor George Hadley and seconded by Travis Hoban to
     direct Council staff to prepare an ordinance to adopt the “soft penalties” for both
     waste removal and off-leash violations as recommended by Council staff.

     Passed 4-0 with Councilors Ellsworth, Fillmore, and MacKay excused.
