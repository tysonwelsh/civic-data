### D. LAND USE ORDINANCE AMENDMENT

**1. Timpanogos Special Service District** — Proposed Utah County Land Use Ordinance text amendment to Section 12.56, and any other applicable section, to include bio-solids handling operations owned and operated by a governmental entity as a permitted use in the Public Facilities (PF) Zone (continued from December 17, 2024 meeting)

**Motion: Chris Herrod — Second: Karen Ellingson**

Motion to move discussion about items E1 with D1. The motion passed with the following vote: "Aye" Glen Roberts, Chris Herrod, Shayne Pierce, Lorraine Davis, Robert McMullin, Karen Ellingson, Sullivan Love. "Nay" none.

Sullivan Love stated a potential conflict of interest as a Timpanogos Special Service District (TSSD) board member and decided to leave the meeting during the discussion of the agenda items.

Greg Robinson informed the commission that TSSD requested a postponement of their agenda item to the February meeting to allow time for review and revisions. He noted that notices had already been sent out for the current meeting, meaning some members of the public may have attended expecting to discuss the issue. He clarified that while staff had made changes requested by the commission, these changes had not yet been shared with TSSD representatives. He offered to present the changes for discussion.

Karen Ellingson suggested reviewing the changes before taking public comments so that both staff and TSSD representatives could incorporate feedback before the next meeting. The commission agreed.

Greg Robinson presented the proposed amendments, which focused on the classification and handling of biosolids. The changes included specifying that only Class A biosolids would be permitted at the facility. Handling references were removed since prior approvals of a sewage treatment facility already covered that aspect. The modifications were highlighted in red, with additions underlined and deletions struck out.

Shayne Pierce requested clarification on the definition of Class A biosolids.

Greg Robinson explained that federal guidelines were referenced to ensure compliance rather than creating a new definition.

**Motion: Karen Ellingson — Second: Glen Roberts**

Motion to open the public hearing. The motion passed with the following vote: "Aye" Glen Roberts, Chris Herrod, Shayne Pierce, Lorraine Davis, Robert McMullin, Karen Ellingson. "Nay" none.

Bryce Armstrong reminded the commission that public comments were limited to three minutes per speaker but could be adjusted if necessary. The commission chose to maintain the three-minute rule.

Steve Bateman expressed concerns about the proposed facility's proximity to his property, emphasizing that he had not received notification about the December meeting. He explained that his dairy farm, which produces a significant portion of Utah's milk and beef supply, could be negatively impacted by potential contamination from biosolids. He cited specific risks associated with heavy metals and phosphorus in biosolids, stating that contamination could result in severe financial losses and threaten the food supply. He detailed the impact of wind patterns in the area, which could spread pollutants onto his land. He requested additional time to present his case, stressing that the commission should take a closer look at the risks before approving the facility.

Shayne Pierce acknowledged Bateman's concerns but clarified that the discussion was currently about the text amendment that would allow biosolids in the public facility zone rather than the specific site approval. He suggested that contamination concerns might be better addressed through zoning requirements rather than the text amendment.

Chris Herrod asked for clarification on the origin of contaminants in biosolids.

Steve Bateman explained that human waste, along with various chemicals from household and industrial sources, could lead to contamination. He cited cases in other states where biosolids had caused severe environmental and health issues, leading to the shutdown of dairy farms.

Chris Herrod then asked whether state agencies, such as the Department of Environmental Quality (DEQ), were responsible for overseeing such environmental risks.

Bryce Armstrong and Greg Robinson explained that while state and federal guidelines must be followed, local zoning decisions were separate from those regulatory requirements. They stated that TSSD representatives, including an engineer, might be able to provide more details about compliance with environmental standards.

Shayne Pierce reaffirmed that state and federal regulations were already included in the zoning text, and the primary change being considered was the requirement to permit only Class A biosolids.

Steve Bateman reiterated his concerns, stating that his business and the food supply chain could be at risk if contamination occurred. He urged the commission to reconsider the location and conduct further review to prevent potential harm to the environment and agriculture in the area.

Lorraine Davis requested access to the research that Steve Bateman referenced regarding biosolids contamination.

Steve Bateman agreed to provide the documents, explaining that the information came from their soil nutritionist, who had recently reviewed test samples from a biosolids facility in South Jordan. He reiterated concerns about the long-term risks of biosolids, particularly the potential impact on their farm and the broader Utah food supply.

Shayne Pierce explained that any submitted documents would need to be officially accepted as part of the public record by the planning commission.

Braden Sheppard, representing Farmland Reserve, clarified that his comments would focus solely on the text amendment. He shared that their dairy farm had been applying biosolids to about 200 acres annually since 2008 but had decided to phase out the practice due to rising levels of heavy metals and microplastics in soil samples. While they had not detected PFOS in measurable amounts, they had observed concerning trends with other contaminants. He suggested adding language to the amendment that would require containment measures to prevent biosolids from leaving the designated site. He noted that federal regulations already mandate containment for some storage sites and emphasized the importance of preventing windborne pollutants.

Karen Ellingson asked whether there were any available biosolid grades that could filter out contaminants.

Braden Sheppard responded that Class A biosolids only remove bacteria and parasites but do not eliminate metals, plastics, or pharmaceuticals. As a result, they had chosen to discontinue the use of biosolids.

Jadeen Steadman, representing Steadman Brothers, voiced concerns about respiratory risks posed by biosolid dust, particularly for nearby homes. She also raised concerns about increased truck traffic near a school bus stop and the negative impact on property values. She worried that the presence of a biosolids facility would discourage future development in the area, limiting opportunities for her family and others.

Shayne Pierce redirected the discussion to focus on the text amendment rather than the zoning map change.

Kyle Steadman questioned whether commission members had personally visited the site to assess its proximity to nearby homes.

Steve Bateman added that in addition to farm operations, the region was also used for cattle and sheep grazing. He reiterated his concern that the facility's location could significantly impact agriculture and suggested finding an alternative site in a less populated area.

Wade Stinson, an engineer working with TSSD, provided clarification on federal containment regulations for Class A biosolids. He explained that the biosolids must be stored on an impermeable surface to prevent contamination of the ground and water. While covering storage areas is not required by regulations, it could be done if necessary. He also reassured the commission that Class A biosolids are solid, not powdery, and are unlikely to be carried far by wind.

Karen Ellingson asked whether federal regulations required biosolids to be covered.

Wade Stinson confirmed that covering was not a requirement but could be implemented if necessary. He compared the consistency of Class A biosolids to a "Cheeto," stating that the material was solid and less likely to become airborne.

Robert McMullin asked if Class A biosolids could contain heavy metals.

Wade Stinson confirmed that while Class A treatment removes bacteria and parasites, it does not eliminate heavy metals or microplastics.

Lorraine Davis then referenced Braden Sheppard's earlier statement about phasing out biosolids use and asked how TSSD would address concerns that biosolids may become an unsustainable practice in the future.

Wade Stinson acknowledged that Sheppard's organization had decided to discontinue biosolids use, but he did not provide a direct response on how TSSD would address the broader concerns. He explained that the collected data on nutrients and heavy metals in the biosolids showed levels well below government-mandated thresholds, including for PFOS. He emphasized that overapplication of any material, including fertilizers or water, could be detrimental to soil and crops. He also pointed out that Class A biosolids were the safest form and had been approved for use in public spaces such as parks and golf courses when applied at proper rates.

Chris Herrod asked whether the county was required by law to provide a designated location for biosolids disposal, similar to zoning requirements for other controversial uses.

Bryce Armstrong responded that while the situation differed from free speech-related zoning laws, the county did permit similar uses, such as sewage treatment plants. He clarified that there was no absolute mandate for a biosolids storage site but acknowledged that such facilities needed to be located somewhere, particularly in remote unincorporated areas.

Eric Steadman, who works for the Utah Labor Commission but was not representing the Utah Labor Commission with his comments, raised concerns about potential occupational hazards for farm employees due to airborne contaminants such as arsenic. He noted that exposure to such substances could result in additional compliance costs for businesses, including monitoring and protective measures required by Utah OSHA. He explained that Utah OSHA enforces federal occupational safety standards and that Steve Bateman's farm, with its 168 employees, might be affected by new safety regulations if contamination risks increased.

Shayne Pierce questioned whether existing language in the text amendment, which requires compliance with state and federal standards, would already cover OSHA-related concerns.

Eric Steadman clarified that workplace safety standards fall under 29 CFR regulations, separate from environmental standards. He suggested that without further sampling under worst-case scenarios, the potential impacts on worker safety remained unclear.

**Motion: Chris Herrod — Second: Robert McMullin**

Motion to continue to February 18th meeting. The motion passed with the following vote: "Aye" Glen Roberts, Chris Herrod, Shayne Pierce, Lorraine Davis, Robert McMullin, Karen Ellingson. "Nay" none.

Lorraine Davis inquired about the possibility of visiting the proposed site before the next meeting.

Bryce Armstrong explained that there were two options: an official site visit could be organized through the planning commission, requiring public notice, or individual members could visit the site independently, provided no more than two members were present at the same time to avoid violating open meeting regulations.

Lorraine Davis further asked whether commission members could meet with affected residents to better understand their concerns.

Bryce Armstrong advised against private discussions, emphasizing that such discussions should take place in a public setting to ensure transparency.

### E. GENERAL PLAN/ZONE MAP AMENDMENT

**1. Timpanogos Special Service District** — Proposed amendment of the Utah County General Plan Land Use Element Map from Agricultural to Manufacturing, and amendment of the Official Utah County Zone Map from the Residential Agricultural (RA-5) and Mining and Grazing (M&G-1) Zones to the Public Facilities (PF) Zone for property located in Sections 5 and 8, T9S R1W, approximately 48 acres, Chimney Rock Pass Road area of unincorporated Utah County (continued from December 17, 2024 meeting)

Steve Bateman used the map to illustrate his concerns about the proposed location, highlighting that two residences were directly adjacent to the site and that his farmland extended for several miles beyond. He compared the facility's potential impact to the existing landfill, stating that despite containment efforts, the wind still carried debris onto his property. He emphasized that dried biosolids could similarly become airborne and affect his farm, workers, and residents. He also stressed the importance of monitoring wells, similar to those installed at the landfill, to detect potential contamination of the aquifer, given the significant investment in local water resources. He warned that the area could eventually be needed for residential development, making the presence of a biosolids facility problematic for future growth.

Braden Sheppard, representing Farmland Reserve, stated that his organization remained neutral on the rezoning proposal but had concerns about containment and long-term land use. He emphasized that biosolids storage presented unknown risks, particularly regarding water contamination. He pointed out that farmland in the area was valued at approximately $35,000 per acre-foot of water, making water protection a significant priority. He also noted that long-term planning efforts had envisioned growth in the region over the next 20-30 years, with Saratoga Springs expanding southward. He cautioned that the facility's presence could conflict with future development efforts.

Jadeen Steadman presented concerns about biosolids transport, citing past spillage incidents in American Fork. She provided photographic evidence of three separate spills from the previous summer, arguing that such accidents posed biohazard risks during transport.

Jared Nelson questioned the cumulative burden placed on local landowners, citing the prior approvals of a landfill and power lines in the area. He argued that the proposed biosolids facility would further devalue their land and negatively impact residents. He pointed to the experience in Lindon, where a sewage plant had led to complaints and declining property values. He asked how local families would be compensated for the loss of land value if the facility were approved.

Robert McMullin acknowledged the concerns raised and assured the public that the commission had sought additional information specifically to allow for a more informed discussion. He thanked the community members for their input and emphasized that the commission was carefully considering the issue.

Shayne Pierce noted that the commission was tasked with determining both the appropriateness of the text amendment and whether this specific location was suitable. He acknowledged concerns about the demand for biosolids as a fertilizer and reiterated that the commission needed to ensure any ordinance was safe while evaluating if this site was appropriate.

Lorraine Davis echoed Robert McMullin's sentiment, stating that the commission was committed to thoroughly examining all aspects of the proposal. She encouraged the public to continue providing research and data, emphasizing the importance of hearing different perspectives to make an informed decision.

**Motion: Robert McMullin — Second: Chris Herrod**

Motion to **continue** until February 18, 2025. The motion passed with the following vote: "Aye" Glen Roberts, Chris Herrod, Shayne Pierce, Lorraine Davis, Robert McMullin, Karen Ellingson. "Nay" none.

**2. Robert and Leslie Hight** — Proposed Utah County Land Use Ordinance text amendment to Chapter 4.44 (Frontage on an Approved Public Street Required, Exceptions), and any other applicable sections, to amend requirements for lots or parcels separated from official frontage by railroad right-of-way

Lorraine Davis indicated a potential conflict of interest and stepped down from the dias and did not participate in this item.

Bryce Armstrong introduced the proposal, explaining that it was a text amendment that, if approved, would apply countywide under certain criteria. He clarified that while the proposal was prompted by a specific case, the discussion should focus on the general policy rather than individual property.

Greg Robinson provided an overview of the amendment, explaining that it aimed to modify an existing exception that allows properties separated from a road by railroad right-of-way to establish access through an easement. The current requirement specifies a permanent, perpetual easement, but the Hights had been unable to obtain such an agreement from the railroad. Instead, they secured a private road crossing agreement, which was issued to the individual property owner but did not automatically transfer to future owners. The proposed amendment would allow private road-crossing agreements as an alternative to perpetual easements. Staff expressed concerns that the lack of a permanent easement could lead to complications if a new owner was unable to secure a similar agreement.

Glen Roberts, who had experience working with railroads, acknowledged that obtaining a permanent easement was unlikely. He supported modifying the language but raised concerns about how the agreement would transfer to new owners. He suggested revising the ordinance language to reflect that the crossing agreement must be reinstated or reissued upon property transfer. He also questioned the use of the term "official form" for agreements and recommended changing it to "the form utilized" by the railroad to avoid ambiguity.

Karen Ellingson asked whether there was a requirement to notify potential buyers that the property's access rights were contingent on maintaining the railroad agreement.

Greg Robinson confirmed that a restrictive covenant would be recorded on the property's title, making the condition visible in title searches.

Chris Herrod sought clarification on whether properties affected by the ordinance had any alternative access routes.

Bryce Armstrong explained that while the amendment was not specific to one property, it generally applied to cases where no other direct access was available. He emphasized that the ordinance primarily addressed access for occupied structures requiring emergency response services.

The commission discussed the potential for the railroad to modify or relocate crossings, which could impact future property access.

Bryce Armstrong noted that this was a common concern and underscored the importance of ensuring property owners understood the potential limitations.

Chris Herrod summarized that the amendment would allow private crossing agreements to serve as valid access for occupancy permits, with the understanding that future owners would need to obtain a new agreement if the property was sold.

Bryce Armstrong added that while the county might not always be aware of property sales, lending institutions, and building permit applications would likely reveal whether a new agreement was necessary.

Glen Roberts raised concerns about balancing property owners' rights with public safety and emergency access.

Bryce Armstrong reiterated that the goal was to ensure reliable access while accommodating cases where direct public road access was not possible.

The commission acknowledged the challenges involved, particularly the restrictive nature of railroad agreements.

Greg Robinson confirmed that the ordinance would still require property owners to maintain access road standards to retain their occupancy permits.

Glen Roberts suggested refining the language in sections 4.44.A.8.D and H to clarify that both perpetual easements and private road crossing agreements should be recognized. He pointed out that some sections of the ordinance referenced easements without including private road crossing agreements, which needed to be corrected for consistency.

Bryce Armstrong and Greg Robinson acknowledged the need for adjustments, explaining that the ordinance originally included "perpetual easement" language to keep options open in case such an easement became attainable.

The commission agreed to update the ordinance to ensure that both easements and private road agreements were properly accounted for.

Robert Hight addressed the commission, explaining that he and his wife had purchased land in Spanish Fork Canyon with the intention of building a single-family residence. He confirmed that the railroad had refused to grant a perpetual easement, instead offering a private road crossing agreement, which they had secured. He noted that the previous property owner had a similar agreement, but since it was not transferable, they had to apply for a new one. He expressed hope that the ordinance could be updated to reflect what was realistically attainable from the railroad.

Shayne Pierce asked Robert Hight about the terms of the agreement, specifically whether it allowed for emergency vehicle access.

Robert Hight confirmed that the agreement permitted access, but property owners were responsible for ensuring that only authorized individuals used the crossing. He noted that around 70 homes used the same crossing, but not all had individual agreements.

Karen Ellingson sought clarification on whether each of those 70 homes had separate agreements.

Robert Hight confirmed that none had perpetual easements, but he was unsure how many had private agreements.

Glen Roberts voiced concerns about the long-term viability of private road crossing agreements, particularly since the railroad could terminate them upon property transfer. He suggested that many homes in the area may have changed ownership without securing new agreements, creating a potential legal and access issue.

Bryce Armstrong explained that since the subdivision had been approved decades ago, the county had no active role in enforcing or monitoring previously approved access rights.

Robert McMullin asked how Hight discovered the requirement for an access agreement.

Robert Hight explained that they had done extensive due diligence before purchasing the property, working with Greg Robinson to ensure compliance with county regulations. They had initially attempted to secure a perpetual easement, but after the railroad refused, they pursued a private road-crossing agreement.

Bryce Armstrong displayed a map of the area, showing how the railroad interrupted direct access from Highway 6.

The commission noted that the existing subdivision had been developed in the 1970s, and while its access was legally recognized at the time, the details of how it was originally approved were unclear.

Sullivan Love raised concerns about property owners unknowingly losing their access rights if they purchased a home without securing a new agreement.

Robert Hight reassured the commission that while the process was difficult, the railroad had a standard procedure for granting agreements.

Greg Robinson and Glen Roberts discussed refining the ordinance language further, particularly ensuring that it did not exclude other valid forms of railroad access agreements, such as licenses.

Glen Roberts warned that the railroad might change its terminology in the future, and the ordinance should remain flexible enough to accommodate different agreement formats.

Jill Taylor, a former planning commissioner, provided additional historical context. She confirmed that there had been a historical road predating the railroad, which allowed residents to maintain access despite railroad threats. She also inquired when the current frontage requirement ordinance had been enacted.

Bryce Armstrong explained that the 4.44 frontage requirement had been in place for about 40 years, and the various exceptions had been added over time, likely within the last 10 to 15 years.

Jill Taylor inquired whether the railroad ever grants permanent, perpetual easements or if private road crossing agreements were the standard practice.

Bryce Armstrong confirmed that staff had learned through this process that perpetual easements were not granted by railroads. The existing ordinance language was originally designed for properties separated by state or federal land, and when railroad crossings were added as an exception, staff had not realized how difficult obtaining a permanent easement would be.

Jill Taylor acknowledged the need for an amendment specific to railroad access and wished the applicants luck. She then raised another concern about the applicants' use of a bridge owned and maintained by the Covered Bridge community. She suggested that, in addition to securing a railroad crossing agreement, the applicants should have an arrangement to contribute to bridge maintenance costs.

Sullivan Love assumed that an HOA agreement might already exist for such matters.

Robert Hight confirmed that he had been invited to attend the next HOA meeting to discuss potential contributions.

Glen Roberts noted that a recorded declaration might already impose obligations on each new property owner for bridge maintenance.

Bryce Armstrong clarified that a separate section of the ordinance addresses off-site access and requires necessary approvals, such as easements, between the relevant parties.

Robert Hight reassured the commission that they were committed to paying their fair share for bridge maintenance and had already spoken with the HOA president. He also confirmed that an easement through Covered Bridge had been included with the purchase of their property.

Glen Roberts shifted the discussion to broader concerns, particularly how federal land agencies like the BLM issue access licenses that can be revoked at will.

Greg Robinson explained that while perpetual easements were preferred and had been obtained in some cases, this particular exception had rarely been used. If similar access issues arose with government land in the future, they could be addressed at that time.

Glen Roberts suggested finalizing the document immediately to prevent the applicants from having to return for another round of approvals.

Greg Robinson noted that while he did not have access to edit the document in real-time, they could verbally list the amendments and ensure they were included in the final written recommendation.

Bryce Armstrong agreed, stating that the written recommendation would be forwarded to the commission and provided to the applicants.

**Motion: Glen Roberts — Second: Sullivan Love**

Motion to **approve** the amendment, including the staff findings and the following modifications:
- In Section 4.44.A.8.H — In the fourth line from the bottom, where it references the "permanent perpetual easement":
  - Add the phrase "or private road crossing agreement" after "permanent, perpetual easement."
  - Delete the phrase "is reinstated" and replace it with "or a new private road crossing agreement is approved by the zoning administrator or its equivalent."
  - Delete the word "official" so that the phrase reads: "In the form utilized by the governmental entity or railroad."
- In Section 4.44.A.8.D — Modify the language to state: "The easement, private road crossing agreement, or its equivalent shall be approved by the zoning administrator."

The motion passed with the following vote: "Aye" Glen Roberts, Chris Herrod, Shayne Pierce, Sullivan Love, Robert McMullin, Karen Ellingson. "Nay" none.

**4. Utah County Planning Commission** — Proposed Utah County Land Use Ordinance text amendment to Sections 2.08, 6.16, and any other applicable section, related to assigning administration of special flood hazard areas in the unincorporated areas to the Engineering Division of the Utah County Public Works Department

Kevin Stinson explained that FEMA requires all land disturbances and structures in a designated floodplain to be reviewed and approved by a floodplain administrator. Historically, this process has been handled by the community development department. However, after meeting with FEMA representatives and the Utah County Public Works Department, staff determined that the technical nature of floodplain reviews made it more appropriate for the engineering and public works department to oversee. He noted that this change aligns with practices in other jurisdictions and has the support of public works. The proposed amendment included defining the role of the floodplain administrator, modifying the zoning administrator definition, and updating references in the ordinance to reflect the new responsibility.

Karen Ellingson asked for clarification on who would serve as the floodplain administrator.

Kevin Stinson confirmed that it would be the Utah County Engineer or their designee.

Glen Roberts summarized that the commission was essentially transferring the authority for floodplain review to a department better equipped to handle it.

Karen Ellingson also inquired if any state statute required additional review beyond what was being proposed.

Kevin Stinson confirmed that there was no such requirement, making the transition straightforward.

Shayne Pierce opened the floor for public comment, but no members of the public chose to speak.

**Motion: Glen Roberts — Second: Karen Ellingson**

Motion to **approve** the amendment. The motion passed with the following vote: "Aye" Glen Roberts, Chris Herrod, Shayne Pierce, Lorraine Davis, Robert McMullin, Karen Ellingson, Sullivan Love. "Nay" none.
