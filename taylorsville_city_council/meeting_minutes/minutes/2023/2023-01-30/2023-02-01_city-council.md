       3.3       Speed Bump Discussion – Ben White

        In response to a January 18 Council request, City Engineer Ben White addressed previous
suggestions to install additional speed bumps in various problematic areas of the city to improve
traffic safety. He explained that the goals of street systems were to move vehicles and pedestrians
safely and efficiently. The speed limit in residential areas was typically 25 mph. In commercial
areas it could be 30 or 40 mph and of course it was higher on highways. The general rule of thumb
for speed counts was that if 85% of the cars are going 5 mph over the limit or less, that was
acceptable. It was important to note that it wasn’t possible to eliminate all speeding.

Mr. White explained that there were three categories of traffic-calming measures—passive, non-
structural and structural. Passive measures typically involved public education such as newsletters,
social media posts, etc. or enforcement of speed limits and perhaps the posting of temporary mobile
speed limit signs.

Non-Structural speed calming measures included painting asphalt with either white lines or middle
yellow lines to remind drivers to slow down. He explained that speed signs were somewhat
effective, but over time they tended to lose their effectiveness. Stop signs were not a device that
could be used to reduce speed (he couldn’t simply erect stop signs everywhere there was a speeding
problem, even if they were effective in reducing speeding. Placing objects along the sides of the
road (i.e., trees, cars parked on side of road, etc.) caused reductions of speed as well.

Structural measures included islands in the middle of roads, bump-outs on the sides of the road,
crosswalks, and dividers in the middle of the road. All are used to help reduce speed. Narrowing
the road at intersections were options, as were speed bumps.

Mr. White presented a map showing the location of all the 2022 speed complaints he had received
in 2022. Most were on what he called neighborhood collector roads. He noted that based on data
he had reviewed, there were not a lot of speeding problems in the city. Most were about 5 mph
above the speed limit, which landed in the “acceptable” range.

     Still, he agreed there were some problem areas and he had identified four specific locations,
one of which was Atherton Drive. Chief Cottam mentioned that he and Mr. White worked together
regularly to address speeding problems in the city, and he had learned quite a bit from him
regarding the engineering aspect of speed/traffic calming. He said it had been very helpful to the
police department.

Councilmember Cochran asked specifically about speed bumps. Mr. White responded that he was
generally opposed to them. There was a cost to installing and maintaining them and those that live
close to speed bumps were typically averse to them.           They could cause damage to vehicles
driven by those who were unaware of them or did not take proper care while driving over them
and made plowing snow challenging as well. A single speed bump would do nothing by itself. He
would typically recommend 3-5 in an area, depending on the circumstances. Often, their existence
caused drivers to take alternate routes, thereby increasing traffic in areas designed for less traffic.
In short, he felt speed bumps were only effective in certain circumstances.

Councilmember Burgess asked specifically about Atherton Drive. Mr. White conceded there was
a speeding problem there, but he did not believe speed bumps would be effective. Still, he was
looking at other options. He pointed out that most of the accidents in the area were caused by
driver error in one form or another-- speeding, being distracted, and bad weather, as opposed to a
problem with the way the road was designed. It was difficult to design around driver error.
Competent drivers could drive through the area at 35-45 mph with no problem.

Councilmember Harker asked what had already been done to address the problem on Atherton.
Mr. White responded that electronic chevron arrow signs had been used, although he did not
believe they had brought a noticeable decrease in problems. Mr. Taylor reminded the Council that
there were between 15,000-20,000 cars making the Atherton Drive turn successfully each day.
Most of the accidents were caused by impaired drivers, and impaired drivers would have accidents
regardless of where they chose to drive their vehicles.

Councilmember Knudsen asked what would happen if a neighborhood decided collectively that it
wanted to have speed bumps installed, although Mr. White might not recommend them in an area.
Mr. White responded that a majority of the city council could make that decision and pointed out
that each speed bump cost roughly $5,000 - $10,000 including the associated signage.
Councilmember Knudsen appreciated that Mr. White was willing to have the discussion.

Councilmember Harker asked if there was money in the budget to even attempt to address the four

commission twice and staff felt it was now ready for Council consideration.

In response to a question from Councilmember Cochran, Mr. Spung defined “home occupation.”
He explained that currently, any home-occupied business was required to obtain a conditional use

resources. The proposed ordinance would bring city code in line with current practice.
Additionally, recent legislation at the state level required some fees to be updated. Staff
recommended that home occupations be considered a permitted use subject to certain standards,
depending on the type of business.

        7.1      Ordinance No. 23-01 – An Ordinance of the City of Taylorsville amending
                 Taylorsville Municipal Code Chapter 5.06: Business License Fees, and Chapter
                 5.76: Rental Dwelling Unit to remove Good Landlord Program Requirements – Jay
                 Ziolkowski

      Jay Ziolkowski gave a brief history as to how this ordinance came about. He was asked to
take on this project as well as business licensing and code enforcement. The Good Landlord
Program was created by the city in 2010 and at the time, it was a good program. There were
currently 807 active rental licenses in Taylorsville, but there was no way to know how many homes
were being rented without a rental license in place. Historically there had been as many as 2,100
licenses in place at any given time.

Mr. Ziolkowski explained that diminished training opportunities and compliance had both become
issues with the program. It was discovered that about 30% of landlords in the program did not
meet the program criteria. As his research continued, he learned that many other cities throughout
the valley were discontinuing their Good Landlord programs for several reasons, the most
significant one being that it was the administration’s belief that all landlords should be good—not
just those who participated in a formal program. That is, all landlords in the city should meet
specific criteria.

       Mr. Ziolkowski provided clarification on the fee schedule. In connection to other fee
schedules, recommendations would be made in the coming months of possibly having a tiered or
escalation system for repeat offenders.

Councilmember Burgess expressed concern for renters if there was no Good Landlord program in
place. Mr. Ziolkowski responded that there needed to be some kind of methodology or educational
enhancement programs, fee waivers, incentives, for landlords who would then come in to receive
