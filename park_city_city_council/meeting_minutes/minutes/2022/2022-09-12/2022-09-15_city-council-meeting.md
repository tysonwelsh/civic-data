Ice Arena and Interlocal Agreement Update:

Discuss Soil Remediation Options at the Gordo Property:
Matt Dias, City Manager, stated soils had been an issue in the community and Ryan
Blair, Environmental Regulatory Program Manager, was a new employee who took on a
lot of work. Blair introduced Jalynn Knudsen, Brad Maulding, and Jay Lyman,
Department of Waste Management and Radiation Control (DWMRC). Blair reviewed the
history of the Gordo property as a soil repository. He displayed some possible solutions:

Option One was to haul the soil away. The Utah Department of Environmental Quality
(DEQ) recognized the soil in Park City as Beville exempt, which would allow the City to
use the soil differently on the City's end. For example, landfills could use the soil. He
displayed a table showing the cost to haul the soil away and it ranged from $3.9 million
to $4.5 million. No permit was needed to haul it away and it would take four to six
months.

Option Two was keeping the soil onsite. This would require a permit. A liner would be
installed and environmental controls would be placed on the site. This could take four
months to complete.

Option Three was redevelopment of the site. There would be a voluntary cleanup
program and an environmental cleanup program. Some soil would be capped and some
removed, and projects could be built on the site. This was the recommended option.

Council Member Doilney asked why Richardson Flat could not be reopened. Maulding
stated the conversations with the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) were
ongoing, so it wasn't a hard no. Knudsen noted there would not be an answer anytime
soon. Council Member Doilney asked if Park City was involved in the conversation and
if there was anything that could be done to move the conversation forward. Knudsen
stated Park City had been engaged in the discussions. The best thing would be to reach
out to the contact at the EPA. Dias reaffirmed Park City was engaged in the
conversation and currently the repository was closed for outside tipping. It was private
property and the property was involved in litigation so there was no near-term solution
for that site. Fortunately, Park City had this temporary repository that met the regulator’s
needs.

Council Member Gerber referred to the redevelopment option and indicated the soils
would be capped in place. She asked if a liner and site monitoring were required with
that option. Maulding indicated inspections would be made periodically. Council
Member Gerber asked what the difference was between capping the soil and
redevelopment versus partial haul redevelopment. Blair stated if there was a permit to
close the facility, then it would only be a closed repository and redevelopment would not
be possible. Maulding indicated his group would work to know what the tailings were,
what soil would need to be removed, and what could be left behind. If soil was left
behind, there would be limitations on what development could go on top of it.

1. Consideration to Approve Park City Forward, the Long-Range Transportation
Plan (LRTP):
Julia Collins and Alex Roy, Transportation Planning Department, presented this item.
Roy reviewed the changes made to the plan in the last several months. He stated the
LRTP would look at all modes of transportation and would guide projects and policies
for the next 25 years. The plan provided a way to frame discussions on transportation
priorities moving forward. Collins indicated the plan was required for state and federal
grants. It would propose policies, but was not a policy. There would be multiple
opportunities to review projects as they were proposed, developed and approved, and
implemented.

Roy stated this plan took a couple years to finalize and overwhelming public input was
received. He reviewed transportation modes discussed in the process. He stated
guiding principles were identified. The goal was to develop a Park Once community.
The plan goals were to collaborate with regional partners, manage peak traffic
conditions, expand biking and walking infrastructure, develop and improve the transit
system, and analyze disruptive transportation ideas.

Roy reviewed the top regional and local projects. Within the plan there were proposed
projects for all forms of transportation. Collins stated staff took the proposed projects
and divided them into phases. Phase One would be projects that could be finished in
five years. She also indicated they were working with partners for regional projects. She
noted the plan would help with the Olympic infrastructure discussions, identify funding
gaps, and pursue regional solutions. Roy recommended the Park City Forward LRTP be
adopted as a supplement to the existing Transportation element of the General Plan.

Council Member Rubell asked if the plan was developed with regional partners. Roy
stated members of UDOT and High Valley Transit were on the technical advisory
committee. This plan was geographically bound so they allowed City staff to explore
different ideas on projects solely within City limits. Collins stated there were
conversations with community partners as well. Council Member Rubell asked what the
scoring rubric was to come up with the top priorities list and where safety played into
that. Roy indicated the rubric looked at The Access, Include, Transform, and Sustain
goals. Safety measures were included in the Access and Transform pieces to make

John Robertson, City Engineer, Alex Roy, Senior Transportation Planner, and Wade
Carpenter, Police Chief, presented this item. Robertson stated speed limits had
historically been controlled by the state legislature. Now there was an opportunity to
look at setting speed limits based on what was best for the community. He indicated
roads were set at general speeds unless studies were done that determined the speed
limit should change. Roads needed to be signed in order to have enforcement. Many
times there were requests to lower speed limits and there used to be a process before a
city could change the limit. In the last legislative session, a law was passed that
municipalities could establish speed limits on municipal roads without completing the
standardized process.

Robertson presented some options for consideration: Option One would maintain
current speed limits and he noted there would be consistency in enforcement. He stated
if 85% of cars drove the speed limit, then it was determined the speed limit was
accurately set. Option Two would set speed limits based on the width and curvatures on
the road, as well as by the number of driveways. He noted it would take a long time to
look at each individual street and there would be a need for extensive sign replacement.
Option Three would designate the speed based on classification. This option would be
an easy determination, but not all the local streets were the same. Some were narrow or
curvy. If the City selected this option, certain roads could have lower speed limits than
others.

Regulating Speed Limits within Park City
                              Speed Limits
                                         Background
PCMC Municipal Code adopted Utah Code 41-6a as the City’s Uniform Traffic Code
 Establishes lawful general speeds:
 • 20 MPH school zones
 • 25 MPH in any urban district
 • 55 MPH for all other locations

 All other speed limits are established after the preparation
 of a traffic engineering and safety study per the Manual of
 Uniform Traffic Control Devices:
 • Roadway Design Speed
 • 85th Percentile Speed
 • Accident History
 • Roadway Conditions
                          Speed Limits
     H.B. 235 Speed Limit Designation Amendment
Grants authority to counites and municipalities to
establish speed limits on municipal roads without
completing the MUTCD process

Opportunities:
• Establish speed limits that are consistent and
  enforceable
• Establish speed limits that meet neighborhood
  safety objectives
• Establish a process to set limits that is easily
  understood and accepted
                          Speed Limits
              Option 1 – Maintain current process
Continue to set speed limits based on the established MUTCD process

PROS:
Nationally established methodology
Consistency in enforcement
No need to change existing speed limit signage

CONS:
Potential that speeds could be increased after analysis
                           Speed Limits
        Option 2 – Designate Based on Geometrics
Set speed limits on factors such as roadway width, curvature, curbs, bike lanes, or
sidewalks

PROS:
Potential for the setting of slower speeds consistent with citizen requests

CONS:
Time intensive to evaluate every road in city to provide a consistent methodology in
setting speed limits.
Potential for variability of interpretation of data
Requires an extensive amount of sign replacement
                           Speed Limits
       Option 3 – Designate Based on Classification
Set speed limits based on roadway classification

PROS:
Relatively easy to establish method of determination

CONS:
Requires for an extensive amount of sign replacement
Not all local roads are the same (Similar to option 2 but not
as extensive)
