3.      PRESENTATION BY RANKED CHOICE VOTING – Ms. Kelleen Potter.

Utah Ranked Choice Voting Representative, Kelleen Potter, reported that she previously served as
Director of Elections for the State of Utah and most recently as the Mayor of Heber City. She was
asked to assist cities in the valley that have implemented Ranked Choice Voting as part of the
legislative pilot project. She shared a survey conducted by Y2 Analytics that provided data specific
to Utah Voters. The majority of voters reside in Ranked Choice Voting (“RCV”) cities. The data
was weighted according to the projections of the likely voter proportions provided by the sample,
including weights for age, party registration, active or inactive, how long they have been
registered, and if they have voted in the past. The margin of error was +/-2.6 percentage points,
which is the gold standard in survey research.

The first question on the survey pertained was how user-friendly the voting method is. Most
respondents found it to be easy. The instructions were considered somewhat clear by 90% of
participants. In all cities with the exception of Sandy, over 50% liked the RCV ballot. Sandy City
was unique in that there were candidates who were opposed to RCV. In the end, 4,000 ballots
were not exhausted, which could have influenced the outcome.

A question was raised as to whether the voters who did not exhaust the ballot were opposed to the
RCV ballot, however, it was inconclusive. Many candidates in Sandy City told voters not to vote
for anyone else on the ballot, which was likely the reason for the outcome. The discussion
continued with explanations as to why people may vote a certain way and how RCV ballots require
a voter to know a little bit more about the candidates. It was noted that there is typically a 40%
voter turnout in Cottonwood Heights.

Being able to see the ballots to determine whether the majority of voters understood the
instructions would be helpful. There is an official Cast Vote Record, and Utah Law specifies that
certain official election material cannot be released. Utah County interpreted the law differently
than Salt Lake County and scrubbed voter information. As a result, they were able to gain access
to ballots and determine how voters responded to the instructions and the ease of voting. Salt Lake
County would not release the same information. Later, the State-backed up Salt Lake County in
its interpretation of the law.

It was noted that with Ranked Choice Voting people tend to be more likely to vote for their favorite
candidate if he/she is not among the top-ranking candidates without feeling like their vote was
wasted. People were generally satisfied with the voting process, which was the same for RCV and
non-RCV participants. The survey showed that 85% of voters would like to see the winning
candidate have the majority vote. It also revealed that 50% of the participants agreed that RCV
should be used for more Utah elections.

House Bill 264 – Municipal Alternate Voting Methods Amendment – addressed some of the
technical changes, such as recounts. The reason for the recount was questioned as well as when it
would occur. The Bill was passed, and the Legislature was still supporting the pilot project,
however, the City would have to determine to opt in for the next municipal election.

A question was raised as to whether it would be possible to have a Primary Election with the
Ranked Choice Voting and still do a final election. Ms. Potter stated that it is in the legislation
and the City could choose to do it that way.

A Council Member commented that as a candidate who ran in an RCV election, it is more difficult
to strategize and know who the direct competition is. He liked the idea of having a Primary
Election. It was confirmed that the Council will be required to vote on whether to use the RCV
method and notify the Lieutenant Governor by the spring of the voting year. There would be
further discussion as a Council at a later date to determine whether the City will move to that type
of voting for the next election.

4.      PRESENTATION BY UTAH ELECTION INITIATIVE – Ms. Cristy Bulkley.

Cristy Bulkley was present from Utah Election Integrity where she has served for two years. After
attending a city council meeting she was presented with information in support of and in opposition
to Ranked Choice Voting. During the meeting, she was swayed by the opposition to RCV. She
stated that she does not get paid for her time but feels it is important information to share. She
asked if Ranked Choice Voting is fair and stated that sometimes the idea of one person, one vote

gets skewed with RCV. It was determined that RCV elections can change the outcome of an
election as much as 17% of the time.

Eliminating a Primary Election can also limit Grassroots and Third-Party candidates. She stated
that RCV centralizes power, is unfair in multi-seat races, makes hand audits impossible, redefines
the majority and voter confidence, and causes minority disenfranchisement and ballot confusion.
It is promoted by global power brokers and is non-monotonicity, which can hurt the first-choice
candidate.

Ms. Bulkley discussed the order of elimination and explained that it makes a difference in an
election in terms of how votes are counted. RCV makes that difficult. She described issues with
centralized power and stated that it destroys liberty. She commented that many voters are
disenfranchised with RCV, and the term “disenfranchisement” was discussed. It was determined
that while voters are not able to vote in the manner they thought they were voting, they are still
able to cast votes. As a result, the language was changed from ‘disenfranchisement’ in the
discussion. In several multi-seat races throughout the valley, many voters were unable to use all
of their voting power due to the RCV chosen for the election.

Ms. Bulkley addressed the reasons Ranked Choice Voting is not the best voting methodology. She
cited several examples from across the nation as well as local elections. She presented the
Cottonwood Heights elections and showed how the numbers worked with the election. She
explained that after speaking with voters following an election, and specifically in Lehi, they
thought they had done the election correctly, however, after some things were explained to them,
they realized they did not understand how works. Thus, had they taken the survey mentioned by
Ms. Potter, they would have indicated that they understood it but in reality, they did not. She stated
that one of the main problems is that people do not know if their ballot is exhausted. It is also not
known by the voting company until after the election is over. Ballot confusion was also high
among the elderly and minority, non-English-speaker groups.

Ultimately, Ms. Bulkley asked that the Council consider their options carefully and do research
before making a decision. She stated that FairVote is the organization paying for RCV and
presented a list of contributors. She reviewed the reasons Ranked Choice Voting is unfair and
urged the Council to consider it carefully.

Mr. Tingey stated that the Big Cottonwood Marathon will take place again in September. The
event is very large and creates challenges for the City. As a result, discussion regarding the City’s
continued participation will need to take place. There have been numerous complaints from the
community and the cost-benefit is not great.

Culture Manager, Ann Eatchel, stated that immediately following the September event, the
Marathon Committee will apply for the following year. They typically announce the following
year’s event at the marathon and want to decide before it is too late. The Marathon Committee

originally approached the Council in 2012 and presented it as a great way to bring in tourism and
put Cottonwood Heights on the map as having a Boston Marathon qualifier. The marathon has

Generally, people do not like it. The permit includes the impact on traffic, security, and safety

        b.       Proposed Budget for 2022-2023 Fiscal Year – City Manager Tim Tingey.

Mr. Tingey reported that the public hearing on the budget would take place during the City Council
Meeting. Since the Tentative Budget was adopted in May, several items will be incorporated into
the budget that staff received direction on. The debt service for the Public Works Building will be
incorporated into the budget this year as well as the Road and Traffic Study, which will represent
a $15,000 increase. Additionally, a reduction in the $14,550 from the original proposal discussed
during the previous meeting will be included. The UFA will adopt its budget on June 21 and
anticipate an $87,611 reduction based on their Tentative Budget and the reductions from the
original proposal. Topical surveys such as Y2 Analytics will cost $5,700 for each survey. Those
funds could be incorporated into the budget, but he would need direction on the number of surveys.

There will be a $34,000 increase in Liability Insurance for the coming year. It addresses deductible
issues as well as rising costs and liability insurance throughout the country. The final property tax
rate and associated new growth revenue were expected the following week. That information will
be presented in preparation for the June 21 meeting. There was discussion at the last meeting
about reducing the Cost-of-Living Adjustment (“COLA”) amounts to 6%. Finally, the market
related to Mayor and Council Member salaries was discussed as well as the cost to bring those
salaries just above the current market needs to be addressed as well as increasing the vehicle
allowance for the Mayor and incorporating something similar for the Council. There was a
proposal to look at those items with a total cost of approximately $40,000. Staff asked for
direction. A separate public hearing would need to be held for the Council salaries per ordinance.

It was reported that two topical surveys are planned for in the budget. Cost-of-living increases
were discussed as well as how much the Council wants to approve for that. Comparisons were
given of cities across the valley. It was determined that a 6% increase would be best. A market
study should take place to provide a better understanding of where they are compared to other
cities. A concern was raised that hiring retention may be problematic if the increase is less than
7%. Council Member Birrell wanted to see the number be conservative, especially with today’s
volatile economy. The Council can always look at it again in September and make a correction if
needed at that time. It was determined that the Council would look at it again in September but
keep the COLA at 6%.

With regard to compensation adjustments for the Mayor and Council Members, the methodology
for determining the amounts was to look at similar cities around the valley. The responsibilities
of the Mayor and Council Members have changed over the years, so it was reasonable to consider
the increase. It requires a public hearing and an ordinance change. The City is currently behind
the market, based on research of other like cities. The compensation is based on 10 hours per week
with most Council Members spending two to three times that amount. The consensus was to move
forward with the public process for the ordinance change for the increase in salaries.

The Final Property Tax Rate would be available the following week. Changes could be made at

David Berry gave his address as 2295 Lorita Way and asked about the amount of the tax increase.

Mr. Tingey stated that no tax increase is proposed this budget year. The property taxes referred to
pertain to the State’s property tax assessment.

Mr. Berry asked about the Merit increase, what the target is, and how much and who will receive
it. Mr. Tingey explained that the Merit increase is based on a table adopted by the Council that
includes percentages based on an employee’s years of service. Merit increases are based on job
performance. These are based on an evaluation each employee goes through. Mayor Weichers
added that it is part of a 10-year plan for increases after which the employee is no longer eligible
for a Merit increase. The average increase is 2.4%.

Mr. Berry asked for clarification on the percentage of employees who will be getting an increase.
Mr. Tingey stated that historically the majority of the employees have qualified for the Merit
increase, but it is impossible to say an exact number because it is based on performance. The
amount in the budget is approximately $250,000. Mr. Berry asked for further clarification.
Mr. Tingey suggested they meet with the Budget Director to discuss additional details.

Runar Boman commented that the City has been receiving grant money and he felt it had been
spent wisely. His concern was that the funds received from the federal government will eventually
be discontinued. He asked if the City has a fund set aside for future expenditures. Mayor Weichers
reported that the City is required to maintain a minimum fund balance of 5%. Cottonwood Heights
has increased that amount and the monies that have come in from grants were allocated to one-
time projects. The City will not rely on those funds in the future. He explained that Merit-based
pay is common in every industry. City employees are eligible to receive Merit-based increases for
10 years, after which they are no longer eligible. It is not an automatic increase, but it is built into
the salary structure.

        5.1      Consideration of Resolution 2022-30 Consenting to Appointments to the
                 Parks, Trails, and Open Space Committee.

Mayor Weichers reported that the Council discussed the above Resolution during the Work
