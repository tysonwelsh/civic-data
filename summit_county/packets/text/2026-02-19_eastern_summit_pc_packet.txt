clarity, and intent of Chapters 1 and 2 of the draft General Plan.  
Background 
The draft plan is a culmination of a two-year planning effort that began with the “Our Summit” visioning 
survey in 2024. That plan established the community’s long-term vision and priorities and provided a 
foundation for the draft document. Throughout 2025, the County conducted General Plan Community 
Open Houses, online surveys, and engagement activities to refine that vision, evaluate existing 
conditions and develop goals, policies and strategies in each element of the plan. The draft has been 
reviewed and commented on by all applicable County departments and staff. Most recently, the State 
mandated Water Element was reviewed and adopted by the County Council on December 17, 2025, and 
has been incorporated into this draft.

Chapter 1: Introduction 
Purpose of The General Plan 
T
he Eastern Summit County General Plan established a long-range policy framework to guide 
growth, development, resource protection, and public investment decisions. The plan reﬂects 
community values and priorities while balancing private property rights, economic opportunity, 
environmental stewardship, and rural character.   
T
he General Plan is intended to support coordinated decision making by elected official, appointed 
board and commissions, County staff, residents, and property owners. It identiﬁes goals, objectives, 
policies, and implementation strategies that provide direction for land use regulations, capital 
improvement planning, infrastructure investment, and community development efforts.  
S
tate law requires Summit County to adopt and maintain a General Plan to guide future growth and 
development. While the plan does not regulate land directly, it serves as the foundation for land use 
regulations, zoning decisions, and County programs.  
T
he General Plan is intended to provide clear  policy direction while allowing ﬂexibility in 
implementation. The goals, objectives, and policies work together to guide decision-making  rather 
than function as rigid standards. Individual decisions should consider the full context  of the Plan, 
balancing community priorities, site-speciﬁc conditions, and long-term impacts. When evaluating 
proposals, decision makers should strive to advance the overall intent of the Plan while recognizing 
that not every policy will apply equally in every situation.  
How to Use the Plan 
The Plan is organized into Chapters addressing key elements of community planning such as, Land 
Use, Housing, Transportation and Connectivity, Economic Development, Natural and Cultural 
Resources, Open Space, Community Health and Recreation, Sustainable Development, Water Use, 
and Public Facilities and Services. Each Chapter describes existing conditions, and establishes 
goals, objectives, policies, and strategies.  
G
oals deﬁne the community’s long-term vision. Objectives identify measurable steps toward these 
goals. Policies guide decision-making, and strategies outline speciﬁc actions. Together they provide 
a framework for implementing the plan. 
T
he General Plan includes a Future Land Use Map illustrating preferred development patterns. This 
map is conceptual and ﬂexible, is not zoning map, and may evolve over time as conditions and 
community priorities change. The map reﬂects the unique character and needs of each area while 
remaining consistent with the overall goals, objectives, policies, and strategies of the plan. 
T
he ﬁnal chapter, implementation, identiﬁes speciﬁc actions, responsibilities, timelines, and 
funding considerations necessary to carry out the Plan and achieve its goals.  
Summit County Context 
3
Eastern Summit County is deﬁned by its landscape, water resources, and rural heritage. For 
thousands of years, the region supported seasonal travel, hunting, gathering and trade by the 
Shoshone and Ute tribes, who established a lasting relationship between people and the land. 
European settlement in the mid -1800s introduced permanent agricultural communities in areas 
such as Henefer, Coalville, and Kamas, where reliable water supplies supported farming, ranching 
and timber production. These activities established the rural working landscape that continues to 
shape community identity today. 
Today, Eastern Summit County is experiencing increasing growth pressures that are reshaping land 
use patterns, housing  demand, and community expectations. Interest in rural living, recreation-
based tourism, and second home development has expanded development potential across the 
region. Managing this growth while preserving agricultural viability, open space, and rural character 
presents one of the County’s most signiﬁcant planning challenges. 
The East Side’s large geographic scale and dispersed settlement patterns create unique constraints 
for infrastructure and service delivery. Many areas rely on individual water and wastewater systems 
and are located far from centralized public services. Exp anding infrastructure across rural 
landscapes can be costly and requires careful coordination with growth patterns to ensure that 
development remains ﬁscally sustainable and does not exceed the County’s ability to provide long 
term services. 
Transportation connectivity is another signiﬁcant issue affecting Eastern Summit County. Residents 
often rely on regional highways and limited roadway networks to access employment centers, 
education, healthcare, and commercial services both within and outside the County. Seasonal 
tourism, commuter travel, and recreational visitation contribute to congestion, safety concerns, and 
maintenance demands on transportation infrastructure. 
Housing availability and affordability have emerged as growing concerns throughout the region. 
Increasing land values, demand for second homes, and limited housing diversity make it difficult for 
local workforce populations, agricultural employees, and younger residents to ﬁnd attainable 
housing within their communities. Maintaining stable, year-round communities while 
accommodating population growth remains a key planning priority. 
Natural hazards, and environmental conditions also inﬂuence land use decisions. Wildﬁre risk, 
ﬂooding, drought, and watershed protection are increasingly important  considerations as 
development expands into wildland and agricultural areas. Protecting public safety, maintaining 
water quality and quantity, and reducing hazard exposure require coordinated land use planning and 
resource management strategies. 
Agriculture continues to play a vital economic, cultural, and scenic role in Eastern Summit County, 
but faces challenges including land fragmentation, rising property values, generational transition, 
and competing development pressures. Supporting long term agricultural viability requires policies 
that balance economic sustainability with preservation of working lands. 
Eastern Summit County is also inﬂuenced by regional economic and travel patterns. Proximity to 
recreation destinations, neighboring counties, and major employment centers create opportunities 
for economic activity while increasing development interest and infrastructure demand. 
4
Coordinating planning efforts across jurisdictional boundaries is essential to effectively address 
transportation, housing, environmental, and service delivery challenges. 
As growth continues and community expectations evolve, Eastern Summit County faces important 
decisions regarding land use, infrastructure investment, environmental stewardship, and 
community development. This General Plan establishes a policy framework to guide these 
decisions, ensuring that future growth supports community values, protects natural resources, and 
maintains the rural character and quality of live that deﬁne the East Side. 
D
emographics 
As of 2024, Summit County’s year- round population is 42,824 residents (United States Census 
Bureau). Of the total 42,824 residents, 6,850 reside in unincorporated Eastern Summit County. 
Historically, the County’s population has increased, but since 2022, the population has started to 
decrease by an average of ~200 persons (United States Census Bureau and Kem C. Gardner Policy 
Institute). The median age of Summit County residents is 41.8 years, among the highest in Utah.  The 
average age in Utah is 31.3 years.

Chapter 2:      Land Use (LU) 
 
Eastern Summit County values both private property rights and long-term stewardship of agricultural 
lands, natural resources, and rural community character. Land use policies within this chapter are 
intended to balance these interests by providing reasonable opportunities for landowners, while 
guiding growth toward locations and patterns that maintain community identity, protect resource 
systems, and ensure sustainable public services.  
 
Land use decisions should consider individual property rights alongside broader community 
impacts, including infrastructure capacity, environmental conditions, agricultural viability, and long-
term ﬁscal responsibility. The intent of these policies is not to prevent growth, but to guide growth in 
a manner that supports stable communities and preserves the qualities that deﬁne Eastern Summit 
County.  
 
The broader regional context, existing conditions, and community engagement ﬁndings that inform 
the Land Use Element are described in Chapter 1. This Chapter builds upon that foundation by 
translating community values and planning priorities into land use goals, objectives and policies.  
 
Community engagement consistently emphasized the importance of protecting open space and 
rural character, improving housing options, managing traffic, and supporting vibrant, well designed 
commercial areas. 
 
Existing Conditions  
 
Eastern Summit County’s land use currently includes a mix of residential, agricultural, recreational, 
and conservation areas. Zoning also allows for limited commercial uses along the highway corridor 
from Henefer to Woodland. Residential densities range from approximately one unit per ﬁve acres to 
one unit per eighty acres, with higher densities concentrated in the established unincorporated 
communities of Woodland, Marion, Oakley, Peoa, Wanship, Hoytsville, Upton and Echo. 
 
The Easten Summit County Development Code states that the intent of this zoning framework is to: 
 
“ …ensure that the rural, agricultural and small-town character of the eastern portion of the 
County shall remain, even in the presence of growth and change. The County intends to 
assure the managed, proper, and sensitive development of land to protect and enhance 
these desired qualities and the lifestyle that exists. ” 
 
In practice, the current zoning pattern supports a concentration of development within existing 
community areas while maintaining large areas of agricultural and rural land at low densities. This 
approach reﬂects long -standing efforts to preserve rural character, protect agricultural lands, and 
limit scattered or sprawling development patterns across the landscape.

Land Use Goals, Objectives, Policies, & Strategies  
 
GOAL: Preserve Eastern Summit County's rural character, agricultural heritage and natural 
landscapes by directing growth to appropriate locations, supporting viable communities and 
working lands, and ensuring development occurs in a manner consistent with available 
infrastructure, resource protection, and long-term sustainability  
 
OBJECTIVE A: Guide Growth to maintain rural character and identity.  
 
Policy 2.1: Direct higher intensity development toward existing community areas and 
locations with access to infrastructure and services.  
 
Strategy 2.1.1: Consider targeted density increases within existing community 
centers where infrastructure and services can support growth.   
 
Policy 2.2: Encourage development patterns that reduce land use conﬂicts between 
residential, agricultural, commercial and industrial uses.  
 
Strategy 2.2.1: Strengthen Critical Lands regulations in the Development Code. 
 
Policy 2.3: Promote compact and appropriately scaled development that preserves open 
space and maintains rural landscape patterns.  
 
Strategy 2.3.1: Evaluate zoning tools that allow neighborhood scale commercial 
and service uses.  
 
Policy 2.4: Support community-based planning through master planning of community 
planning areas.  
 
Strategy 2.4.1: Prioritize master planning for identiﬁed community hubs.  
 
 
OBJECTIVE B: Ensure development is supported by sustainable infrastructure and public services.  
 
Policy 2.5: Coordinate land use decisions with the availability and capacity of 
transportation, utilities and emergency services.  
 
Strategy 2.5.1: Require infrastructure and service adequacy review for 
development proposals.  
 
Policy 2.6: Promote ﬁscally responsible growth patterns that minimize long-term public 
service and infrastructure costs.  
 
Strategy 2.6.1: Coordinate with utility and service providers during development 
review.  
11
OBJECTIVE C: Support Agricultural viability and preservation of working lands.  
 
Policy 2.7: Protect agricultural lands and open space through conservation tools, and 
voluntary partnerships. 
 
Strategy 2.7.1: Support conservation easement programs and partnerships with 
land trusts.  
Strategy 2.7.2: Increase allowed density in existing unincorporated community 
hubs as identiﬁed in this document (Henefer, Echo, Hoytsville, Peoa, Marion, 
Woodland etc.).  
Strategy 2.7.3: Amend the zoning map and land use table to allow commercial 
uses/services in areas where people live and work. 
 
Policy 2.8: Limit development patterns that fragment prime agricultural lands or interfere 
with ongoing agricultural operations.  
 
Strategy 2.8.1: Consider agricultural impact assessments for development near 
active agricultural operations.  
 
Policy 2.9: Encourage economic diversiﬁcation and long-term viability of agricultural 
operations.  
 
Strategy 2.9.1: Encourage agritourism, value added agricultural uses, and local 
food systems.  
 
OBJECTIVE D: Protect natural resources and Critical Lands. 
 
Policy 2.10: Protect critical environmental features including wetlands, riparian corridors, 
watersheds, wildlife habitats and groundwater recharge areas.  
 
Strategy 2.10.1: Maintain and update Critical Lands regulations. 
Strategy 2.10.2: Promote habitat restoration and invasive species management.  
 
Policy 2.11: Minimize environmental impacts associated with development through site 
design, clustering, and land conservation techniques.  
 
Strategy 2.11.1: Coordinate watershed protection standards with the Water 
Element.  
 
Policy 2.12: Promote responsible water use and long-term watershed health.  
 
Strategy 2.12.1: Encourage water efficient landscaping and building practices.  
 
OBJECTIVE E: Support stable, livable rural communities.
