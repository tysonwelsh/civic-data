The following are the minutes of the City Council meeting of the Herriman City Council. The
meeting was held on Wednesday, October 30, 2024, at 5:00 p.m. in the Herriman City
Council Chambers, 5355 West Herriman Main Street, Herriman, Utah. Adequate notice of
this meeting, as required by law, was posted in the City Hall, on the City’s website, and
delivered to members of the Council, media, and interested citizens.

Councilmembers Present: Jared Henderson, Teddy Hodges, Sherrie Ohrn, and Steven
Shields

Staff Present: City Manager Nathan Cherpeski, Assistant City Manager Wendy Thomas,

1. Council Business
Councilmember Sherrie Ohrn expressed confusion regarding the recent communication
about the Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District concerning the representation for West

issue could be due to the fact that the areas in question were served by two different
districts, which could explain the double representation. He proposed working with the
Mayor to send a formal letter representing the City’s concerns.

   2.1. Review and discuss a proposed amendment to the Rosecrest Amended and
        Restated Master Development Agreement to reduce the project acreage from
        ±1,082.12 to ±254.6, and reduce the maximum dwelling units from 4,288 to 321,
        which is an average gross density of ±1.26 dwelling units per acre, for property

        Recreation Zones – Michael Maloy, City Planner
City Planner Michael Maloy noted he would like to focus on the recommendations offered by
the Planning Commission regarding the development agreement. He noted the development
team was in attendance to respond to questions. The recommendations offered by the
Planning Commission would influence the preparation of an ordinance for a future meeting.
City Planner Maloy briefly touched on the scope of the project, noting that it would involve
reducing the Rosecrest project area and transferring certain development rights to a new
master development agreement. City Manager Nathan Cherpeski highlighted that the
proposal would also eliminate the automatic ability to add new land to the project area.

Councilmember Steven Shields clarified that the ability to apply for adding land had not been

removed, but the automatic addition of land, which was a focus previously, was being
addressed. City Manager Cherpeski confirmed the statement. City Planner Maloy presented
the current master plan compared to the reduced master plan. The overall concept for the
three projects was highlighted to identify any issues. Councilmember Shields raised concern
about Section 7, which dealt with open space accounting. He pointed out that the land in
pods 42A and B, which was being transferred to the Panorama development agreement, was
being counted as open space in both the Rosecrest and Panorama areas. He questioned the
rationale behind double accounting and sought clarification on why the approach was
considered acceptable. Councilmember Shields noted that the proposed amendment
suggested that the open space requirements had been generally satisfied based on what has
been provided in the past and questioned what negotiations led to the decision.

City Planner Maloy addressed the challenge of limited financial resources for projects,
particularly in relation to a new park plan associated with the Panorama project in pod 42A.
He acknowledged concern about whether funds were being counted twice for the same
project and was open to the possibility that there could be a valid justification that he was
unaware of. DAI Legal Counsel Chase Andrizzi explained that the fourth amendment to the
Rosecrest Master Development Agreement included provisions where areas 42A and 42B
were dedicated to the City. These areas were part of the Panorama Development
Agreement, which obligated the developer to invest money in those spaces. The focus was
not on the specific acreage or percentage of open space within the Panorama project, but
rather on using 42A and 42B, which were originally part of a separate agreement to direct
funds into improvements. He noted that recent discussions with City staff have involved
planning improvements including potential off-site enhancements to nearby parks, such as
Juniper Canyon.

Legal Counsel Andrizzi clarified that the contributions of different developers were donating
the land as natural open space under the Rosecrest Agreement, and Panorama, which was
contributing funds for improvements were not counted twice. He explained that Panorama’s
role was not to provide land, but to invest in enhancing the value of the natural open space,
particularly in areas of higher land use intensity, like 42A and 42B. This was important
because such areas need accessible open spaces, which could serve as “pressure relief
valves” for recreation and play for families and children.

Councilmember Shields reflected on the challenges of limited usable green space,
particularly in the area south of the high school, where the lack of green space made it
difficult for children to play. He acknowledged the efforts to reclaim and create new green
spaces but emphasized that the absence of the space for a while was a significant issue.
Councilmember Shields expressed approval of the idea of investing in native open space and
creating a beautiful park but was concerned whether the original open space requirements
October 30, 2024 – City Council Minutes                                          Page 4 of 12

of the Rosecrest Master Development Agreement were being met through the changes. He
asked whether the change would reduce the amount of usable green space available to the
City. He suggested that the net impact of the changes on the open space for residents
needed to be examined and requested the numbers be reported to the Council. He noted that
in previous master development agreements, there were typically a Planned Unit
Development component that included a 20% open space requirement, which was important
to consider in this scenario.

City Planner Maloy explained there was no specific requirement for open space in the
context of a Master Development Agreement because of case-by-case negotiations. He
pointed out that if the City Council wished to establish a specific quantity of open space for
the Panorama project, they would have the authority to either accept the developer’s
proposal or negotiate a different arrangement. City Planner Maloy clarified that there was
no benchmark for open space because the project did not include a Planned Unit
Development component. Instead, the project would rely entirely on the Master
Development Agreement tool. Mayor Palmer recalled discussions regarding the field types
and terrain were focused on practical considerations, such as what could realistically be
developed in those areas, rather than on quantifying the exact amount of open space. He
noted there has not been any detailed discussion about specific amounts of open space yet.

Councilmember Shields emphasized that he valued the quality of open space over its
quantity, preferring well-designed, functional parks over less useful spaces, such as steep or
poorly designed areas. He acknowledged that the current focus was not just on having a
certain percentage of open space but ensuring it is of good quality and usable for the
community. Councilmember Shields requested the numbers to assess whether the proposed
plan for open space met the City’s standards and aligned with the Park General Plan. He
wanted confirmation from staff that the plan was well-designed, sufficient, and in line with
good planning principles. He also expressed concern about the potential for poorly designed
open spaces that would be difficult to maintain or that might end up being neglected,
referencing the possibility of unusable areas or spaces that could become problematic for
the City. He noted that he has heard estimates suggesting the project might involve $5-6
million in improvements but wanted more detailed information about what amenities these
funds would cover and what the outcome would entail. He sought clarity on how the
proposed improvements would benefit the community and ensure the project aligned with
sound planning goals. Councilmember Sherrie Ohrn agreed she didn’t want 20% open space
just to have unusable open space. Assistant City Manager Wendy Thomas interjected that
the money could be put into amenities for other park locations.

Councilmember Jared Henderson discussed the complexities of the financial aspects and the
integration of various agreements. He highlighted that while there wa an expected amount in

impact fees, the revenue generated, approximately $5.6 million, was insufficient to cover the
required infrastructure obligations. He expressed concern about how the different financial
elements, such as reimbursement agreements, impact fees, and the Community
Development Project Area, were not clearly tied together. He explained the numbers in the
project area were being finalized, and two separate agreements were being developed to
address these concerns. The agreements would also clarify past contracts, especially
regarding the distinction between restricted and less restricted funds and would clean up
older agreements. He stated that a Planned Unit Development overlay was being added to
formalize and integrate these agreements into the project. Community Development

Councilmember Shields raised concerns about the financial sustainability of the new fire
station, noting that the revenue from the area would cover only 58% of its operational costs,
while it would be responsible for 83% of the station’s expenditures. City Manager Cherpeski
clarified it was based on the projections for a new station, and the exact number of calls the
station would handle wouldn’t be known until it was operational. Councilmember Henderson
highlighted the disconnect between new growth and the funding required for public safety,
emphasizing that property taxes alone wouldn’t be enough to cover these costs. The Council
agreed that commercial revenue would be needed to offset the growing public safety
expenses, particularly as the City grows. Councilmember Ohrn pointed out the challenges in
explaining to residents why new growth wasn’t fully paying for itself, as it could lead to
pushback.

The Council agreed that a balanced land use approach was necessary, including commercial
development, for long-term sustainability. They discussed the need for broader policy
considerations including the need for continued dialogue on taxation, city development, and
financial planning.

    2.2. Review and discuss a proposed General Plan Amendment, Zoning Map
         Amendment, and a new Panorama Master Development Agreement to facilitate
         the construction of ±1,716 dwelling units and ±36.8 acres of commercial uses on
         ±330.3 acres of vacant property located approximately at 15202 S Mountain View
         Corridor Highway in the R-2-15, R-2-10, and R-20-43 Multi Family Residential
         Zone, and C-2 Commercial Zone – Michael Maloy, City Planner
City Planner Maloy explained that the Planning Commission had reviewed the General Plan
amendment, zoning amendment, and the new Master Development Agreement for
Panorama. While each of these items required separate public hearings, they would be
presented as a combined package in a formal meeting. City Planner Maloy emphasized that
the discussion would focus on the recommendation from the Planning Commission, and the
relevant maps and documents were available for reference, if needed.

Legal Counsel Andrizzi addressed the first topic which was a potential change in the street
and block layout due to the possibility that Panorama might not be able to acquire property
from neighboring developments, like Rosecrest. Planner Maloy asked whether Panorama
would still proceed with the same general street layout in case the property acquisition
didn’t go through. Legal Counsel Andrizzi confirmed that the layout would remain the same,
and any necessary modifications would be handled administratively at the engineering level,
in line with the South Mountain region master plan. He shifted the discussion to the topic of
architectural review and oversight. The Planning Commission had raised concerns about
ensuring that the City’s interests were protected in terms of maintaining high standards for
design and construction. Legal Counsel Andrizzi reassured the group that any changes to the
design guidelines would require City approval, addressing concerns that the master
developer might make unilateral changes. He explained that while the developer's
Architectural Review Committee would oversee design compliance, the City's architectural
review process would remain in place, ensuring consistency with City standards. City
Planner Maloy also pointed out that City staff had become more proactive in auditing plans
to ensure they complied with these guidelines.

City Planner Maloy discussed the fiscal impact of the project noting the initial expectation
had been for a collaborative approach to the fiscal analysis, City Manager Cherpeski and
other staff members had already been reviewing the development’s financial impact for
some time. City Manager Cherpeski indicated that the City would handle the analysis
independently, using the data provided by the developers to generate its own estimates, a
process the staff was already familiar with and prepared for.

City Planner Maloy noted a key concern from the Planning Commission was the
construction timeline for Juniper Crest Road, specifically regarding when it would be built
and whether it would be tied to the issuance of certificates of occupancy for related
October 30, 2024 – City Council Minutes                                          Page 7 of 12

subdivisions. Legal Counsel Andrizzi explained that Panorama was committed to completing
the road by August 2025, as it had a permit from UDOT that would expire at that time.
Failure to complete the road by then would require going through the permitting process
again, which would incur additional costs. The discussion included a proposal to not issue
certificates of occupancies for subdivisions that relied on Juniper Crest Road for secondary
access until the road was completed. This approach looked to prevent residents from having
to use Rosecrest Road as an alternative route. The developers agreed with the concept,
noting that this would incentivize them to move forward with the project.

Director Thomas explained that there were no concerns regarding the roadway, mentioning
that staff was working with UDOT. Engineering had confirmed everything was sufficient,
and they were ensuring that the trail plan was well integrated. He mentioned that through
the collaborative effort they had resolved all the issues from the Planning Commission and
asked the Council if there were any other questions.

Councilmember Shields raised an issue regarding the maximum number of residential units.
He noted a discrepancy in section 3.1, where it mentioned 1,717 units, but the first reference
had stated 1,716. He then referred to section 2.3.4, which mentioned taxes, asking if the
clause would prevent them from setting up special assessments or making adjustments to
the mill rate if there were insufficient funds to pay off a bond. He asked if the clause would
limit the ability of the Public Improvement District to make adjustments. City Manager
Cherpeski clarified that the clause applied to city-imposed taxes and didn’t necessarily
address the District. He noted staff would double-check the wording with legal, just to
ensure there were no issues.

Councilmember Shields also discussed a part of the agreement that stated the administrator
would approve any application exceeding the maximum number of residential units if the
changes were due to adjustments in lot layouts. He expressed concern that this could lead to
substantial changes being approved administratively, without Council involvement, and he
didn't like the word "shall" because it meant the approval was mandatory. He felt that such
changes should be brought to the Council, if substantial. He suggested the Council might
want to consider creating an internal policy to ensure that significant changes would be
brought before them. City Manager Cherpeski agreed that the "shall" language was
problematic and suggested that the Council might create a policy to determine when such
changes should be reviewed. Legal Counsel Andrizzi explained that the intent of the
development team had been to avoid coming back to the Council for small adjustments due
to changes in site conditions, but Councilmember Shields emphasized that if the changes
were substantial, the Council should be involved.

Councilmember Teddy Hodges asked if there would be concerns if the units were reduced,
October 30, 2024 – City Council Minutes                                          Page 8 of 12

to which Councilmember Shields responded that he wouldn’t have a problem with reducing
units, but he was concerned about large increases. Councilmember Ohrn pointed out that
defining what constituted a "substantial" change was difficult, and Councilmember Shields
suggested that it would be helpful to clarify this definition in the agreement. The Council
discussed how lot layouts could result in significant differences in revenue and impact fees.
City Manager Cherpeski suggested that an internal policy could define the thresholds for
changes that require Council review.

Councilmember Shields highlighted an issue with the language in item 3.2 of the agreement
regarding the developer’s obligation to construct portions of the project. He wanted
clarification that if the developer had done everything correctly for 15 years, they wouldn’t
be penalized for delays, and the agreement would automatically extend to 25 years. City
Manager Cherpeski confirmed that this was correct. Councilmember Shields expressed his
concern that agreements extending over long periods could become less predictable and
suggested that there should be regular reviews of the project, similar to past Master
Development Agreements like Olympia, to ensure that the developer and City’s priorities still
aligned.

Councilmember Shields also raised concern regarding section 3.5.1, questioning whether
there was any risk if grading began before a final plat or site plan was approved. City
Engineer Bryce Terry explained that the developer could begin grading with a land
disturbance permit, but it would be at their own risk, and any changes to the layout later
could require adjustments. Legal Counsel Andrizzi clarified a point in section 3.6 regarding
the excavation of natural materials. He explained that the language in the section was meant
to allow the developer to extract material for use in later phases without needing to tie it to
specific subdivisions. Councilmember Shields expressed concern that the language could be
interpreted to mean the developer could excavate wherever they wanted, which could be
problematic. Legal Counsel Andrizzi agreed to work on refining the language to make the
intent clearer and alleviate those concerns.

Councilmember Shields shifted the discussion to section 3.10, which concerned whether a
developer would lose density if they sold or donated land for civic use. He questioned the
clarity of the language, especially regarding fair market value and density loss. Legal Counsel
Andrizzi explained that the intent was to ensure that if land was donated for civic use, the
developer would not lose density. If land was sold, the developer would lose the density for
that land. However, if a portion was donated and a portion sold, the developer would retain
density proportional to the land that was sold. The Council discussed the implications, and
Legal Counsel Andrizzi said the development team was willing to remove the section, if
necessary.

Councilmember Shields raised another point about the term of the agreement, noting that it
was potentially set to last 25 years, even though initial expectations were for it to take 10 to
12 years. He emphasized the importance of maintaining the developer’s right to develop as
long as they were compliant. He suggested that after 15 years, there should be a review to
ensure that both the developer and the City’s needs still aligned. He proposed that a formal
review at the 12-year mark, like the Olympia agreement, could help avoid any future
misunderstandings. City manager Cherpeski agreed that such a process could work, and
they could include it in the agreement, ensuring regular meetings to track progress and
adjust expectations.

The Council focused on key aspects of a development or engineering project, with detailed
discussions on the engineering review fees, certification acceptance, and review processes
to ensure that City standards were upheld throughout the project. One of the main points of
discussion was the engineering review fee. Councilmember Shields raised concerns about
the current fee structure, specifically the $50 per hour charge for engineering reviews. He
questioned whether this amount adequately covered the City's actual costs for conducting
these reviews. He pointed out that if the fee was based on a flat rate, like $3,000 plus 3%, it
could end up being more than what had been spent on the entire development and
engineering process to reach the review stage. DIA Representatives responded by clarifying
that the City was statutorily required to charge only for the actual costs incurred in
performing the review, with no profit margin. The group agreed that using actual costs,
rather than a flat statutory fee, would ensure that the City’s expenses were adequately
covered without overcharging developers. They discussed the possibility of revising the
language to reflect these actual costs rather than sticking with a fixed statutory rate, which
could lead to discrepancies between the fee charged and the actual costs of the review.

The conversation shifted to a provision about the acceptance of certifications for
development applications. Councilmember Shields expressed concern about a provision that
would automatically accept development applications if they were signed off by a licensed
professional, such as an engineer. He worried that this could lead to the City accepting plans
without proper review, particularly if changes had been made to the designs after the
professional’s certification. The Development team clarified that the intent behind the
provision was to ensure that the original designer, who had stamped and signed the plans,
would remain legally responsible for the design. This would prevent a situation where the
City inadvertently takes on the responsibility of the design if the plans are altered
significantly during the review process. It was explained that the City didn’t want to take on
responsibility for changes that might have been made after the plans had already been
stamped by the professional, as this would legally place the burden on the city if issues arose
later. The group discussed ways to protect the review process while ensuring that the City's
standards were still met.

Further concerns were raised about the City potentially making changes to plans that
deviated from established standards. Some members of the Council expressed unease about
the implications of requiring modifications that might not align with the original design
intent. The conversation touched on the need to ensure that the City’s standards were
adhered to while still providing flexibility in certain areas. The Council discussed adding
language to the provision that would specify that only plans meeting the City's established
standards would be accepted, and that any deviations from those standards would need to
be carefully reviewed. It was suggested to have a process for addressing minor deviations
from standards in specific circumstances, where flexibility might be allowed but still within
the framework of the City’s overarching standards.

Additionally, there was a consideration to revise some language in the agreement to clarify
that the review process would continue even if the plans had been signed off by a licensed
professional. The Council agreed that ensuring the City’s review authority was maintained
was crucial, as there were concerns about the potential for approving plans without fully
assessing whether they met all necessary standards. To address these concerns, the Council
discussed the possibility of rewording certain provisions to make it clear that designs would
be approved only if they met the City's standards and that any changes outside of those
standards would be subject to thorough review. By adding this language, the Council hoped
to prevent misunderstandings and ensure that the City's engineering standards were
consistently applied to all development projects.

The Council requested that language regarding blanket approvals for certain backbone plans
needed to be modified to accommodate the Engineering Department’s concerns. It was
clarified that the City would not give a blanket approval for entire projects, but rather would
review specific alignments and rights-of-way to prevent future issues.

   2.3. Review and discuss a proposed General Plan Amendment, a Zoning Map
        Amendment, which includes the removal of the Hillside Overlay Zone, and an
        amendment to the South Hills Master Development Agreement to facilitate the
        construction of approximately 1,306 residential dwelling units on ±555.3 acres of
        vacant property located approximately at 15590 S Mountain View Corridor
        Highway in the R-1-10 Single Family Residential Zone, and R-2-10, R-2-15, and R-
        20-43 Multi Family Residential Zones, and C-2 Commercial Zone – Michael Maloy,
        City Planner
City Planner Maloy outlined the South Hills Area Master Development Agreement and
associated zoning amendments. He clarified the process of discussing the South Hills Area
Master Plan and the zoning amendments together with the development agreement. He
pointed out that there was a need to revisit certain zoning aspects since the original zoning
October 30, 2024 – City Council Minutes                                        Page 11 of 12

application did not account for the hillside overlay zone, which had been established after
the approval of the Master Development Agreement. This hillside overlay zone was designed
to protect the steep slopes and natural vegetation of the area, preventing excessive grading
and the destruction of scenic views. However, City Planner Maloy explained that the
agreement had already been approved prior to the hillside overlay regulations being put in
place, which meant the development had to be reassessed to balance both the goals of
development and the environmental preservation outlined in the new regulations.

Director Thomas presented a study using data to help identify the areas of the hillside that
were still undisturbed by human activities. He pointed out the areas where natural
vegetation and steep slopes remained intact, suggesting these should be prioritized for
preservation in the development plans. The development team was open to working with the
City to preserve the undisturbed areas while recognizing that some parts of the land had
already been disturbed or were actively being mined by a local mining company. The mining
operations were a significant factor in determining which areas could be preserved and
which were already too altered to warrant preservation. It was emphasized that the focus
should be on saving the more pristine sections of the land while acknowledging that parts of
the site would require development due to prior disturbances.

The discussion also addressed concerns raised by the community regarding the integration
of open spaces, parks, and recreational areas within the development. The developers
expressed a willingness to collaborate with the City and incorporate provisions for parks,
trails, and open spaces in line with the Park Master Plan. This was in response to concerns
that the development could lead to the loss of public space or reduce the quality of life for
future residents. The developers committed to ensuring that the project would include green
space and recreational amenities, contributing to a balanced and sustainable community.

The Council raised the issue of potential noise pollution from the nearby Camp Williams
military base. The developers recognized the problem, as the military base sometimes
generates loud noise from its activities, which could be disruptive to nearby residents. To
mitigate this issue, the developers proposed measures such as including real estate
disclosures in property transactions to inform buyers about the proximity of the military
base and its associated noise. Additionally, they offered solutions for noise reduction in the
design of homes, such as using upgraded insulation and double-pane windows to minimize
sound transmission. Despite the concerns, the developers emphasized that they would work
with the military to ensure transparency about the noise issue and address any potential
complaints.

City Planner Maloy indicated the Planning Commission acknowledged the importance of
maintaining the hillside overlay zoning to protect the area’s natural resources, but they also
October 30, 2024 – City Council Minutes                                     Page 12 of 12

recognized the unique circumstances of the development, including the existing mining
activities and the broader goals of the project. He relayed the suggestion of the Planning
Commission to find a compromise, allowing for some areas to be preserved while others
could be developed to meet the needs of the community. The Development Team reiterated
their commitment to creating a high-quality, aesthetically pleasing development that would
be integrated well with the natural environment. They emphasized that they were committed
to working within the City’s environmental preservation guidelines, even in areas that had
already been altered by mining.

The Council discussed revising the Master Development Agreement to simplify and
consolidate previous amendments. This process would streamline the development process
for all parties involved, making it easier to implement the necessary changes while
maintaining the balance between development and environmental protection. The
Development Team agreed to continue collaborating with the City, stakeholders, and the
community to ensure that the development would meet everyone’s needs and align with the
city's long-term goals for growth and sustainability.

Councilmember Hodges seconded the motion, and all voted aye.
