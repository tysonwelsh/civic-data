Councilmember Green said he had serious concerns about the proposed density for the
west side of the City, which he said was 2.5 to 3 times higher than the density in New

Larry Gardner stated the purpose of the West Foothill Zone was to create more of a
manufacturing area than the industrial area to the east, with a residential component that
would align with the State’s desire for affordable workforce housing. No more than 10%
of the West Foothill Zone would be multi-family housing, with a density of 30-60 units
per acre, and similar amenity requirements to those in the Planned Community Zone. Mr.
Gardner posed the following questions:

     •    What reasonable language could be added to the draft ordinance to ensure
          residential was not built first?
     •    Were there merits to a zone of this type?
     •    What residential density was acceptable?
     •    Would it be beneficial to identify general locations, within the overall 565 acres
          park, where residential and commercial could be built?

Councilmember Worthen said she would prefer to not see a residential component in the
subject area. Councilmember Whitelock said she also was not in favor of a residential
component.

Chair McConnehey said he would like to see the subject area used to complement the
City’s already significant residential, and said he envisioned something similar to the
research park near the University of Utah on the east side of the Valley.

Chair Quinney suggested reducing the percentage of multi-family housing allowed, but
stated he did not believe it made sense to prohibit housing in the area. He suggested the
right type of housing could benefit those working in the area.

Councilmember Green responded that interchange areas were designated as the
appropriate locations for higher density housing in the City. He said he believed it would
be irresponsible to approve extremely high density at the subject location, especially
given recent experiences with the COVID-19 pandemic.

Councilmember Worthen agreed with Councilmember Green, and said she agreed with
the research park suggestion. Councilmember Lamb pointed out that the area was already
zoned for a research park. Councilmember Whitelock said she felt there was a lot of
housing available in the area, and emphasized that without a balance between residential

    b. ACCESSORY DWELLING UNITS
Scott Langford gave a presentation about accessory dwelling units (ADUs). He explained
that in West Jordan, Accessory Living Quarters (ALQs) were conditionally permitted in
the Rural Residential (RR), Residential Estate (RE), and Agricultural (A) Zones.

     Accessory Living Quarters: An area detached from the principal dwelling unit
     provided for guests, relatives or domestic employees of the occupants of the principal
     dwelling, which may include cooking, sleeping and sanitary facilities, and which is
     not leased or rented, except to family members related by blood, marriage or
     adoption. The term shall not include “apartment” or “dwelling unit”.

     Accessory Dwelling Unit: A complete secondary residential unit that can be added to
     a single-family residential lot. ADUs can be attached to or part of the primary
     residence, or be detached as a separate building in a backyard or a garage conversion.
     An ADU provides completely separate living space including a kitchen, bathroom,
     and its own entryway.

The Council and Planning Commission electronically responded to questions asked by
Mr. Langford. A majority responded in favor of:

     •    allowing ADUs in all zones that permit single-family homes (if certain conditions
          are met);
     •    limiting ADUs to one accessory living quarter per principal dwelling unit;
     •    requiring that all properties with an ADU record a deed restriction on the property
          indicating the restricted uses and conditions of the ADU;
     •    requiring an affidavit from the property owner(s) stating the ADU will comply
          with all of the restrictions and conditions associated with the ADU;
     •    permitting home occupation/business in ADUs;
     •    setbacks specifically developed for ADUs;
     •    basing the number of required off-street parking stalls on the number of bedrooms
          in the ADU;
     •    requiring that ADUs have a permanent foundation; and
     •    requiring ADUs to be part of the Good Landlord program.

A majority responded against:

     •    limiting ADUs to lots that are 40,000 square feet or larger (current ALQ code);
     •    requiring adjoining property owner(s) consent for ADU approval;
     •    restricting size of ADU compared to the gross floor area of the principal dwelling
          unit; and
     •    only permitting ADUs in conjunction with new developments, as part of approved
          master plans such as in PC or PRD Zones.

Responses were closely split regarding:

     •    whether or not a property owner should be required to live on site (either in the
          principal dwelling or accessory dwelling);
     •    whether or not ADUs should be allowed in front, side and corner side yards;
     •    whether or not detached ADUs should be required to be architecturally
          compatible/complementary with the principal dwelling;
     •    placement of required ADU off-street parking, and whether or not off-street
          parking requirements should be reduced or waived if ADU is within ¼ mile of
          TRAX station; and
     •    whether or not ADUs should have a separate utility meter and address.

Concerns were expressed regarding: parking; upkeep/maintenance of property; potential
strain on existing infrastructure; and potential changes to the nature of established single-
family neighborhoods. Councilmember Jacob argued in favor of improving City code and
code enforcement to address individual concerns, many of which exist independent of
ADUs. Mayor Burton commented on the need to address licensing and enforcement.
