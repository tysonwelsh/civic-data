Fund Organization and Restrictions

Mr. Anderson explained that while the city budget appears large as a whole, many funds are
legally restricted and cannot be used interchangeably.

Examples discussed included:

   •    General Fund
           o Supports core city services including:
                  ▪ Police
                  ▪ Fire
                  ▪ Public Works
                  ▪ Parks and Recreation
                  ▪ Community Development
                  ▪ Administrative functions such as Finance, Human Resources, and Legal
                     services
   •    Library Fund
           o Restricted by state law and can only be used for library-related services and
              operations.
   •    911 Fund
           o Restricted solely to emergency communication services and operates as a self-
              sustaining fund.
   •    Special Governmental Funds
           o Includes entities such as redevelopment agencies with expenditures limited to
              their authorized purposes.
   •    Enterprise Funds
           o Includes utilities and similar operations with strict legal limitations regarding use
              of funds.

He emphasized that the total budget amount can create the impression that funds are
broadly available, but many revenue sources are restricted and cannot simply be transferred
to unrelated city needs.

Long-Term Planning Discussion

Council discussed the capital project schedule and the distinction between shorter-term and
longer-term planning.

Discussion points included:

   •    The formal capital schedule includes a five-year planning horizon, but some
        departments project needs much farther into the future.
   •    Longer-range projections may identify projects 6–20 years out.
   •    These longer-term items are considered less certain and subject to change.

Council noted several benefits of maintaining longer-range projections:

   •    Provides residents with general expectations regarding future infrastructure work.
   •    Helps staff identify projects in the planning pipeline.
   •    Allows departments to coordinate projects.

Examples discussed included:

   •    Coordinating road reconstruction projects with:
          o Stormwater improvements
          o Water system improvements
          o Sewer infrastructure improvements

Council noted that coordinated infrastructure planning can reduce repeated road construction
and improve efficiency.

Fraud Risk Assessment: Richard Anderson, Finance Director

  •    Mr. Anderson presented the annual state-required fraud risk assessment review to the
       Council. Logan City received a score of 355 out of 395 points, placing the City in the
       low to very low fraud risk category.
  •    Mr. Anderson explained that Logan performs well in key areas including:
           o Conflict of interest policies
           o Procurement policies
           o Ethical behavior standards
           o Internal controls and audit practices
  •    Two areas where additional points could potentially be earned were discussed:
           o Councilmember participation in fraud prevention training within a four-year
              period.
           o Written ethical behavior acknowledgments from all city employees.
  •    Mr. Anderson noted that obtaining full compliance for every employee, including full-
       time, part-time, and temporary staff, would be difficult to maintain and is not currently
       considered practical.
  •    Council members were encouraged to complete available fraud training and submit
       confirmation so records can be maintained for future assessments.
  •    Mr. Anderson confirmed the City maintains a fraud hotline and periodically sends
       reminders to employees regarding reporting procedures and fraud awareness.
  •    Additional discussion emphasized that while state assessment tools provide a general
       framework, the City also relies on other internal controls and Audit Committee
       practices designed to prevent and deter fraud.
  •    Mr. Anderson noted professional efforts to strengthen fraud prevention, including
       expertise in identifying fraud risks and opportunities.
  •    Discussion also addressed the increasing sophistication of fraudulent activity,
       including phishing and scam attempts, with Mr. Anderson reporting that the City
       receives 20–30 suspicious emails per day attempting to obtain money or
       information.
  •    Mr. Anderson emphasized that fraud prevention efforts focus on:
           o Deterring fraudulent activity
           o Maintaining strong policies and procedures
           o Preparing employees to recognize risks
           o Strengthening internal controls where possible
  •    It was noted that completely eliminating all fraud opportunities is unrealistic; the City's
       approach is to minimize risk and remain proactive.

Pay for Performance: Richard Anderson, Finance Director


  •    Rich Anderson reviewed the proposed employee compensation structure included in
       the budget. The proposal includes:

  •    3% allocated for pay-for-performance increases
  •    2% allocated for market adjustments

  •    Mr. Anderson explained that the two categories are separated because not all
       employees receive the same adjustments:
  •    Pay-for-performance increases are based on employee performance evaluations,
       with stronger performance resulting in larger increases.
  •    Market adjustments are used to address compensation competitiveness, recruitment
       and retention concerns, and department-specific staffing needs identified by Human
       Resources and department leadership.
  •    Council discussion included questions regarding how the City was able to fund the
       proposed compensation increases.
  •    Mr. Anderson explained that funding for the proposed increases was made possible
       largely through:
  •    Conservative revenue forecasting practices used in prior years
  •    Revenue growth and adjustments to revenue projections
  •    Increased confidence in projected revenues, particularly within sales tax and other
       funding sources
  •    Mr. Anderson noted that sales tax remains the City's largest General Fund revenue
       source and that while it is volatile, projections have been adjusted to be somewhat
       more aggressive while still maintaining a conservative approach.
  •    It was explained that prior conservative projections allowed excess revenues to be
       directed toward capital projects, creating flexibility in future budget planning.
  •    Mr. Anderson indicated that the State has recently expressed concerns regarding local
       governments maintaining larger reserves and accumulating excess funds for future
       projects.
  •    Discussion included the governmental concept of inter-period equity, which suggests
       that those receiving the benefit of services or infrastructure should also bear the
       related costs.
  •    Mr. Anderson noted differing perspectives regarding long-term capital funding
       strategies, including:
  •    Pay-as-you-go funding through reserves
  •    Financing projects through debt or bonding
  •    Maintaining a balanced approach between reserves and debt
  •    Council discussed the challenges cities face in adapting to changing State guidance
       regarding reserve levels and financial practices, noting that frequent changes can
       create long-term planning difficulties.
  •    No formal action was taken; discussion was informational and intended to provide
       budget context for Council consideration.

Ambrie Darley, Human Resource Director

Ambrie Darley, Human Resource Director addressed the Council and explained the

        employee retirement contributions through the Utah Retirement Systems (URS).
   •    Ms. Darley explained that Logan City participates in the state retirement system and is
        required each budget year to adopt a resolution establishing the City's employee
        contribution pickup practices.
   •    Discussion included clarification regarding Tier 1 and Tier 2 employee classifications:
   •    Employee tier status is determined by URS eligibility dates, not by Logan City.
   •    Employees who became eligible through any URS employer prior to July 2011 are
        classified as Tier 1 members.
   •    Employees becoming eligible after July 2011 are classified as Tier 2 members.

   •    Ms. Darley explained the City's current contribution structure:
   •    Logan City pays 100% of required employee contributions for Tier 1 members.
   •    Tier 2 employees are responsible for their employee contribution portion, while
        the City continues paying the employer-required portion.
   •    Council discussion included questions regarding employer contribution rates and
        differences among employee categories.
   •    It was explained that employer contribution percentages vary based on employee
        classifications, including:
   •    Public employees

Logan City budget transfers Fiscal Year 2027 – Richard Anderson
   •    Mr. Anderson reviewed the City's transfer policy, noting that transfers are made from
        enterprise funds to support general city services.
   •    Current transfer rates include:
           o Sewer Treatment Fund: 5.5%
           o Water Fund: 7%
           o Environmental Fund: 8%
           o Electric Fund: 8%
   •    He explained that while transfers can be a complex and sometimes controversial
        topic, they provide an important source of indirect funding for city operations.

Purpose of Transfers

   •    Transfers help offset costs associated with providing services to:
           o Nonprofit and tax-exempt organizations that do not pay property taxes.


                                                                                       8 | Page
       Logan Municipal Budget Workshop Meeting Minutes ~ Logan, Utah ~ May 12, 2026
            o Governmental entities and institutions located within Logan.
            o Services provided outside city boundaries.
   •    Utah State University was referenced as an example of a major community asset that
        occupies significant property within the city but does not contribute property tax
        revenue.

Financial Impact

   •    Mr. Anderson explained that transfers generate approximately $7 million annually.
   •    Without transfers, the City would face significant impacts, including:
           o Major reductions in services such as police, fire, public works, and parks.
           o Significant property tax increases to replace lost revenue.
   •    Discussion emphasized that eliminating transfers would shift costs from utility users
        and tax-exempt entities directly onto property taxpayers.

Ratepayer Considerations

   •    Mr. Anderson stated that utility rates should remain competitive and reasonable for
        customers.
   •    As long as rates remain comparable to other providers, transfers help support city
        services while maintaining affordable utility costs.
   •    Enterprise fund transfers are incorporated into utility rate studies.

Administrative Support and Shared Services

   •    Council discussion noted that enterprise funds benefit from shared city resources,
        including:
            o Administrative services
            o Legal support
            o Technology services
            o Risk management
            o Mayor and executive support
   •    Administrative fees are charged separately from transfers; however, not all support
        costs are fully recovered.

Transfer Policy History

   •    The current transfer structure was established by Ordinance 15-40 in 2015.
   •    Mr. Anderson stated that transfer percentages have generally decreased over time
        and have remained unchanged since 2015.
   •    Previous efforts included separating administrative fees from transfer calculations for
        greater transparency.
