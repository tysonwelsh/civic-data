5. Planning Commission Training — Utah Property Rights Ombudsman’s Office.
Jordan Cullimore introduced himself as the lead attorney in the Utah Property Rights Ombudsman's
Office and thanked the Planning Commission for their time. He has worked with the office since
being hired by Brent Bateman in 2015 and has been the lead attorney since 2019. The Ombudsman's
Office has been a resource for property owners, local governments, elected and appointed officials,
and developers for almost 30 years. It is a neutral state agency whose primary role is to help citizens
resolve property rights disputes with the government. There are two other attorneys in the office,
Marcie Jones and Richard Plehn, as well as planner Rob Terry, who coordinates training throughout
the state.
One of the primary dispute resolution tools the Ombudsman's Office provides is mediation in eminent
domain cases. The legislature created the Office as a resource to help local governments understand
their obligations regarding eminent domain, and to help property owners know their rights and
provide them with necessary resources. The primary dispute resolution tool for land use is the
Advisory Opinion. Property owners request mediation for eminent domain issues but either party can
request an Advisory Opinion. The Ombudsman's Office has provided nearly 300 advisory opinions
since its creation, and it has a good reputation for providing legal analysis to move disputes forward
and resolve them. Mr. Cullimore noted that there is always an attorney on call, and calls are usually
returned the same business day.
Mr. Cullimore reported that zoning and land use is a local concern and the issues Bluffdale faces are
different from Riverton, Moab, St. George, or Tremonton. He then discussed legislative and
administrative decisions. He noted that it is helpful for even experienced commissioners to discuss
the process of making legislative recommendations to the City Council. The Council is the only body
tasked with determining City Code and development standards. By State Statute, the Planning
Commission is a technical advisory committee that considers the facts and makes recommendations
to the City Council. The idea is that the Planning Commission is removed from politics and therefore
can make a more measured decision based on technical aspects.

In response to a question from Mr. Cullimore, Community and Economic Development Director,
Grant Crowell confirmed that all administrative land use decisions are made by the Planning
Commission, not the City Council. Mr. Cullimore stated that recent legislative changes prohibit the
Council's involvement in subdivision decisions. It can be the administrative decision maker in other
cases but it is recommended that they be wholly removed from administrative decisions. The City
Council is very good at listening to public opinion and making decisions based on that opinion, which
is appropriate for legislative decision-making.
Legislative decisions involve making laws based on weighing broad, competing policy
considerations. For example, the specific areas of the City where houses, gas stations, restaurants, or
flex warehouses should be allowed. The City Council makes the ultimate decisions about the General
Plan, land use ordinances, development standards, the zoning map, annexation decisions, and
sometimes development agreements. Legal standards of review include that the Planning
Commission's recommendation and City Council's decision must be consistent with applicable state
and federal law. For example, the state has mandated specific cases in which Accessory Dwelling
Units ("ADU") must be allowed. If the City passed an ordinance that restricted ADUs beyond what
the state allows, that would be illegal. The court's job is to interpret the law, not make the rules. Ifa
local government has made a rule that does not otherwise conflict with state or federal law, then it
stands as long as it is reasonably debatable to be in the public interest. Public interest could be
determined by evaluating technical data or listening to the public. The Planning Commission can
consider residents’ opinions on what they want the character of their community to be.
In administrative decision-making, public opinion cannot be considered. For example, if an
application is made for a Conditional Use Permit or commercial site plan, the Planning Commission
determines if the application complies with the rules. Where the Code does allow discretion,
conditions can be imposed for things like traffic mitigation, but in that case, substantial evidence is
required. For example, a traffic or engineering study or geotechnical report that supports the
discretionary decision being made. It has to be consistent with state or local law, and any
determinations must be supported by evidence.

Chair Cragun indicated that the Planning Commission does not often get public comment, but
occasionally they do have an audience. She felt it is a good reminder of the Planning Commission's
role. Elected officials consider public clamor very differently than the Planning Commission. When
residents are speaking passionately about the negative impact of a decision, it is easy to get swept up
in that emotion, but that is not the Commission's role. She asked Mr. Cullimore to expand on the
topic of public clamor.
Mr. Cullimore stated that in a legislative context, what someone says is referred to as public input.
In the administrative context, if that same opinion is not supported by a Code provision, it is
considered public clamor. He noted that there is often no definitive way to fully satisfy someone who
has brought their opinion before the Commission. Some local governments take a 15-minute break
after considering legislative items, then go into the administrative items. Some will read a statement
outlining the standards they will follow in making administrative decisions. For example, they might
provide examples of things they can and cannot consider and why. Educating the public in that way
can be beneficial, especially with contentious items.
Mr. Cullimore noted that state law does not require public hearings for administrative decisions and
asked if Bluffdale had local requirements. Mr. Crowell indicated that there is a hearing requirement
for Conditional Use Permits, although most conditional uses have been removed from the use table,
and plat amendments may also require a public hearing. Mr. Cullimore stated that if the Commission
holds many hearings where they have to tell people that their opinions cannot be considered, the City
may want to consider removing the public hearing requirement from those items, Public hearings are
required by state law for legislative decisions, but not administrative decisions.
Chair Cragun stated that the topic that is brought up most often is traffic. She appreciates it when
people are engaged and want to participate in the process, but the traffic study will indicate one thing
and people in the audience who live on that street will have a strong opinion to the contrary. The
traffic study is evidence, and the public comment expresses fear, worry, and opinion. Mr. Cullimore
stated that traffic mitigation is an area where the City needs to support its decision with substantial
evidence on the record. He believes it is important to acknowledge and recognize the speculation and
conjecture expressed by the public because they are expressing real concerns, but from a property

Mr. Crowell asked how pending ordinances affect vesting. Mr. Cullimore stated that the pending
ordinance rule is an exception to the vested rights rule. In most states, if a rule is changed before
building commences, the new rule will apply. Utah is an early vesting state. When a complete
administrative application is submitted and the fee is paid, the rules are locked in and cannot be
changed. There are, however, two primary exceptions. First is the prevailing countervailing public
interest. For example, if someone submits an application to build a home and the next day a geological
hazard is discovered on a neighboring lot, that is an exception. Another exception is when a pending
ordinance has been initiated in the manner provided by local ordinance prior to submission of the
application.

In response to a question from Mr. Cullimore, Mr. Crowell stated that the Bluffdale City Code does
not specify when a pending ordinance has been initiated. Mr. Cullimore recommended that language
be added so that the City has 180 days to consider and pass each ordinance. If an application comes
in that may be affected by the pending change, the applicant can be put on notice regarding the
potential rule change. He noted the importance of clarity and specificity. “We're looking at
regulations related to residential zones," is too broad to overcome the vested rights rule. "We are
looking at front, rear, and side setback requirements in the R-1-10 zone," is specific enough to
overcome the rule. If someone submits an application proposing setbacks based on the current Code,
they can be notified of the potential change. If the ordinance is passed, the new setbacks will apply
