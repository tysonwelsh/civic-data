1. Discussion regarding accessory dwelling units – Planning Staff 
 
Community Development Director Patrick Putt explained that the Planning Commission 
typically does not see information regarding accessory dwelling units, because they are allowed 
uses in the Development Code.  As part of the Development Code rewrite, they may want to look 
at the language regarding accessory dwelling units.  Typically, in other jurisdictions accessory 
dwelling units are not an allowed use but tend to be heavily regulated and go through a public 
process.  In Summit County, accessory dwelling units are considered to be a desirable use if done 
in the right way, because they provide benefits such as moderate-priced rental units, multi-
generational housing, and housing opportunities for disabled individuals.  In re-writing the Code, 
Staff would like to address some of the issues they face when approving accessory dwelling 
units.  It is not uncommon to run across a plan where, either by design, or unintentionally, the

plan shows multiple dwelling units that cross the line from an accessory unit in a primary house 
to a multi-family structure. 
 
Director Putt reviewed the current standards for accessory dwelling units and explained that, if 
an accessory dwelling is detached, the size is restricted, but if it is within the primary structure, 
there is no size limit.  Nothing would prevent the dwelling from becoming a duplex and doubling 
the density on the property, which would cause additional impacts on the neighborhood.  The 
current standards also require a restrictive use covenant on the accessory unit.  They would like 
to provide better predictability and problem-solve in advance with more concrete criteria without 
creating a disincentive for accessory dwelling units. 
 
Director Putt reported that Staff has discussed several changes they could make to the standards 
for accessory dwelling units.  One would make the size limitation consistent for both detached 
and attached accessory dwelling units.  One way to do that would be to limit the accessory unit 
to 25% of the primary unit’s size with a maximum cap of 900 square feet.  Another would be to 
specify a maximum of two bedrooms in the accessory unit and a maximum of one bathroom.  
The Code currently does not address how many accessory dwelling units can be on a parcel.  It is 
assumed there would be one accessory dwelling per parcel, but the Code does not specify that.  
There is also no specific provision to deal with parking, and Staff believes there should be a 
parking metric tied to the number of bedrooms.  They also recommend that the accessory units 
be metered as part of the primary residence and that the accessory dwelling units not be used for 
nightly rentals.  They should also require a restrictive use covenant as they do now. 
 
Commissioner DeFord asked if an accessory dwelling unit would have a separate entry.  
Director Putt replied that usually it would.  The idea is that it functions as an independent living 
structure.  Commissioner Peck asked why the accessory unit would be metered with the main 
structure if it is designed for independent living.  Director Putt replied that would be a safeguard 
to assure that the accessory unit is not sold separately from the main dwelling.  Commissioner 
DeFord confirmed with Staff that this would not preclude someone from putting a second 
kitchen or other facilities in a home that might look like an accessory dwelling.

Commissioner Peck asked what Staff is trying to achieve.  Director Putt replied that they are 
trying to create predictability.  Commissioner Peck asked why 2,000 square feet is allowed for 
agricultural and 1,000 square feet for residential.  Director Putt explained that agricultural 
dwelling units would be like a bunkhouse for employee housing, and what they are discussing 
does not apply to that category.  Staff is talking about setting up predictable standards that 
someone can easily achieve.  The original intent of accessory dwelling units was for a property 
owner to use a small amount of area to create an ancillary, secondary living space, not something 
equal to the primary dwelling unit.  He did not believe it was intended to create additional 
density or additional impacts on water and sewer.  Staff more frequently runs into situations 
where plans appear to function as multi-family structures, and the square footages are equal to 
the primary structure.  They want standards Staff can easily administer and that the public can 
easily understand.  This can be a legitimate tool in terms of creating affordable housing, and they 
want to be able to promote that. 
 
Commissioner Harte commented that the idea of a secondary unit looking like a secondary unit 
makes sense, but he struggled with limiting it to 1,000 square feet.  He did not feel comfortable 
with imposing a limit on total square footage.  He was not sure they want an ordinance that 
precludes nightly rentals, because the County could lose out on tax revenue that could come by 
allowing nightly rentals. 
 
Commissioner Lawson stated that he is in favor of what Staff proposes.  He was comfortable 
with the 900-square-foot limitation and believed 900 or 1,000 square feet could be quite 
functional.  He suggested that Staff research operations like Air B n B and see if that is 
something they do or do not want to be in favor of. 
 
Commissioner Peck asked why the County cares about nightly rentals.  Commissioner DeFord 
explained that the intent is to push these units toward affordable housing and rent them long 
term, such as for seasonal employees, rather than using them for nightly rentals for someone who 
comes in to ski for a day or two.  Director Putt explained that this discussion is not about 
changing their philosophy on nightly rentals but about targeting accessory dwelling units to solve 
other housing needs.  Allowing accessory dwellings to be used as nightly rentals creates the
