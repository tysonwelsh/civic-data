3.      REPORTS

        3.1      Y2 Analytics – Kyrene Gibb

      Kyrene Gibb, Partner and Vice President of Research at Y2 Analytics explained that there
were 468 residents (out of several thousand) who had responded to the survey that year which
resulted in a margin of error of approximately + or - 4.5%. She displayed a map which outlined
where in the city the respondents resided.

The survey time-frame was November 12 through December 29 and perhaps due to the busy
nature of that season, the response rate had been lower than what was ideal. Ms. Gibb stated they
would distribute the survey again in January next year and hopefully get more responses. The
survey questions addressed general health of the city, city services and communications,
community events and development priorities, and water conservation.

After Ms. Gibb reviewed many of the survey results, Councilmember Harker pointed out that there
were many residents that questioned the validity of the survey when they received it. Ms. Gibb
responded that her firm had seen an increase in pessimism and skepticism amongst the public over
the last three years—not just in Taylorsville, but among all groups. She recommended that in the
future, perhaps her staff work more closely with city communications staff to get the word out that
a survey would be forthcoming.

Councilmember Cochran’s constituents had expressed concern that they did not receive the survey.
Ms. Gibb explained that the challenge was that in order to be statistically representative and have
a random sample, they couldn’t have everyone participate in that particular survey. If it would be
helpful going forward, they could look for opportunities for after the scientific fielding period had
concluded—opening the survey up to any resident who wished to participate.

     3.2 Prosecutors Report

      City Attorney Tracy Cowdell explained that his firm had contracted with Taylorsville for 13
years. Stowell & Crayk had been providing indigent defense work for the City for even longer.
Although the justice court had been in the news in previous years, he was pleased that those days
were over and mentioned that Judge Bown was great to work with.

There were four current challenges his office was facing. They were:
   • A trend at the District Attorney’s office of declining more cases than they ever have
       before—particularly on property crimes
   • The jail was releasing people that would have previously been held.
   • It was getting harder to keep prosecutors on staff as large firms began offering more money.
   • There was a growing coalition of those who sought a softer, gentler type of policing. Now
       it appeared that the legislature was also beginning to target police and prosecutors, and
       putting their hands in how they conduct business in the courtroom.

Mr. Cowdell explained how little of a court fine represented city revenue and how much went
elsewhere. For example, ninety percent of a DUI fine went to the state while the city only received
ten percent. Other types of crime might have a lower sur-charge, but most of every fine went to
the county and the state rather than the city. So, despite what some believed, justice courts were
not cash cows for Taylorsville or any other municipality.

                 3.3 Unified Fire Authority – UFA Captain Richard Rich

Captain Richard Rich presented statistical information pertaining to the 4th quarter of 2022,
explaining that the numbers for medical calls remained fairly consistent over the last several years.

Activities in the UFA:
   • Asst. Chief Higgs had retired after 45 years of services
   • New recruit class began earlier in the month with 33 recruits
   • Lateral firefighter and paramedic recruitment remained open

Recent Promotions in UFA:
   • Dustin Dern was recently promoted to Asst. Chief
   • Wade Russell was recently promoted to Operations Chief
   • Jay Torgerson and Kenneth Aldridge were promoted to Battalion Chief

Captain Rich explained the “Close Before You Doze” program which sought to educate the public
about the importance of keeping interior bedroom doors closed at night. Such doors could provide
up to 30 minutes of safety in the event of a house fire. Conversely, if the door was left open,
occupants might have only 3 minutes in which to escape. The synthetic nature of today’s home
furnishings made them much more flammable, and they contained substances that became toxic
when ablaze. Most fatalities from fire incidents were a result of inhaling toxic gases and smoke
rather than from burns.

Councilmember Harker commented on the importance of educating school children about fire
safety.

        5.1      Ordinance No. 23-02 – An Ordinance of the City of Taylorsville approving a
                 Zoning Text Amendment to the Taylorsville Municipal Code Sections
                 05.06.040, 13.07.020, 13.08.020, 13.09.030, 13.10.020, 13.11.040, 13.26.080; and
                 Chapters 13.36 and 5.54, related to Home Occupation Standards – Jim Spung

       Senior Planner Jim Spung reminded the Council that they had asked him to review certain
aspects of this proposed ordinance, particularly with regard to home daycares. One change was
that the ordinance now allowed for daycares in townhomes as long as there was a private fenced
play area dedicated to that particular unit.

Mr. Spung presented a map showing the locations of current daycares in Taylorsville. He noted
they were fairly well-dispersed although there were six areas that would be non-compliant if not
for the fact that they would be grandfathered in.

The new version of the ordinance allowed a major arterial road to separate daycares that fell within
the 300’ limit.

Regarding on-site visits, the 24-trip maximum had been removed from the proposed ordinance.
Instead, the number of kids allowed at any time had been reduced from 12 to 8 children. Pick-up
and drop-off of children could not take place in the public right of way.

Councilmember Harker thanked Mr. Spung for reviewing the proposed ordinance and taking the
Council’s concerns into account.
