                                 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Originally founded in 1926 by the Utah Copper Company to support a small community of miners,
Copperton Metro Township has grown only moderately, in the past 100 years. The majority of the
township is included in the Copperton Historic District, which was added to the National Register of
Historic Places in 1986. Over time the system of storm drain pipes, catch basins and outfalls has
slowly deteriorated or undergone minor improvements to fix immediate concerns. To gain a better
understanding of Copperton’s current storm drain system Salt Lake County Public Works and
Engineering (County) retained Bowen Collins and Associates (BC&A) to develop a comprehensive
Storm Drain Master Plan for the Copperton Township. The intent of the Master Plan is to inventory
existing storm drain infrastructure, address the challenges facing the storm water drainage system,
and create a plan for future improvements to the system.

As part of this master planning effort, a storm water computer model was created. This allows the
County to better understand how the storm water system is working today, and how it might work
with future improvements. The model was used to simulate storm water runoff response to 10-year
and 100-year rainfall events. The model results suggest most of Copperton’s current drainage system
maintains adequate capacity during storm events. However, the County and local residents have
identified flooding concerns in specific areas in Copperton.

Based on these results and discussions with the County, BC&A developed a list of recommended
improvement projects. The improvements were evaluated in the model and a general cost estimate
was developed for each project. The prioritized list of recommended projects has been compiled into
a Capital Improvement Plan, or CIP, and can be found in Table 7.1 of Section 7.0 of this report. A map
showing the location of the recommended improvements can be found on Figure 7 of Appendix A.

As new development is constructed it is recommended that the master plan be used as a guide for
local storm drain improvements. New developments should be modeled and reviewed on a case by
case basis to ensure that new storm drains fit within the overall Master Plan. The storm drain model
should also be updated regularly as improvements are made and development occurs.

                                        SECTION 1
                                      INTRODUCTION

1.1 BACKGROUND AND INTRODUCTION
Over the past decade western Salt Lake County has experienced substantial growth and urbanization
with significant impacts to new and existing infrastructure. The evolution from a rural to urban
landscape has in particular, required effective planning and management of stormwater conveyance
and collection facilities. Although growth in the Copperton Township has been relatively slow, there
is a need for a comprehensive Storm Drain Master Plan for the area.

Copperton is a small township located in the southwestern portion of Salt Lake County at the mouth
of New Bingham Canyon, which is home to the Rio Tinto (formerly Kennecott) Copper Mine.
Historically a mining town, Copperton was built to support a small community of workers.

Today the Copperton Metro Township is a member of the Greater Salt Lake Municipal Services
District (MSD). The Salt Lake County Public Works and Engineering Department oversees the
maintenance, planning and development of infrastructure needs for the MSD. Storm drain planning,
design, and construction efforts are overseen by the Engineering Division, while maintenance and
operation of storm drainage facilities are conducted under the direction of the Operations Division.

The County and Copperton Township require a comprehensive storm drain master plan to inventory
existing infrastructure, identify and address storm drain deficiencies, and to plan for future
development.

1.2 OBJECTIVES
In February 2022 Salt Lake County retained Bowen Collins and Associates to develop a
comprehensive storm drain master plan for the Copperton Township with three primary objectives:
   •   Inventory and map existing stormwater drainage and collection facilities.
   •   Evaluate the adequacy of existing facilities to manage stormwater runoff for both existing and
       future conditions using an integrated hydraulic and hydrologic computer model.
   •   Develop a priority list of recommended storm drain and detention facility improvements.

1.3 SCOPE OF WORK
The scope of the Copperton Storm Water Master Plan includes:

Task 1 – Collect and Review Existing Information

BC&A collected and reviewed existing information and work pertaining to the Copperton storm drain
system (SDS), including:
   •   Topographic mapping, soil mapping and aerial photography
   •   Soil investigation and mapping for washes and surrounding areas
   •   GIS storm drain inventory data
   •   Previous storm drain related studies within the study area
   •   Transportation, zoning, and land use plans, including mining operations
   •   Demographic projections

Using a drainage basin map, developed by the County, and information from Tasks 1, 2 and 5, BC&A
developed a hydrologic model of the study area. The County delineated 27 subbasins to be included
in the hydrologic model. As part of the hydrologic model, BC&A developed a map of the existing and
projected land use conditions based on aerial photography and input from the County. A digital copy
of the ASSA model has been provided to the County.

Task 5 – Rainfall Hyetographs
BC&A established appropriate rainfall depths and design storm distributions for 10-year and 100-
year storm events. These were based on NOAA Atlas 14 and Farmer-Fletcher storm depths, durations
and distributions.

Task 6 – Perform System Modeling and Analyses
Using the combined hydraulic and hydrologic models BC&A estimated runoff and identified potential
SDS deficiencies. We worked closely with County operations staff to identify areas that experienced
past flooding or maintenance issues and to develop design and operating criteria for storm drain
facilities.

Task 7 – Identify Storm Drain System Improvements
Using the combined hydraulic and hydrologic model, BC&A identified storm drain improvements and
potential alternatives. We met with County personnel to review proposed improvements and ensure
their compatibility with existing system operations.

Task 8 – Capital Improvements Plan
BC&A developed a list of recommended SDS improvements needed to meet the desired level of
service using model results. Prioritization was based on the significance of deficiencies and input
from County staff. Conceptual cost estimates were developed for the prioritized improvements and
include permitting, materials, construction, engineering, and administrative costs.

                                    SECTION 2
                            DESCRIPTION OF STUDY AREA

2.1 LOCATION
The study area, as shown in Figure 1 of Appendix A, is located on the west side of Salt Lake Valley in
southwest Salt Lake County.

The area includes portions of the Copperton Township and the Bingham Canyon watershed in the
Oquirrh Mountains. The Copperton Township is located along the Bingham Highway from the mouth
of Bingham Canyon on the west, to Bingham City Cemetery located near the junction between New
Bingham Highway and Old Bingham Highway on the East. Bingham City Cemetery is included in the
township. Areas north of the highway are largely undeveloped, while a suburban residential
community is located south of the highway. Storm drain facilities evaluated in this study are bounded
by the residential community.

To evaluate the adequacy of Copperton’s SDS, contributing drainage areas north of Bingham Highway
were included or accounted for in the study. This includes portions of the Bingham Canyon watershed
in the Oquirrh Mountains. The watershed drains through Copperton from northwest to southeast.

2.2 ELEVATION AND CLIMATE
Study area elevations range from approximately 5,400 to 6,650 feet with most developed residential
areas within the 5,440 to 5,520 feet range.

The climate in the Salt Lake Valley is characterized as semi-arid, with annual mean relative humidity
of 55 percent (ranging from 22 to 79 percent). Average temperatures range from 59⁰ to 88⁰
Fahrenheit in the summer, 40⁰ to 65⁰ Fahrenheit in the spring and fall, and 22⁰ to 40⁰ Fahrenheit in
the winter. Average annual precipitation in the valley is approximately 15.5 inches, almost half of
which comes from snow.

2.3 NATIVE SOILS
Native soils were categorized based on the NRCS’s hydrologic soil groups. The NRCS classifies soils
into four groups (A, B, C and D) based on runoff potential. Group A soils have the lowest runoff
potential and Group D soils have the highest. Soils in the Copperton study area were either Group C
or Group D indicating high runoff potential. Group C soils consist of sandy clay loams and cover about
10% of the study area, predominantly in the high elevation foothills. Group D soils have low
infiltrations rates and consist of clay loams, silty clay loams, sandy clays or clays. Group D soils cover
about 90% of the study area.

2.4 LAND USE CONDITIONS
The study area covers 465 acres which encompasses portions of the Bingham Canyon watershed and
Copperton Township, including the developed area south of Bingham Highway. Overall, the study
area is approximately 15 percent developed. A majority of the developable area is built-out while the
remaining area consists of foothills property privately owned by Rio Tinto.

Within the developed portion of the study area, roughly 85 percent is residential development, with
15 percent commercial and light industrial areas.

2.5 EXISTING DRAINAGE PATTERNS
Natural slopes in the area range from approximately 5 to 13 percent downhill and run from
northwest to southeast of the study area. Storm water runoff generally follows the natural drainage
patterns characterized in Figure 2 of Appendix A.

Runoff from the foothills in the northern part of the study area flows south and collects along
Bingham Highway. From Bingham Highway water is either routed west to an outfall located along
the western border of the township, or east to a large culvert between Gemmell Club Drive and Carr
Fork Road. Flow in the culvert is piped to a main outfall located along the eastern border of the
township near Copperton Circle.

Runoff from the residential areas travel along road gutters to catch basins. Water collected in the
catch basins is routed through pipes to several discharge points along the southern borders of the
study area, including the main outfall near Copperton Circle. Ultimately discharged water either
infiltrates or flows to Bingham Creek. Bingham Creek is the study area’s major natural drainage.

2.5.1 Bingham Creek
Bingham Creek drains the Bingham Canyon watershed and is the major natural drainage channel to
which all runoff in the study area is routed. Due to mining operations all runoff from Bingham Canyon
is retained in evaporation ponds south of Copperton. The remainder of the Bingham Creek drainage
continues east through West Jordan City and ultimately drains to the Jordan River.

                                                               HYDROLOGIC ANALYSIS

4.1 INTRODUCTION
The hydrologic analysis evaluates runoff generation in response to precipitation. This section
provides an overview of the methods and results of the analysis.

4.2 PRECIPITATION
For the purposes of this study, the worst-case condition for Copperton’s storm drain system is
assumed to be a typical summer intense cloudburst event characterized by brief, high-intensity
rainfall. This assumption is consistent with previous studies for the Salt Lake Valley.

To mimic this type of rainstorm, 10-year and 100-year recurrence interval events were selected.
These events correspond to a 3-hour rainfall depth of 1.11 inches (10-year event) and 1.95 inches
(100-year event) over the entire study area, based on data from local rain gauge stations reported in
the NOAA Atlas 14.

The rate at which this rainfall depth falls over the duration of a 3-hour event is assumed to follow a
modified Farmer-Fletcher temporal distribution. This is illustrated in Figure 4.1.

4.3 Study Area Response to Design Event
During the design rainfall event several interactions occur between the water and study area.
Initially precipitation may be intercepted by vegetation and structures. Once reaching the ground
precipitation may drain into the soil, evaporate or form transient ponds. Eventually excess rainfall
flows over the landscape, accumulates into rills and flows to larger stormwater conveyance systems.
Hydrologic models aim to simulate these interactions and generate an estimated runoff volume and
flowrate from the design storm event in the study area. There are several methods and models
available, but for this study, the USACE HEC-1 method was used in combination with Autodesk Storm
and Sanitary Analysis (ASSA) software.

HEC-1 is a dynamic rainfall-runoff simulation model used to estimate runoff quantity by representing
the basin as an interconnection of smaller hydrologic and hydraulically analogous components (a.k.a.
subbasins). Subbasins receive precipitation and generate runoff. ASSA software routes runoff from
subbasins through a system of channels, pipes, and storage devices. ASSA tracks the quantity of runoff
generated within each subbasin, and the flow rate and flow depth through each open or closed
channel during the simulation period comprised of multiple time steps (HEC-1 Flood Hydrograph
Package User’s Manual v. 4.1, 1998).

A summary of the ASSA model inputs used to characterize the study area can be found in Appendix
B.

4.4 Drainage Basin Delineation
As part of a preliminary modeling effort Salt Lake County divided the 465 acre study area into 27
subbasins ranging in size from 0.7 to 46 acres. BC&A reviewed the subbasins delineations as part of
the modeling effort.

Subbasins represent the approximate area draining to a single outlet. Each subbasin receives
precipitation and generates runoff. Runoff is then collected at the outlet and routed through
conveyance facilities to downstream outlets and outfalls.

Basin delineation is based on topography, conveyance facilities (both natural and manmade) and
land development patterns. Topography and aerial imagery were obtained from the Salt Lake County
AGRC (aerial imagery dated 2019). Topography resolution is based on 0.5 meter LiDAR (1.64 feet,
2013).

Basin delineations are shown in Appendix A (Figure 4).

4.5 Hydrologic Model
Autodesk Storm and Sanitary Analysis 2022 (ASSA) Version 13.4 software was used to create a
computer based hydrologic model of Copperton’s storm water system.

Model parameters, shown in Appendix B, were entered into the computer model for each subbasin.
Using the HEC-1 method ASSA calculates the runoff from each subbasin in response to rainfall events.

Continuity error can generally be used to gauge the stability and reliability of a model’s results based
on inputs. The continuity error for this model is less than 0.01%. A continuity error greater than 10
percent is typically considered to indicate numerically unreliable results. Anything less than 5
percent indicates good model results based on the inputs.

4.6 Hydrologic Computer Model Calibration
Model calibration is the process of adjusting the initial model assumptions to generate results that
consistently match observed field conditions. Calibrating storm water models is challenging because
we often lack storm water flows and field observations that correspond to the modeled rainfall
events (e.g. 10-year rainfall event). Because runoff responses to rainfall is non-linear, smaller storm
events cannot typically be used to calibrate models for larger events.

For this study BC&A relied on previous studies in the Salt Lake Valley and input from the County to
calibrate the ASSA model. Model results were compared to results from previous studies, typical
runoff in local urban areas, and County feedback regarding areas of flooding concern. The final model

                                      SECTION 5
                                  HYDRAULIC ANALYSIS

5.1 INTRODUCTION
A hydraulic analysis was performed to evaluate how drainage infrastructure responds to runoff. This
section provides an overview of the methods and results of the hydraulic analysis.

5.2 HYDRAULIC MODEL CREATION
Storm water runoff from the study area is routed through a network of roadside gutters, open
channels, and underground pipes. Periodically, this runoff accumulates or ponds in local depressions
or in the streets. The intent of the hydraulic model is to mimic the flow conditions in this network
during the design rainfall event and identify areas that may need to be improved.

This model is limited to primary drainage corridors. The primary drainage corridors are comprised
of major overland flow paths, critical curb and gutters, and main storm drain culverts and trunklines.
Runoff from each subbasin is routed through these drainage corridors to provide an overview of how
the stormwater system responds.

Autodesk Storm and Sanitary Analysis 2022 (ASSA) Version 13.4 software was used to create a
computer based hydraulic model of Copperton’s storm water system. ASSA was used because it
allows the user to link the hydrologic and hydraulic models. A digital copy of this model has been
provided to the County for use in planning for and evaluating future developments.

5.3 MODELED PIPELINES
The model incorporated major storm drain pipes in Copperton. Storm drain pipes were incorporated
into the model where they played a critical role in conveying stormwater. The decision of which pipes
to include was based on the subbasin characteristics, field observations and input from County
personnel. The modeled trunklines included in the hydraulic model are shown in Figure 3, in
Appendix A.

Information on the physical characteristics of the pipes included in the model was determined
through field observations, and data from Salt Lake County, including GIS data and CCTV footage.
However, some pipes lacked available data, in which case we relied on reasonable assumptions of
the pipe material and size (e.g. known upstream and downstream pipe characteristics).

5.4 OVERLAND FLOW AND CURB AND GUTTERS
In the hydraulic model runoff from one subbasin often needs to be routed through one or more
downstream subbasins, until it reaches a pipe or an outfall. In reality this runoff is typically conveyed
through a pipe, a drainage channel or curb and gutter. Hydraulic characteristics of the open channels,
in the model, like roughness and width, were modified to mimic overland flow paths or curb gutters.

5.5 HYDRAULIC MODELING CALIBRATION
As more of the storm water system is surveyed and inventory, it is recommended that the hydraulic
model be updated with the best available data. This will improve the accuracy of the model in
reflecting actual field conditions.

                                      SECTION 6
                                  SYSTEM EVALUATION

6.1 HYDROLOGIC MODELING RESULTS
The hydrologic model was primarily used to estimate the total runoff and the runoff per acre in each
subbasin. Runoff in the developed residential area typically ranges from 0.4 to 0.7 cfs/acre during
the 10-year storm and from 0.8 to 1.3 cfs/acre during the 100-year storm. The range of runoff from
undeveloped foothills subbasins is minimal (0.01 cfs/acre) during the 10-year storm and ranges from
0.1 to 0.24 cfs/acre during the 100-year storm. Figures 5.A and 5.B, in Appendix A, show the
hydrologic modeling results, including runoff per acre during the 10-year and 100-year storms,
respectively.

Areas north of Bingham Highway, including the former site of Bingham High School, have been
identified as areas for potential future development. Based on similar residential development types
throughout Copperton, peak runoff rates in the areas are estimated to increase substantially. Runoff
from new development will be routed to catch basins along Bingham Highway. The additional runoff
from the former Bingham High School site will warrant installation of catch basins along Bingham
Highway and a piped connection to the existing pipeline along Park Street.

6.2 HYDRAULIC MODELING RESULTS
As runoff is routed through pipes in the model, water is allowed to pond on the ground surface when
flow exceeds the pipe’s capacity. As the pipe drains and capacity becomes available, the ponded water
is allowed to enter the piped system. The model reports pipe capacity at peak flow conditions and
the total volume of ponded water where flow exceeds capacity. The modeling results suggest that
there are no ponded areas and pipes maintained capacity during the modeled storm events. Figure
6.A and 6.B, in Appendix A, show the hydraulic modeling results for the 10-year and 100-year storms,
respectively. Results are shown as runoff as a percent of pipe capacity.

Stormwater pipes in developed areas are generally designed to carry the 10-year design storm.
During a 100-year storm runoff from developed areas therefore often exceeds pipe capacity. In these
cases runoff from developed areas is conveyed through both stormwater pipes and along roads. In
native foothills areas and large watersheds, the intent is to preserve native channels and storm drain
capacity sufficient to carry a 100-year design storm. In the case of Copperton, the modeled pipes
along Park Street and Apex Road were evaluated against the 10-year storm, while the modeled pipes
along Bingham Highway were evaluated against a 100-year storm. The pipes along Park Street and
Apex Road were assumed to primarily accommodate runoff from the surrounding developed areas.
The large culverts along Bingham Highway, however, were assumed to accommodate runoff from the
surrounding foothills. Based on these parameters, the modeling results suggest that the existing
pipes have adequate capacity for both the 10-year and 100-year storms.

Despite what the modeling results suggest, Copperton residents have reported flooding at the inlet
near 10261 Apex Road. At this location the existing stormwater pipes turn east and pass under
private homes towards the outfall near Copperton Circle. Since the modeling results suggest
adequate pipe capacity, flooding at this location likely occurs as a result of insufficient catch basin
inlet capacity.

                         SECTION 7
            RECOMMENDED CAPITAL IMPROVEMENTS PLAN

7.1 INTRODUCTION
The combined hydrologic and hydraulic model of the existing system and discussion with the County
were used to identify system deficiencies and develop a list of improvement objectives. The primary
improvement objectives are summarized as follows:
   •   Remove stormwater infrastructure from private property
   •   Mitigate known flooding near 10261 Apex Road
   •   Connect the Park Street pipeline to the sumps at the intersection of Park Street, Bingham
       Highway, and Dinkeyville Way
   •   Eliminate outfalls and improve intake structures along Hillcrest Street
   •   Plan for future development north of Bingham Highway.

Several improvement alternatives were developed based on these objectives. The hydraulic model
was used to simulate the improvement alternatives and develop a prioritized list of recommended
capital improvements.

Several factors were considered when prioritizing the recommended improvements, including the
impact and severity of potential flooding. Storm water improvements also have a tendency to move
problems downstream. For practical reasons, the downstream projects usually need to occur first in
order to provide the increased conveyance for later upstream improvements.

7.3 GENERAL GUIDELINES FOR NEW DEVELOPMENT
In the past, the County has established general guidelines for limitations on runoff from new
development. For example, regional studies such as the 2002 Southwest Canal and Creek Study
recommended limiting storm water discharge in areas of planned development to 0.2 cfs/acre, and
limiting storm water discharge in areas not anticipated for development to predevelopment runoff
levels. These regional studies are currently being updated. In general, study updates reflect that
development has been more dense and more expansive than anticipated 20 years ago, with a
decrease in average lot sizes and extension of development into foothill and other areas not
previously envisioned. As a result, cumulative peak runoff rates and volumes from developing areas
have generally been found to be higher than expected.

In addition, recommendations to limit runoff to predevelopment conditions are subject to
interpretation and often misconstrued to allow new development runoff significantly higher that
historic native conditions (e.g. by using the most recent land use, such as plowed farmland or poor
rangeland, as the predevelopment condition rather than the historic natural watershed condition).

Based on historic flow records for large watersheds in the Salt Lake Valley, native watershed runoff
rates are estimated to be in the range of 0.02 to 0.05 cfs/acre. This is a reasonable guideline to
compare with estimates of predeveloped runoff conditions, and a reasonable guideline for general
runoff limitations. It should be noted that runoff limitations should apply to both 10-year and 100-
year events.

In general the direction of the State of Utah, who administers the Utah Pollutant Discharge
Elimination System (with primacy granted by the U.S. EPA), is increasingly to limit runoff peak
discharge, volume and water quality to predevelopment (i.e. historic native watershed) conditions.
The intent of this is to limit negative impacts to downstream streams, lakes and rivers. In keeping
with this, drainage from new development in Magna should consider peak discharge, volume and
water quality associated with storm water runoff.

7.4 MASTER PLAN LIMITATIONS
The master planning process is used to develop an overall plan and recommendations for general
storm drain pipe sizes, locations, and associated conceptual level construction cost estimates. The
estimated design flow and pipe diameters were developed from computer models that should be
refined with detailed analyses and data as it becomes available during the detailed design process.
This master plan was developed based on common assumptions and standard design criteria to
ensure uniformity in the recommended improvements and associated cost estimates. This master
plan does not include details such as exact alignment, design slopes, and pipe depths; utility conflicts;
and permitting requirements. During the design phase of the recommended improvements,
additional detailed hydrologic and hydraulic analysis should be performed to identify the final pipe
sizes, flow rates, and slopes of the proposed storm drain pipes. A pre-design report that documents
the recommended pipe sizes, design flow rates, model results, detailed cost estimate, and addresses
other pertinent design questions should also be prepared prior to design and construction to refine
the general recommendations made in the master plan documents.

                                        SECTION 8
                                       CONCLUSION

Copperton is a vibrant community with a rich mining history, but while the community has renewed,
their storm drain infrastructure has gradually aged. Aging infrastructure can lead to costly damage,
tedious maintenance and create health and safety hazards.

The purpose of this Storm Water Master Plan was to evaluate Copperton’s existing storm water
infrastructure and develop a list of recommended improvements to maintain and improve the
functionality of the local storm drain system. The recommendations provided in this master plan can
serve as road map to improving Copperton’s storm water drainage over time as resources and
opportunities become available. Investing in these improvements protects the community from
growing issues, that are likely to become even more costly if neglected, and promotes a clean and
safe neighborhood with a better quality of life for those living and working in Copperton.
