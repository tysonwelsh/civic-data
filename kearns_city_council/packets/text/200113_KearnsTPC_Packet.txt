                                             PROJECT DESCRIPTION
The applicant is requesting approval to add 4 additional units in multifamily dwellings to the existing apartment complex,
Carrington Square. The entire Carrington Square Apartment complex currently has 256 units entirely within multi-family
dwellings. Carrington Square sits on approximately 15.07 acres and has a current average density of approximately 17 units
per acre across three phases. Following the addition of the proposed 47 units, the density would be almost 20 units per
acre on average (with Phase 1 approaching 25 units per acre). The proposed units are only in Phase 1 and Phase 3, which
are already developed and dense for the site.

                                                      SUMMARY
History:
          Bach Homes first made application for the proposed Carrington Square apartment complex in December of 1994.
At that time, Bach Homes proposed 168 units. In February of 1995, over 371 residents signed a petition in opposition to the
apartment complex being developed. Much of the opposition to the project was due to concerns about potential impacts to
the traffic and parking issues in that area. At that time, Cougar Lane held the Oquirrh Park, single-family homes and the
Kearns High School and no high-density development. The residents in the area felt that the traffic between 5415 South and
6200 South along Cougar lane was already congested and the traffic would be intolerable if the apartment complex with that
many proposed units were approved. There was also concern that this area was a fox habitat that needed to be preserved.
The County Commission ultimately approved Phase 1 in 1996 but reduced the number of units to 144 units.
          In January of 1998, the General Plan for that area was amended to allow for high density residential and office uses.
At the same time, Bach Homes made an application to rezone the middle section of the parcel from M-1 (Manufacturing) to
R-M. Bach Homes also made application for a phase two consisting of 120 apartment units. When the rezone application
went to the Planning Commission, the Planning Commission voted to approve the zoning as an R-M/zc (High density
Residential with zoning conditions). One of the zoning conditions placed on the zone change was a density restriction of 15
units per acre, affecting only Phase 2. Due to this condition the approval for Phase 2 dropped to 96 units across 6.46 acres,
or 14.87 units per acre. Overall, the current density for the entire site is 14.6 units per acre.

          The applicant came back in early 2000 for a third phase consisting of two buildings in the southern area of the
property, giving the applicant an additional 16 units in the complex sitting on just over one acre. The zoning on that area of
the parcel is C-2 (Commercial).
          On July 23, 2019, Applicant filed a conditional use application to construct a number of new buildings within the site.
After various discussions with staff, Applicant proposed a revised site plan in that proposed adding 38 units to Phase 1 and 9
units to Phase 3. The new buildings consisted of three four-family buildings, one two-family buildings, and two multi-family
buildings. On November 18, 2019, the Planning Commission held a public hearing on the application. In preparation of the
hearing, staff issued a report expressing the staff’s opinion that the Kearns Metro Township Code (the “Code”) required the
Planning Commission to deny the application because: (1) the proposed density for the project exceeded the maximum
density limits set forth in Sections 19.44.110 and 19.62.130 of the Code for the two-family and four-family units and staff did
not believe the Code provided an exception that adjusts the density requirements for developments that involve different
types of dwellings; and (2) the proposed density was not appropriate for the site in light of the general plan and other
considerations. At the hearing, applicant requested a continuance to provide further time to determine which entity within
Kearns is responsible for making density determinations and for hearing appeals of density determinations. On December 4,
2019, Nathan Bracken, counsel for the Kearns Metro Township, issued a letter to the Planning Commission and applicant’s
attorney finding that the Planning Commission is responsible for making density determinations under Sections 19.44.110
and 19.62.130 of the Code and that Kearns’ hearing officer is responsible for hearing appeals of such determinations.
          On December 17, 2019, applicant submitted a revised site plan that converted the proposed structures into four
three-story multi-family dwellings; namely: (1) building A with six two-bedroom units; (2) building C with 24 units, including
20 two-bedroom units and four one-bedroom units; (3) building D with ten two-bedroom units; and (4) building F with seven
two-bedroom units. As proposed, the revised plans would add 40 new units to phase 1 of the site, increasing the number of
units to 184 and raising the ratio of units per acre from 19.12 to 24.44 – a 27.96% increase above current levels. The seven
new units proposed in phase 3 as part of building F would increase the number of units to 23 and raise ratio of units per acre
from 14.67 to 21.10 – a 43.83% increase above current levels. For the site as a whole density would increase by 18.4%.

Buildings C and F contain most of the proposed increased density and would remove a significant portion of the remaining
open space on the site.

Staff Recommendation:
          As discussed in greater detail below, the application proposes a significant increase in the density of the Carrington
Square project. The revised site plan complies with the 25.0 unit per acre maximum density limits set forth in Sections
19.44.110 and 19.62.130 of the Code for multi-family units. The maximum possible density is not the same as the “allowable”
density for a given site, however, which staff believe the Planning Commission should determine on a case by case basis
under Section 19.44.110 and 19.62.130. Staff further believe that a case-by-case analysis of the site shows that the maximum
density of 25.0 units per acre is not appropriate for this site and that allowable density should be closer to current density
levels. For these and other reasons discussed below, staff recommends that the Planning Commission deny the application
as proposed. Staff would, however, recommend approval of the project if the site plan were modified to remove buildings C
and F. Without buildings C and F, the per unit density of the site would increase slightly from 14.6 to 15.6. – a 7% increase.

                                          GENERAL PLAN CONSIDERATIONS
         As noted above, Sections 19.44.110 and 19.62.130 of the Code require the Planning Commission to consider the
General Plan when determining the “allowable density” for the site. The current General Plan was adopted on August 11,
2009, after all three phases of the site were developed. Chapter 4 of the General Plan includes a map that is intended to
serve as a guide to areas of anticipated and desired stability or growth absorption and includes various colors that indicate a
range in the level of stability and intensity of activity within the Township. The map depicts the site in a blue color. A “blue
area” is an area that has “limited potential for the absorption of growth and is likely to experience only minor changes in
overall character over time.” The level of stability of the blue area is defined as follows:
         1- Subtle changes in land use may occur. Overall, land uses in the area/corridor will exhibit less diversity and less
              intensity. Changes will be limited to a small number of dispersed sites, leaving the majority of the area/corridor
              unchanged.
         2- Improvements may occur which subtly alter the appearance, economics, or sustainability of the area/corridor.
              Most improvements will consist of individual projects and may not require coordination with parcels beyond
              their immediate vicinity.
         3- Mobility networks are less formalized and will remain largely as built, but minor changes may occur. Public
              transit typically will have no dedicated right of way. 2

                                         PLANNING STAFF ANALYSIS

Staff makes the following findings regarding the application:
     • Proposed buildings A, C, D and F are within the maximum allowable density for multi-family dwellings, but the case-
        by-case analysis required by Code Sections 19.44.110 and 19.62.130 show that the maximum limits are not suitable
        for the Property.
     • The Carrington Square Apartment Complex currently has adequate recreational facilities for the existing density.
     • The proposed 45 additional units will result in buildings replacing a significant amount of the existing open space in
        phase one and phase three.
     • The traffic along Cougar Lane, 5415 So., and 6200 So. is likely to increase due to the proposed density contemplated
        in the application.
     • The accessibility to the buildings by the fire department has been questionable.
     • If the proposed additional density was reduced by excluding some buildings, particularly buildings C and F, a
        reduction to the anticipated traffic impacts on the surrounding roadways may occur.
     • Applicant is only showing a 45% open space amount. The long-standing policy that has guided open space
        determinations since 2000 requires 50% for a dwelling group.
     • A proposal excluding buildings C and F, however, would minimize the increase in density and the then-proposed
        density would be close to 7%, which (1) may be considered “subtle” for purposes of the General Plan and (2) would
        retain sufficient open space to satisfy open space requirements.

        The application, as proposed, requests a density that is inappropriate under the case-by-case analysis required in
the Kearns Metro Township Code. Pursuant to Sections 19.44.110 and 19.62.130, the staff believes the Planning Commission
should deny the application on this basis.

         Under Code Sections 19.44.110 and 19.62.130, the “allowable” density is not necessarily the same as the maximum
allowable limits that are listed. Instead, the “allowable” density should be determined on a case-by-case basis as described
in those Sections. Here, the maximum allowable densities in Code Sections 18.44.110 and 19.62.130 are not appropriate as
allowable density for the site given the findings of the Fire Department and the transportation engineers, the loss in open
space, and the requirements of the General Plan, which only contemplates a “subtle change in land use.” 3

         More specifically, the proposed density increase is inconsistent with the General Plan, consideration of which is an
express consideration of Sections 19.44.110 and 19.62.130. Staff believe that a change in average density of approximately
18.4% for the site as a whole and 27.96% and 43.83% increases for phase 1 and phase 3 do not qualify as “subtle changes in
land use” as stated in the General Plan. Mitigation of the associated traffic impacts may also “require coordination with
parcels beyond (the project’s) immediate vicinity,” which is also inconsistent with the General Plan. In short, even though
each of the proposed dwellings qualifies as a multi-family dwelling with a maximum allowable density of 25.0 per acre, the
case-by-case analysis required by Sections 19.44.110 and 19.62.130 shows that the allowable density limits are not
appropriate for this site and the proposed increased densities are inconsistent with the General Plan.

         Additionally, the site is already developed, and it is possible that the current density of the site, or a level close
thereto, may be the maximum “allowable” limit. The density for each phase of this property was considered when each phase
was developed. These prior approvals authorized densities that were far less than the maximum allowable limits or the
density levels applicant is requesting now. As noted previously, Bach Homes originally sought 168 units for Phase 1, which
the County Council reduced to 144 due to public outcry over potential impacts. The current proposal would increase the
density in Phase 1 to 184 unit – 40 more than current levels and 16 more than Bach Homes originally proposed for Phase 1
in 1994. Moreover, with the proposal of buildings C and F, much of the open space in Carrington Square will be taken out.
Long-standing policy also sets open space requirements for dwelling groups is 50%. With buildings C and F it will be at 45%.

        Staff notes, however, that removing buildings C and F from the site plan would only increase the unit per acre density
by 7%, which staff believes would qualify a “subtle” increase under the General Plan.

          The “allowable” density analysis that Code Sections 19.62.130 and 19.44.110 require is a separate threshold analysis
that is separate and apart from the conditional use process. Nevertheless, if the Planning Commission were to approve the
application as proposed, staff believe the following impacts will need to be mitigated through reasonable conditions.
Importantly, Section 19.84.060 states that Planning Commission should find that any development of the site conforms to
these standards “prior to approval.”

          1.   Section 19.84.060(C) states: “The proposed use and site development plan shall not present a serious traffic hazard
               due to poor site design or to anticipated traffic increases on the nearby road system which exceed the amounts
               called for under the county transportation master plan.” The applicant has not offered a reasonable or
               advantageous recommendation to help elevate the traffic congestion on Cougar Lane and the connecting roadways.

          2.   Section 19.84.060(D & E) state: “The proposed use and site development plan shall not pose a serious threat to the
               safety of persons who will work on, reside on, or visit the property nor pose a serious threat to the safety of residents
               or properties in the vicinity by failure to adequately address the following issues: fire safety….” The proposed use
               and site development plan shall not adversely impact properties in the vicinity of the site through lack of
               compatibility with nearby buildings in terms of size, scale, height, or noncompliance with community general plan
               standards.” The Fire Department has stated that the proposed Building “C”’s location with its proximity to buildings
               3,4, and 5 and without direct emergency access presents serious life safety issues. The applicant has not provided
               Planning Staff or the Fire Department building elevations to show the proposed height. The applicant has only
               provided approximate heights of the building eaves. And therefore, the fire department has only been able to give
               a conceptual acceptance of the applicant’s proposal. As the fire department has noted, it cannot commit to giving

Definitions:
   • Recreation Facility: A centrally located place designed and equipped for the conduct of sports,
        informal recreation and/or leisure-time activities. It provides areas for aerobic exercises, game
        courts, swimming, etc. The number and type of facilities should be chosen to anticipate tenant mix.

    •    Active Recreation: Activities of a formal nature and often performed with others, requiring
         equipment and taking place at prescribed places and sites.

    •    Passive Recreation: Activities that involve inactive or less energetic activities, such as walking,
         sitting and picnicking, etc. These activities have less potential impact on surrounding land uses.

    Active and passive areas should be equally distributed throughout the site.


Minimum Requirements and Adjustments:
   Any development containing 10 or more bedrooms shall comply with the Recreation Facility and Open
   Space Standards. There are three parts to the recreation facility standards that must be met by every
   development. They are:

         1.   A playground of not less than 1,000 sq. ft. (See Recreation Facility List)

         2. An additional number of recreation facilities are based on number of bedrooms. The total
            number is determined by using the Recreation Facility Table.

         3. Each recreation facility shall meet the minimum size requirement shown below.

    When a proposed recreation facility exceeds the required minimum square footage related any of the
    above standards by 1.5, 2, 2.5 times, etc. It may be counted as that number of facilities.

        As you know, under Utah law, a local government is bound by the rules, requirements
and standards of its land use ordinances. Utah Code Ann. § 17-27a-509(2)(1)(a)(i) (“An
applicant . . . is entitled to substantive land use review of the land use application, under the land
use laws in effect on the date that the application is complete. . . .”) (Emphasis added.) “An
applicant is entitled to approval of a land use application if the application conforms to the
requirements of the municipality’s land use maps, zoning maps, a municipal specification for
public improvements applicable to a subdivision or development, and an applicable land use
ordinance in effect when a complete application is submitted . . . .” Utah Code Ann. § 17-27a-
509(1)(a)(ii). A county may adopt a land use ordinance permitting conditional uses of property,
however, the standards for compliance must be set forth in the applicable ordinance. Utah Code
Ann. § 17-27a-507(1). In sum, CLUDMA provides that land use applications are to be reviewed
under the municipality’s adopted and effective laws as contained in its land use ordinances.
None of these sections mentions any required compliance with non-legislative “policies”
supposedly imposed by unelected planning commissions.

       It is clear that “a legislative function cannot be properly delegated to an executive body.”
Salt Lake County Cottonwood Sanitary Dist. v. Sandy City, 879 P.2d 1379, 1382 (Utah Ct. App.
1994) (citing Sandy City v. Salt Lake County, 827 P.2d 212 (Utah 1992)); See Mistretta v. U.S.,
488 U.S. 361 (1989) (“The delegation of lawmaking authority to the Commission is, in short,
unsupported by any legitimate theory to explain why it is not a delegation of legislative power.
To disregard structural legitimacy is wrong in itself - but since structure has purpose, the
disregard also has adverse practical consequences . . . the consequence is to facilitate and
encourage judicially uncontrollable delegation.”) The same logic would hold true regarding any
attempted legislative delegation to an appointed body with statutorily limited powers such as the
Kearns Planning Commission.

        Development standards, guidelines or policies created by a county or municipal planning
commission are not governing law unless and until adopted by the government’s legislative body
and incorporated into an ordinance. See Gibbons v. Town of Vincent, 124 So. 3d 723, 732 (Ala.
2012) (“a planning commission is an advisory body” and the legislative body has the sole
authority to create rules on re-zoning property). In The City of St. Petersburg v. Schweitzer, 297
So.2d 74 (Fla. App. 2 Dist. 1974), the Planning Commission denied a site plan and application
for a building permit for a seventy-two unit apartment complex as a “special use exception”
based not upon requirements of the governing ordinance, but upon requirements of the Planning
Commission’s self-created policies. The Court rejected the City’s argument that the
Commission-created policies provided the Commission with proper guidelines to perform its
delegated administrative authority in approving or denying land use applications. The Court held
that guidelines used by the administrative authority “must be established by the legislative
authority in the ordinance itself.” Id. at 76. Allowing the Commission to promulgate the rules
which it then applies to grant special exceptions would be an unlawful delegation of legislative

authority. Id.

       CLUDMA, in Subsection 506(1), allows a county to condition an approval based on
compliance with “applicable ordinances”. CLUDMA does not allow a county to impose
conditions on a conditional use based on non-legislative “policies”. Similarly, Section
19.84.040.A of the Kearns Zoning Code requires proposed conditional uses “comply with all
applicable ordinances and codes.” Compliance with mere non-legislative “policies” is not a
requirement. Nor could it legally be so under CLUDMA.

        The Office of the Private Property Rights Ombudsman has opined that not even the
“policy” section of a lawfully zoning ordinance dealing with a conditional use can trump the
standards for the use expressed in the substantive provisions of a city’s zoning code. Advisory
Opinion No. 164, Pleasant View City/Horizon Development, October 26, 2015. Opinion No. 64
relied upon a holding of the Utah Supreme Court in Price Development Co. v Orem City, 2000
UT 26 ¶ 23, 995 P.2d 1237:

                 We have referred to a statement of legislative purpose as a
                 "preamble" to the operative provisions of a statute. See De Baritault
                 v. Salt Lake City Corp., 913 P.2d 743, 745 (Utah 1996); Crawford
                 v. Tilley, 780 P.2d 1248, 1250 (Utah 1989). And we have held that
                 as such, a preamble is nothing more than a statement of policy which
                 confers no substantive rights. See Westley v. Board of City
                 Comm'rs, 573 P.2d 1279, 1280 (Utah 1978). In Westley, section 34-
                 19-1 of the Code, a section within the Labor Disputes Act, was
                 described as a "declaration of policy." Id. We held that this
                 declaration did not "constitute an enlargement of authority or power
                 in excess of that contained in succeeding sections." Id. While some
                 statutes have a policy section and some have a preamble, the effect
                 to be given these provisions is the same: they provide guidance to
                 the reader as to how the act should be enforced and interpreted, but
                 they are not a substantive part of the statute. See Norman J. Singer,
                 Sutherland Statutory Construction §§ 20.03, 20.12 (5th ed. 1993).
                 They may be used to clarify ambiguities, but they do not create
                 rights that are not found within the statute, nor do they limit those
                 actually given by the legislation. See id.; De Baritault, 913 P.2d at
                 747.

        If a legislatively adopted “policy” stated in an ordinance or statute is not a “substantive
part of the statute”, “confers no substantive rights”, does not “create rights” and does not “limit
[those rights] actually given by the legislation” it is obvious beyond dispute that a Policy of the
Township Planning Commission that substantially alters the zoning of the Project and
significantly damages its economic value has no more weight in the consideration of the
Project’s CUP than the paper it is written on or the weightless electrons in the electronic version.

                                     GENERAL PLAN CONSIDERATIONS
The Kearns Township General Plan, adopted on August 11, 2009, identifies this property in blue. A “blue area” is one that
has limited potential for the absorption of growth and is likely to experience only minor changes in overall character over
time. The level of stability of the Blue area is defined as follows:
         1- Subtle changes in land use may occur. Overall, land uses in the area/corridor will exhibit less diversity and less
             intensity. Changes will be limited to a small number of dispersed sites, leaving the majority of the
             area/corridor unchanged.
         2- Improvements may occur which subtly alter the appearance, economics, or sustainability of the area/corridor.
             Most improvements will consist of individual projects and may not require coordination with parcels beyond
             their immediate vicinity.
         3- Mobility networks are less formalized and will remain largely as built, but minor changes may occur. Public
             transit typically will have no dedicated right of way.




                           ISSUES OF CONCERN/PROPOSED MITIGATION
A major issue of concern is that the construction of a second single-family dwelling on a single lot is a substantial change to
the neighborhood. It is not a subtle change or a small improvement, as defined in the Kearns General Plan. The lot is 0.17
acres, and two single-family dwellings would substantially increase the density of this small lot. There is no precedent in
this neighborhood for the addition of a second single-family dwelling on this lot.

A second issue of concern is that the second single-family dwelling has multiple building code violations that involve the
health, safety, and welfare of the residents. Primarily the fire wall between the two units is not up to code requirements.
