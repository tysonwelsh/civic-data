        A.1     Issue:        DAYBREAK VILLIAGE 3 MULTI FAMILY #1, PRELIMINARY PLAT

                Location: 4680 W. Vermillion Drive
                File No:   PLPP201900673
                Applicant: Daybreak Communities

City Planner Greg Schindler reviewed the background information on this item from the Planning Packet
Staff Report. Daybreak Communities has filed an application for preliminary subdivision plat approval
for the Village 3 Multi Family #1 subdivision. The proposed subdivision will divide the property into 24
townhome lots, 1 park lot (P-lot) and associated private right-of-way. This parcel, recorded as lot C-102
(civic or commercial lot) in the Kennecott Daybreak Plat 9 subdivision, was originally intended to be
developed as a small non-denominational church or a small reception center. After over 10 years of
seeing little or no interest in this type of development for the property, Daybreak Communities has
decided to subdivide the lot for residential use. On February 4, 2020, Daybreak Communities held a
“neighborhood informational meeting” regarding this proposed project. The proposed townhomes will be
three to four stories (approx. 37 ft. – 47 ft.) in height. Each unit will include a 2-car garage. The
residential density of this proposal is 20.2 units per acre (gross density) and 24.0 units per acre (net
density), which is consistent with the P-C zone and adopted Community
Structure Plan for Daybreak. The proposed lot sizes range from 1,080 sq. ft. to 3,868 sq. ft. with an
average lot size of 1,811 sq. ft. WCEC Engineers has completed a traffic assessment for this proposed
subdivision (see memo attached). Their findings indicate, “The projected traffic volumes generated by the
proposed development are minimal and well within the master plan expectations and will be
easily accommodated by the roadway network and associated on-site improvements.” The PC zone
provides for the approval of design guidelines developed for a specific subdivision or site plan. The
proposed design guidelines, specific to this plat are attached for review.

Gary Langston, Daybreak Communities, he said I will briefly go over our process when considering
these developments. He talked about the Master Plan and multiple other Master Agreements that was
approved by the city back in 2003-2004. These are the foundation for all we do in Daybreak. He said this
project is found in Plat 9. As Greg mentioned, within the Community Structure Plan, this falls within an
area designated as Town. This allows us to do a variety of things in the area. We could put commercial
uses in the area, it could be office or retail, or residential uses of any type up to 50 units per acre. When
Plat 9 was created it was our desire to have a small commercial use, ideally a nondenominational church,
a reception center or a something along the lines of a daycare. In November 2007 we recorded the final
Plat for Plat 9 and have spent the last 12 years working with a variety of different groups to understand
potential uses of the property. We as Daybreak Communities believe it is time to finish the development
in the area. We have decided to proceed with a different development plan for the property and the
proposed use that we have submitted to the city falls within the Town designation in the Community
Structure Plan. He said each time we begin a project we start with an evaluation and there are many items
we consider and evaluate as we pull together our final design and proposal that goes to the city. We
develop our plan and develop the design drawings that are given to the City Engineering and Planning
Departments for approval. We have received resident feedback on this project in a variety of different
forms. Many of the questions posed by them are some of the exact same questions we asked ourselves.
Some of the questions have to do with the following topics – parking, traffic impacts, trash can location,
impacts on schools, architecture, site plan function, density and scale of the project, and ultimately how
does it fit within the Master Plan that we have for Daybreak. From a parking perspective, we are required
to have 1 parking stall per unit. Each of the units have a two car garage. We are also assuming there
would be on street parking available for residents and their guests. Traffic impacts, we had the same
question about what the impact of this project potentially would be on the area. We solicited a traffic
engineer to do an analysis for us and that was included in your packet. The peak am and pm volumes for
this project range from 11-13 trips per hour on each of the two lanes. The total number of trips for the
project is approximately 176 and that is using the ITE Manual to do an estimate on that. If we were to put
commercial uses in the area it would likely generate much more traffic than the townhomes we are
proposing. When we originally designed Plat 9 there were 587 lots in the development. With the Plat
amendments that we have made over the last several years and including the 24 units we are proposing,
there are a total of 585 units currently with the proposed developments. The total unit count has remained
the same from the first plat that we recorded 12-13 years ago. The overall traffic impact is the same or
less as compared to the original approval and a copy of that traffic study is in your packet. He said we
recognize there were comments about trash cans. We believe there is adequate space to place 24 cans on
each side with approximately 12 cans being north of the lane approach and 12 cans on the south side of
the lane approach. We have provided a garbage can plan to the City Engineering and Public Works
Departments. He said schools are important to us. We have spent a lot of time and effort working with our
partners at the Jordan School District to make sure that they understand not only our forecasted
developments projections for our project but also what we actually put in the ground. Members of our
team on average meet with Jordan School District about once per quarter to provide updates to them for
what we are proposing to do in Daybreak.

Commissioner Morrissey said for this type of project what type of demographics are we going to see as
far as student impact right away.

Mr. Langston said we don’t know for sure. Our belief is that we will have a cross section that would have
anywhere from single home owners who may not have any children, to potentially empty nesters who
want to buy a unit that is low to zero maintenance and we expect there will be some families somewhere
in between. That would be typical of our townhome developments.

    3.   Environmental Issues. Plat 9 of Daybreak lies in the far northeast corner of what was known as the
         “Kennecott Utah Copper South Jordan Evaporation Pond” area. Historically, these evaporation ponds were
         used to store and evaporate storm water runoff, lime treated waters, and mine waters originating from the
         Bingham Canyon Copper Mine and the surrounding watershed. In 1989, Kennecott Utah Copper was
         purchased by Rio Tinto, a worldwide leader in the mining industry. Following completion of certain
         studies, clean-up activities for the ponds began in June 1994 and were completed in late 1994 pursuant to a
         plan approved by the Environmental Protection Agency. At that time the “ponds” were dry and consisted
         of settled sediment. As part of the clean-up, Kennecott Utah Copper removed some of the pond and
         associated canal sediments to an off-site repository and consolidated the remaining material into a “North”
         consolidated area and a “South” consolidated area. After this consolidation was completed, the entire
         South Jordan Evaporation Pond area was capped with between 1 and 5 feet of fill material and 10 inches of
         topsoil. Upon its own initiative, Master Developer has moved the consolidated sediments from the North
         and South consolidated areas to an off-site repository.

         Approximately the eastern quarter of Plat 9 previously contained evaporation ponds, which were used to
         store pond sediments and treated sludge prior to the clean-up efforts detailed above. A feeder canal to the
         ponds crossed the southwest corner of Plat 9. The rest of Plat 9 consisted of areas were not used as
         evaporation ponds or canals.

         The 1994 South Jordan Evaporation Pond consolidation activities, which affected Plat 9, were performed
         pursuant to an EPA approved plan. Upon completion of the clean-up project, in 2001 the EPA issued a
         Record of Decision that recognized the proposed future use of the area for construction of a planned
         community with low and high density residential, commercial and industrial development. The Record of
         Decision stated that no further action at the ponds was needed or required. Testing conducted on the
         remaining site soils after the pond sediments were removed indicated that clean up efforts surpassed EPA-
         approved project clean-up goals with respect to concentrations of lead and arsenic, with the exception of one
         sample that was slightly above the approved clean-up goal with respect to arsenic only (the lead level was
         below the clean-up goal). The area of the sample that was slightly above the clean-up goal for arsenic was
         subsequently further tested and a second sample above the clean-up goal was detected. Approximately 1600
         cubic yards of soil in the vicinity of this sample were removed and disposed of at an off-site location.
         Sampling done after this soil removal showed arsenic concentrations below the clean-up goal. Testing
         therefore indicates that concentrations of lead and arsenic are within commonly established clean-up levels
         for residential settings in the Salt Lake Valley.

         Additionally, elevated concentrations of sulfate were identified in pond sediments within Plat 9 prior to
         clean-up efforts. Sulfates, lead and arsenic, and other metals migrated into the groundwater lying 100 feet to
         300 feet beneath portions of Daybreak. While such sulfate, lead, arsenic and other metals in the groundwater
         may render the underground water undrinkable, they do not pose a health or safety concern or threat to
         individuals who may work, live, or recreate in Daybreak. Kennecott Utah Copper, the State of Utah, and the
         Environmental Protection Agency have proposed a plan to pump and treat groundwater from the impacted
         area. The treated water could potentially be used as a drinking water source and for other purposes. This
         plan is currently under consideration by state and federal agencies.

         Due to the presence of elevated sulfate concentrations, some of the soils may be corrosive and/or conductive,
