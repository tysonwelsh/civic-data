A. Discuss Policy Ranking
Matt Dahl said in Section 2.12.050 of the Midvale Municipal Code states that, “The city
council establishes policy through broad general policy directives and general task
assignments of a goal-oriented nature.” The methods by which the city council establishes
the policies of the City are varied, but typically they include the adoption of ordinances,
plans, and budgets, as well as the approval of specific projects, programs, and initiatives.
Once adopted, it is the administration’s role to implement the policies. Some policies, like
those established in City plans, require the regulatory authority of an ordinance or
allocation of funding in order to be implemented.
The policies considered by the city council originate from a variety of sources, including:
e Federal and State Legislation/Rules
e Coordination with Other Government Entities
e City Adopted Plans
e City Council Identified Issues
e City Administration Identified Issues

e Community Identified Issues
The total number of policies that warrant consideration is substantial. While federal and |
state mandates may require timely action or an issue in the community may warrant
immediate attention, many policies are not addressed due to the staff resources available
to pursue them. Often new policies require public input, in-depth research, legal review
and drafting, and an iterative review by the city council. In the case of land use policies,
a strict procedure for modifications is dictated by state code. These processes take a
minimum of one month, but in most cases the process takes several months. The timing
is determined by the complexity, impact, or sensitive nature of the policy.
Given that the number of policies warranting attention exceeds the time and resources
available to immediately address them, staff is proposing that the city council consider
establishing a priority list that provides staff with guidance on which policies to work on.
He asked the Council the following questions:

A. PUBLIC HEARING TO CONSIDER MAIN STREET FORM BASED CODE
TEXT AMENDMENT AND REZONE
Alex Murphy said following the City Council's adoption of the Main Street Small Area Plan,
City Staff has been working with the consultant, Planning Commission, City Council, and
property owners in the Main Street neighborhood to develop the Main Street Form-Based
Code (“FBC”). The proposed FBC would repeal and replace the City’s existing Historic
Commercial zone with the Main Street FBC zone. The FBC zone would then be applied
to properties within the Historic Commercial (HC), Regional Commercial (RC), Medium
Density Multifamily Residential (RM-12), and Single-Family Residential (SF-2) zones, as
identified on the Form-Based Code Boundary Map in Figure 1. A larger version of this
map can be found within the attached FBC draft.

The City’s Municipal Code and State Code establish procedural requirements for text
amendments and rezones initiated by the City. Beginning in August, Staff held
discussions, workshops, and other meetings with the Planning Commission, City Council,
and property owners in the affected area.

The first public hearing on the FBC was held by the Planning Commission in November
and was preceded by an open house, held at City Hall. Property owners affected by the
rezone were mailed notice in advance, describing impacts the proposed rezone would
have on their property, and additional notices were posted in the newspaper, online,
around the neighborhood, and mailed out to other property owners within 300’ of the
rezone area. The Planning Commission tabled the item and directed Staff to address a
list of items, before issuing a positive recommendation to adopt the FBC to the Council in
their December 2019 meeting.

surplus City property, with analysis from all City departments, notice to affected property

L not finalized.

A Better Neighborhood Cleanup

After a year and half of trying a monthly bulky waste, curbside pick-up program, Midvale
is ready for an upgraded bulky waste program to better meet the needs of all City
residents. Our goal in the redesign is to create a program that offers an equitable,
accessible and convenient bulk waste collection service for all residents.

While the current curbside program serves a few, it is not meeting our environmental or
community needs. Some issues with the current program include the following:

The guidelines on accepted items for collection, as well as the 2-day set-out time, are
often violated. This means that unsightly piles routinely sit and grow on streets for days
and sometimes weeks. They contain loose trash of all types, electronic and household
hazardous waste, organic waste, and more.

_ These piles of trash on the street are not a best practice for the environmental
stewardship of our local water bodies, including the Jordan River and Great Salt Lake,
because those items have the potential to wash directly into the stormwater system.
Regarding green waste in particular: The Jordan River is classified as “impaired” under
the federal Clean Water Act for several pollutants, including Low Dissolved Oxygen
(DO). Low DO results from sediment (soil and dirt) and organic matter (grass clippings,
leaves, etc.) making their way into the Jordan River from area stormwater. As this
organic material decays, it pulls oxygen out of the water and thereby harms aquatic life.
In addition, the piles, particularly those containing organic material and small loose
items, could clog gutters and storm drains, leading to localized flooding and property
damage.

The current program also leads to illegal dumping and other program violations (e.g.,
type of material, quantity of material, duration piles remain out) in the community in such
a way that enforcing or preventing infractions is extremely difficult.

In an effort to remedy these issues, and still provide options for residents to handle their
bulky waste needs, the City would like to move to a new Neighborhood Dumpster
Program.

Resident Scheduled Neighborhood Dumpster Program — This program would allow
residents that pay for City curbside garbage/recycle services, to schedule the delivery of
a 15-yard dumpster to their residence, twice per calendar year at either a reduced rate
or at no charge. There will be a limit of six dumpsters available each day. Scheduling

Trees: Park Strip vs Parking Lane, 66-Ft
In this 66-foot example, the canopies of park-strip trees can only reach 18’ diameter before they start to
touch building facades, requiring constant trimming. Trees with naturally smaller canopies are also naturally
shorter, and that means canopies will end up blocking business signs and great “Main Street” architecture.
The lack of shade will make pavement and the air around it far hotter in the summer, and heat reduces
pavement life. Small canopies also require nearly twice as many trees, to feel like you have any trees. That
might prove more expensive for initial planting, and small = more frequent replacement (also expensive). In
the bottom diagram, the trapezoidal “bump-out” planter boxes in the blue parking lane only cause a loss of
one in every nine parking stalls. In trade for that, you get a fantastic “tree tunnel” effect, which covers 58%
of the entire right-of-way, vs just 27% with the smaller trees. You also recover more usable space in the park
strip for outdoor seating, street art and vendors, cycle-track, etc.

Tree: Park Strip vs Parking Lane, 50-Ft
Some streets with on-street parking and sidewalks on both sides can be as narrow as 45-50 ft. In these cases,
how can you possibly fit a tree? In “theory” you can still create a tiny 3-4-ft strip next to a 4-ft sidewalk. But
notice below that these trees can only have a 10’ canopy before they are rubbing on buildings. In this case,
just 11% of the street gets any shade, and the tiny trees will struggle to even stay alive.
Even on this super-narrow street, the “Tree Zipper” format allows for 22’ canopies. Thus on a smaller street,
this is still larger than the 18’ park-strip format of the larger 66’ street. Beautiful! And all achievable with the
sacrifice of just 1 in 9 parking spaces.

Best practice is “structured soil,” which means 100% great, loose soil... inside a plastic structure that is
strong enough to hold pavements above. Shown on this page are two competing products: “Silva Cells”
by a firm called “DeepRoot,” and “RootSpace” by a firm called “Century Products.” There may be others
also.
Both systems allow storm water to be routed into the cells for irrigation and to reduce overall runoff
volume. Also, in heavy snow areas where salt is used, these systems make it easy to flush winter salts
away for healthier trees. Both product depths tend to range from 2 to 4 feet deep, which is often above
existing sewer and water pipes.
