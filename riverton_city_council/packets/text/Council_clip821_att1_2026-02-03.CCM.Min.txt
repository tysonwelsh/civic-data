36          Water Rate Study Discussion
37
38   Nick Geer, Administrative Services Director, provided a brief history of Riverton’s water rates,
39   noting that base rates and tier changes since 2015 had been relatively modest and largely flat over
40   the past decade. He then introduced Shawn Koorn of HDR Engineering, highlighting his extensive
41   experience in utility rate studies across North America and in Utah specifically.
42
43   Mr. Koorn explained that the rate study is meant to create fair, cost-based water rates by first
44   identifying total revenue needs, then allocating costs among customer groups, and finally designing
45   the rate structure. Riverton typically conducts these studies about every five years. The discussion
46   stressed the importance of funding operations, reserves, and infrastructure replacement, and noted
47   that different water sources can either have their costs spread across all users or be reflected in
48   tiered pricing.
49
50   Mr. Koorn reported that the rate study shows that Riverton’s current water revenues and reserves
51   are not enough to fund needed capital projects like aging pipe and meter replacements, so future

10            Budget Study on Revenues
11
12   Mr. Geer reviewed non-tax revenues, starting with a positive update that November sales tax was
13   the strongest in four years and now about 3.65% above last year, though December data is still
14   pending. He explained tax increment revenue from redevelopment averages around $2.8–3.1
15   million annually but largely balances out because it is reimbursed to developers and other taxing
16   entities. Other general fund revenues, such as licenses, permits, services, fines, interest, and
17   grants, are mostly flat or slightly declining, partly due to a shift away from the “non-reciprocal”
18   practice of not charging the City for its own water use. Some increases in development services are
19   tied to zoning and plan reviews for large projects like Mountain View Village, but these are
20   considered one-time revenues.
21
22   Mr. Geer explained that impact fee revenues are unpredictable and will likely decline as Riverton
23   reaches development build-out, since these are one-time fees tied to new construction and can only
24   be used for specific new capital projects, not maintenance. Culinary water revenues are projected
25   to rise slightly due to new rate tiers, growth, and system changes, while secondary water revenues
26   remain flat because they are based on fixed rates and only increase with new development. The
27   City is still short about 500 secondary water shares and actively seeking sellers, with rising prices
28   making acquisition more challenging.
29
30   Mr. Geer reviewed several “other revenue” sources, including HB 244 funds, which provide about
31   $700,000 over 15 years for infrastructure, stormwater revenues that flow into the general fund to
32   support future capital projects, and one-time reimbursement grants for large projects.

34            Secondary Water Meters
35
36   Cary Necaise, Public Works Director, discussed completing installation of the remaining secondary
37   water meters, especially larger meters on private properties and private HOA lanes, noting that
38   about 110 are still needed and roughly 50 face access issues. With a 2030 deadline and state
39   funding covering about 70% of costs, he recommended sending formal letters to property owners
40   requiring installation or risking disconnection. Councilmembers supported moving forward while
41   requesting copies of the letter and a list of affected properties.
42
43            Traffic Calming (Speed Bumps) Discussion
44
45   Mr. Necaise discussed traffic calming, focusing on speed bumps and humps, noting the City
46   currently has 111 of them, which creates ongoing maintenance and operational challenges.
47   Although speed bumps are effective at reducing speeds in the short term, they are costly (about
48   $9,000 each), require frequent upkeep, delay emergency response, create noise, vibration, and
49   vehicle wear, and often frustrate drivers, leading to faster driving between bumps. Most neighboring
50   cities no longer allow them for these reasons, and Riverton’s policy is to remove them during major
51   road maintenance and not replace them.

26            Sentinel Ridge Median Discussion
27
28   Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, reviewed traffic projections for the area,
29   including the impact of the Edge Homes development and new apartment complex, and explained
30   that some intersections are projected to experience substandard traffic flow in the future. He
31   clarified that “failure” in the traffic study refers to performance below optimal levels and
32   emphasized the need for careful consideration of median materials and design.
33
34   Council discussed the Sentinel Ridge median and traffic management and raised options
35   including stop signs, flashing school-zone lights, and roundabouts, weighing pros and cons for
36   traffic flow, safety, and future development. Staff committed to returning to a work session within
37   the next two meetings with detailed options and data to enable informed Council decisions.
