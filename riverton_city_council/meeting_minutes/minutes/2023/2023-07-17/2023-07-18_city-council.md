         Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District Proposed Tax Increase

Sherrie Ohrn, Board of Trustees of Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District, provided details
about the truth in taxation hearing which is scheduled for August 9 at 6 pm at the Jordan Valley
Water Conservancy District Offices located at 8215 South 1300 West in West Jordan. She said
the proposed tax increase for the District this year would be 9.3%. The District aims to keep the
increase as low as possible while still fulfilling their obligations. She emphasized the importance
of having taxing ability to maintain good bond rates and fund crucial infrastructure projects, such
as water-related capital projects.

Ms. Ohrn said that to illustrate the impact of the tax increase, the District provided a flyer with
examples of annual tax increases for homes at different price points. For instance, a $400,000
home would see an increase of $6.38 annually, a $550,000 home an increase of $8.77, and a
$700,000 home an increase of $11.17. The flyer also outlined some of the District's upcoming
capital projects, including a treatment plant in Herriman worth $70 million, new storage
reservoirs totaling $40 million, a southwest aqueduct costing $35 million, groundwater
development at $10 million, and debt service amounting to $30 million.

Councilmember McCay asked who the bond benefits and what capital projects it funds. In
response, Ms. Ohrn highlighted the significance of the planned capital projects, which included
upgrades to the treatment plant, construction of storage reservoirs, aqueducts, and new wells.
She clarified that the bonds would be utilized to fund these infrastructure initiatives. The new
reservoirs and other projects are intended to serve the needs of the community in the coming
decades, providing necessary resources for continued growth and development. The bonds,
which have a long-term repayment period, will be assessed on future residents who will benefit
from the infrastructure improvements made possible through the bonds.

Councilmember McCay suggested that the Jordan Valley Water Commission explore the
possibility of charging the developers for the infrastructure improvements, rather than burdening
existing residents with the cost. She believes the benefits of these improvements would
primarily be reaped by future homeowners and generations, while the current residents might
not directly benefit from the development.

Ms. Ohrn explained that while the burden of the bond increase is shared among all residents,
including existing ones, new growth is expected to pay its share through property taxes and
other contributions. She said it was important to recognize that investing in infrastructure
benefits the entire community, not just new developments. The District's efforts in water supply,
flood control, and other essential services provide advantages to all residents, irrespective of
their individual contributions to the funding. She said she would share Councilmember McCay’s
concerns with the Board.

Councilmember McDougal said he shared the same concerns as Councilmember McCay and
believes the current tax increase might disproportionately impact infill cities with minimal
development. As an infill city, residents may feel like they are financing the growth of other cities
without receiving direct benefits from impact fees that other cities might impose. He said it was
important to have a balanced approach that considers the fair distribution of costs and benefits
among all cities involved. He asked if there was a possibility of future tax increases to cover the
costs associated with the Olympia Hills project or other potential developments.
Ms. Ohrn said the District’s capital facilities plan outlines various capital projects scheduled to
be implemented over the next several years. The plan shows the projects that will come online
and the services they will provide during the next decade and beyond. The Olympia Hills

development is designed to have its own water tanks, piping, and related infrastructure. Funding
for these components will be sourced through a public infrastructure district (PID) established

Mayor Staggs emphasized the importance of investing in infrastructure without burdening

Councilmember Pierucci asked about debt service and if there would be a tax cut at some point.

Shazelle Terry, Assistant General Manager for Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District,
clarified that the tax increase under discussion was specifically for the current service area and
did not apply to the Kennecott lands that were not yet annexed.

Regarding the debt service, Ms. Terry noted that the current period had significant growth,
especially in the southwest area. This led to the largest and most intensive capital development
phase the District had ever experienced, with a particular focus on constructing a substantial
pond.

Ms. Terry said the Capital Improvements Plan for the next decade highlighted that the current
and upcoming years would likely see the highest capital investment. The expectation was that
this infrastructure development would support the community for many years to come, and as a
result, costs should gradually decrease over time.

         Resolution No. 23-56 - Authorizing the Mayor to execute an agreement with
         Redoubt Restoration to complete the Jordan Riverbank Access Project and an
         agreement with the State of Utah for the Utah Outdoor Recreation Grant

Cary Necaise, Public Works Director, said there was an agreement with a contractor to
complete the bank stabilization work. This work was crucial for the City to qualify for a $150,000
grant. He explained that the money for the grant was sourced from the previously discussed
rollover of $210,000.

Mr. Necaise said that in order to move forward with the grant application and receive the
funding, it was necessary to finalize the agreement with the contractor first, as the bank
stabilization had to be completed before the grant could be signed on. This project was a priority
to ensure the stability and safety of the affected area, and securing the grant funding would
facilitate its completion.

Councilmember McCay raised a question about the bank stabilization project near the Jordan
River Trail. She shared her recent experience driving through Colorado, where she observed
concrete banks along streams and canals. She does not like the appearance of concrete banks.
She emphasized the desire to preserve the natural aesthetics of the Jordan River rather than
opting for concrete solutions.

Mr. Necaise said they would be using natural materials while implementing measures to prevent
erosion. This project aims to maintain the natural look and aesthetics of the Jordan River while
addressing the stability and safety concerns effectively.

Councilmember Buroker acknowledged that while the delay on the stabilization project might
have increased the project's costs, it allowed for collaboration with Draper, who would be
working on the opposite side of the river. She emphasized the importance of understanding that
water flows and river dynamics are interconnected, and protecting one side without considering
the other could be counterproductive.


         Resolution No. 23-59 - Approving an Amendment to the Riverton City Personnel
         Policies and Procedures Manual

Councilmember McCay expressed her support for allowing military employees to have time off
but had some concerns about the proposed change. She noticed that the proposal required a
minimum of 14 calendar days' notice to the City before leaving for military service, but it did not
mention the total number of days allowed for military leave in a year.

In response, Ryan Carter, City Attorney, provided further explanation and context regarding the
proposed changes related to military service leave. He stated that the number of days an
employee is entitled to for military service leave is already covered by state statutes. Therefore,
he said it was not necessary to specify this number in the City ordinance. The reason behind
this decision was to avoid constantly updating the ordinance in case of any changes to state
statutes in the future.

         Ordinance No. 23-13 -Text Amendment, Amendment to the Riverton City Code
         Section 18. (20,25,30,35,40).050.9, Amending the total allowed lot coverage in
         single-family residential zones, and amending Section 18.05.030 Definitions,
         excluding pools from coverage calculations. Amendments proposed by Riverton
         City.

Mr. Lethbridge said the current code sets a maximum lot coverage of 30%, which includes the
footprints of all structures, such as homes and accessory buildings. Swimming pools are also
counted as structures under the existing ordinance.

Mr. Lethbridge said the proposed amendment, which was discussed in a previous work session,
aims to increase the maximum lot coverage to 40%. This means allowing a slightly larger area
of each lot to be covered by structures, while excluding swimming pools from this calculation.
The amendment also ensures that pavement is not included in the 40% coverage calculation.

Mr. Lethbridge explained that the feedback from the Planning Commission and the public was
generally positive regarding the amendment. Residents expressed a desire for more flexibility in
terms of expanding their homes and accessory buildings, while also aiming to be more water-
wise by reducing irrigated or landscaped areas.        He said the Planning Commission
recommended approval of the amendment, and the staff also concurred with their
recommendation.

Mayor Staggs called for public comments; there being none, he closed the Public Hearing.

Councilmember McCay MOVED that the City Council adopt Ordinance No. 23-13 -Text
Amendment, Amendment to the Riverton City Code Section 18. (20,25,30,35,40).050.9,
Amending the total allowed lot coverage in single-family residential zones, and amending
Section 18.05.030 Definitions, excluding pools from coverage calculations.

         AI Guidelines Discussion - Councilmember Pierucci

Councilmember Pierucci raised the topic of generative AI and its increasing impact on society.
He noted that AI, including platforms like ChatGPT, has become more prominent in recent years
and is being used in various ways across different industries.

Councilmember Pierucci expressed concern that while the state and counties are starting to
address policies and regulations related to AI, cities have not yet taken significant steps in this
direction. He suggested that it is essential for cities to engage in discussions about how they
want AI to shape their communities and how they can interact with and utilize AI to serve their
residents better. He proposed that the City should start having conversations about creating
guidelines or policies to provide staff with clear boundaries and expectations regarding the use
of AI.

Mr. Carter said he thought it was important for Council and staff to engage in discussions on AI,
despite not being experts in the field. He expressed concerns about the policy's encouragement
of "responsible experimentation" with AI, as it could potentially lead to unintended
consequences. He suggested that the City should consider crafting its own AI policy based on
the Boston model or explore other options, such as conducting research, questionnaires for
employees, and obtaining training on AI usage. He also proposed the idea of seeking a
consultant through an RFP (Request for Proposal) to help develop an AI policy specifically
tailored for municipalities.

Mayor Staggs suggested that the League of Cities and Towns should be involved in providing
guidance on AI policies for municipalities. He emphasized the City's investment in the
organization and the need for the League to take a proactive approach in offering model
legislation or recommendations for dealing with AI-related issues.

Councilmember Buroker said she listened to a panel discussion organized by the Chamber of
Commerce and highlighted the challenges faced by the state in crafting a statewide IT policy.
She also mentioned the importance of being cautious about automating certain functions and
how AI could impact transitions and changes in the City's current computer systems. Based on
her current understanding and the rapidly changing nature of AI, she expressed reservations
Page | 9 Minutes – City Council Meeting
July 18, 2023




about moving forward with any policy without further research and discussion.

Councilmember McDougal said that AI technology is in the early stages and agrees with Chief
Barfuss's measured approach in seeking further information before making decisions. He also
appreciated the Mayor's comment about the League of Cities and Towns taking the lead in
addressing AI policies.

Councilmember Wells agreed with the cautious approach and emphasized the need to be
careful about potential unintended consequences of implementing AI policies too early. He also
supported the idea of the League of Cities and Towns taking the lead on this matter, as they are
well-informed about developments in the state legislature and can provide guidance on any
potential policy changes related to AI.

Councilmember Pierucci emphasized that while they may not be looking to regulate AI usage
outside the City's context, he believes it's essential to have a policy that guides and encourages
responsible AI usage within the City by its employees. He believes that being proactive and
setting guidelines early will help prevent potential issues and ensure that AI is used responsibly,
with a focus on innovation while considering the implications on civil liberties and bias.

Councilmember McDougal shared the perspective that AI should be viewed as another tool,
similar to other software applications like Excel or WordPerfect. He believe that as long as
employees use AI responsibly and within the existing employment guidelines, it should not
encroach on civil liberties.

Councilmember McCay agreed that it's worth having additional discussions about AI and its
usage in the City. She suggested considering a policy that requires City employees to inform
their supervisors or department heads if they are using AI tools.
