OPENING CEREMONY:

Lisa Stoner, a Logan resident and representative of the Cache Valley Starlight Alliance,
addressed the council to promote responsible outdoor lighting during Utah Dark Sky
Month. She shared her background as a dark sky advocate and emphasized her long-
standing connection to the community and commitment to preserving its quality of life.

She explained that “dark sky” does not mean darkness, but rather reducing light pollution
caused by excessive, misdirected, or intrusive lighting. Properly designed lighting—
focused, appropriately bright, and used only when needed—can improve visibility,
safety, and comfort. She highlighted common issues such as light trespass and the
negative effects of overly bright or poorly aimed lighting, especially on aging eyes.

Ms. Stoner outlined key principles for responsible lighting: ensure lights have a clear
purpose, are properly directed, are no brighter than necessary, and use adaptive controls
like motion sensors. She also recommended warmer, amber-colored lighting as more eye-
friendly and less disruptive than blue-toned LEDs. Importantly, she noted that brighter
lighting does not necessarily increase safety.

She described several benefits of night-friendly lighting, including improved public
health, energy savings, wildlife protection, and preservation of the night sky. She also
discussed the growing economic potential of astrotourism, which can attract visitors and
support local businesses.

Ms. Stoner acknowledged Logan City’s existing lighting ordinances and suggested
strengthening enforcement. She highlighted resources available to support improvements
and shared upcoming community initiatives, including an event at the library,

Consideration of a proposed resolution adopting the Community Development
Block Grant (CDBG) Annual Action Plan (Program Year 2026) for the City of
Logan - Resolution 26-10 – Amanda Pearce, CDBG Coordinator

CDBG Coordinator Amanda Pearce addressed the Council regarding the proposed
resolution. She stated that plan outlines funding priorities, project allocations, and
strategic goals for the 2026 program year (July 1, 2026 – June 30, 2027), consistent with
the 2024–2028 Consolidated Plan.

The City anticipates receiving approximately $550,000 in federal CDBG funding. The
plan prioritizes infrastructure improvements, public services, housing rehabilitation, and
program administration, with required federal caps observed (15% for public services and
20% for administration).

Following Steering Committee recommendations and subsequent adjustments, the final
funding allocations are:

   •   ADA Ramps: $198,005
   •   Community Development Corporation of Utah (Land Acquisition): $130,000
   •   Sunshine Terrace (Residential Rehabilitation): $29,215
   •   Bus Stop Shelter Murals: $20,000
   •   Senior Center Nutrition Program: $62,780
   •   Administration & Planning: $110,000
       (Total: $550,000)

The plan emphasizes improving living environments, supporting low- and moderate-
income residents, enhancing service provider capacity, and addressing accessibility
barriers. Public input was gathered through hearings, outreach, and coordination with
community organizations.

The council discussed a proposed low to moderate-income (LMI) housing project using a
land trust model, where a nonprofit retains ownership of the land while homeowners
purchase only the structure. This approach helps maintain long-term affordability by
capping equity growth.

There was discussion about how property taxes would apply under this model, with the
understanding that taxes would likely still be assessed on the homes and possibly the land
depending on use, though details may vary and require further clarification.

Consideration of a proposed resolution approving the Logan 2045 General Plan as
presented. The General Plan creates a long-term vision for the City of Logan to
implement over the next 20 years - Resolution 26-09 – Russ Holley, Community
Development Director and Cody Ferguson, Houseal Lavigne Associates (43:18)

Community Development Director Russ Holley addressed the Council regarding the
proposed resolution to approve the Logan 2045 General Plan which has been a work in
progress for the past 18 months. The general plan was reviewed by the Planning
Commission during two meetings and they proposed six recommendations.

Mr. Holley said the council has also participated in various ways over the last 18 months.
In total, there were 15 workshops, over 390 participants, and 63 residents and
stakeholders were involved in targeted focus groups. Staff received approximately 300
responses to the online survey, conducted unique mapping exercises, and held pop-up
events at community gatherings. Altogether, there were about 830 points of engagement.

There have also been opportunities to comment on all three drafts of the plan. The
consultant team, including subconsultants and staff, made numerous visits, 15 people in
total, collecting data and meeting with the steering committee, Planning Commission, and
City Council.

Mr. Holley stated that the State of Utah requires certain elements in a general plan,
including a moderate income housing section, a water use and preservation section, and a
transportation section that considers both city and regional connectivity.

The top issues identified for Logan include affordable housing, walkability, traffic
congestion, water infrastructure keeping up with growth, economic opportunities, and
preservation of open space.

The plan also builds on previous efforts, including the 2008 General Plan, neighborhood
plans, water and sewer master plans, transportation plans, housing plans, annexation
plans, public art plans, historic district guidelines, parks and recreation plans, bicycle and
pedestrian plans, and the downtown plan.

A general plan identifies the city’s existing assets and strengths, outlines community
needs and aspirations, and serves as a guide for future policy and code. It’s a dynamic

document meant to evolve over time. It’s important because it tells Logan’s story,
informs residents and potential businesses, and provides a foundation for zoning,
ordinances, and long-term planning. It also supports budgeting and capital improvement
planning and helps identify future issues.

The planning process includes five phases: kickoff and existing conditions, public
engagement, visioning and recommendations, draft plan, and final draft.

The plan itself is organized into 12 chapters, including introduction, outreach, community
profile, vision and goals, land use, housing, economic development, transportation,
community facilities, parks and recreation, community design, and implementation.

Focusing on land use, the plan includes a future land use map—often called a “FLUP”
with 10 districts. This was designed to allow flexibility when applying zoning. For
example, a single color on the map might correspond to multiple zoning options,
allowing the city to choose what fits best in each area.

Importantly, the future land use map does not entitle a property to a specific zoning
designation. It’s a guide, not an automatic approval.

Overall, the plan adjusts current direction but doesn’t dramatically change course. It
aligns with goals the city is already working toward.

One key area of focus of the plan is the Fourth North corridor. This area shows
significant potential for increased density and redevelopment. Originally developed as
single-family housing, it has transitioned over time, now about 98% of the properties are
rentals, many of which are aging and ready for redevelopment.

The corridor presents an opportunity to connect the university with downtown, support
student housing, and increase commercial activity. It also serves as a major gateway to
Logan Canyon, Bear Lake, and Yellowstone, making it an important corridor for
placemaking and visual identity.

Currently, it’s a fairly basic five-lane road, and improvements such as streetscaping,
sidewalks, and visual enhancements could encourage reinvestment, similar to what was
done on West Center Street.

The proposed plan designates this area for higher-density and mixed-use development.
An alternative option would focus higher intensity development at key intersections (like
200 East and 600 East), with more gradual transitions to surrounding neighborhoods.

A third option, suggested by Councilmember Simmonds, is to create a corridor-specific
overlay possibly form-based to guide development more intentionally and create a
cohesive look and feel.

She spoke to Mr. Holley about overlays and different zoning opportunities and

should create a sense of place that connects Logan, the university, and the canyon.

She would like to look at the area in between using a form-based approach, so the council

Councilmember Simmonds also voiced her concern that we’re starting to bleed into

Councilmember Lee-Koven said there was also discussion about how the future land use
map will evolve. While it reflects current intentions, it is not fixed and will change over
time. However, it can create misunderstandings if people assume it guarantees immediate
development rights. Careful communication about intent will be important.

Mr. Holley explained that the future land use plan is designed to accommodate growth
through 2045, when the city’s population could nearly double. It identifies where growth
should occur while considering compatibility between land uses. The plan will need
periodic updates, and zoning changes will follow to help implement it.

Development along the corridor will likely occur incrementally, with a mix of small and
large developers. As density increases, property owners, many of whom are investors
may be more inclined to sell. A form-based approach would help ensure cohesive design
regardless of project size.

Mr. Holley stated that Utah State University was involved early in the planning process
and served on the steering committee.

Mayor Anderson noted that on April 20, 2026 the USU Landscape and Architecture

Budget Adjustments FY 2025-2026 appropriating: $200,000 financial assurance
funds provided by the developer of the Lloyd Estates Subdivision to construct or
reconstruct missing or inappropriately constructed improvements - Resolution 26-
11 – Richard Anderson, Finance Director (1:17:51)

Finance Director Richard Anderson addressed the Council regarding the proposed budget
adjustment.

Lloyd Estates Subdivision: The resolution appropriates $200,000 in financial assurance
funds provided by the developer. These funds are designated for the construction or
reconstruction of missing or improperly constructed improvements within the
subdivision. These bonds are typically collected to ensure developers fulfill their
obligations, allowing the city to step in if they do not.

In this case, the developer failed to complete necessary work despite multiple
opportunities, and with the bond nearing expiration, the city must act. The primary issue
involves a stormwater detention basin, along with other outstanding items on a punch list.

The bond funds are expected to be available soon, after which the city will hire a
contractor to complete the work. However, it is anticipated that the bond may not cover
the full cost, meaning the city and potential taxpayers could bear some additional
expense.

Mr. Anderson noted that situations like this are rare, with this being the first instance in
many years, though the project has experienced several challenges throughout its
development.

The proposed resolution will be an action item and public hearing at the April 21, 2026
Council meeting.
