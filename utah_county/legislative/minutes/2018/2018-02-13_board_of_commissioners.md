1.     ADOPT A RESOLUTION ESTABLISHING THE UTAH COUNTY PROSECUTORIAL REVIEW
       BOARD
(Track A, 14:10)
Commissioner Ivie explained a resolution was adopted last year to authorize the creation of the Utah County
Prosecutorial Review Board. Anticipating lengthy discussion, he noted this item will be continued to the next meeting
of the Utah County Board of Commissioners as there are individuals who have expressed desire to speak on this
subject but are unable to attend today. Commissioner Ivie invited public comment at this point and the first to come
forward was Connor Boyack, President of the Libertas Institute. Mr. Boyack spoke about the public understanding of
prosecutorial authority, which is very low despite high authority granted to prosecutors. After commenting on plea
deals and allegations of overreach and misconduct both nationwide and in Utah County, and the need for oversight,
he expressed support of this resolution.

(Track A, 24:20)
Provo resident Diane Christensen gave examples of the unintended and frightening consequences of this well-meant
proposal. She spoke of the potential significant damage to victims and their families who are repeatedly forced to
endure the suspect claim innocence. Ms. Christensen noted the county could be held liable for misuse of the
Prosecutorial Review Board.

Ryan McBride, a prosecutor in the Utah County Attorney’s Office, shared experiences of the many nights he has lost
sleep and the heavy burden to bear when prosecuting crimes. His stories included a man accused of sexually assaulting
two little girls after marrying their older sister who is now serving thirty years to life behind bars, and a case wherein
a woman’s boyfriend had abused the woman’s daughters and eventually murdered them. Mr. McBride explained he
must think he can prove a case beyond any reasonable doubt in order to prosecute. He chooses to make personal
sacrifices for this career he believes in, but this proposal is a vote of ‘no confidence.’ Mr. McBride summarized the
commissioners are stating, ‘we want someone to verify everything you do – we don’t trust you,’ despite the many
agencies already responsible for oversight. He stated he understands their goal of helping and protecting innocent
people, and reminded the Board that is his goal, too. Mr. McBride suggested the commissioners further research any
alleged complaints about prosecutorial misconduct.

(Track A, 43:30)
Attorney and Pleasant Grove resident Ben Stanley supports this proposal for a Prosecutorial Review Board, and
pointed out Ryan McBride just suggested the commissioners do the same thing this review board would do: due
diligence in reviewing the facts to make sure justice is served. Sam Pead of the Utah County Attorney’s Office
reiterated Mr. McBride’s comments that there are already built-in oversights for this issue. Mr. Pead mentioned Ben
Stanley’s perspectives are biased based on attempted political gain when previously running for an elected office. Mr.
Pead reminded the commissioners the County Attorney is also an elected official, and it is an overstep of governmental
UTAH COUNTY BOARD OF COMMISSIONERS
MINUTES – February 13, 2018
Page 7

authority for the elected commissioners to tell the elected prosecutor how to do his job. Provo resident Robert Stevens
presented ideas on how to conduct more research without furthering governmental overreach or overstepping the
bounds of the United States Constitution.

Commissioner Ivie returned discussion to the bench, and Commissioner Graves replied to some of the public
comments made. Commissioner Graves clarified Ryan McBride was suggesting the commissioners specifically
review open complaints against past cases, and not take on the role of complete Prosecutorial Review Board as Ben
Stanley had stated. He complimented Connor Boyack’s intelligence but disagreed his statements regarding
Commission authority. Commissioner Graves also addressed Ryan McBride directly to say, “thank you,” adding his
thanks for all of the men and women working as public servants for Utah County. Commissioner Graves noted he
regrets misspeaking at a previous meeting of the Board when he said “all of the employees here are committing time
fraud.” Commissioner Graves said the Board would have dealt with any specific cases of time fraud; county
employees are very good and truthful people. He talked about taking an oath to uphold the Constitution and pointed
out the Utah County Attorney is the only local elected official whose entire role is completely defined by code.
Commissioner Graves stated some of the language of this proposed resolution is disturbing, and his belief that it would
be a violation of his oath to accept it as written.

Commissioner Lee explained the intent of the Prosecutorial Review Board, stating it is not a re-trial or claim that they
are not doing a good job. Commissioner Ivie echoed that statement, adding this is an avenue for Utah County to set
a positive example locally and nationwide. The commissioners discussed the nomination process, board positions,
and fiduciary responsibilities.

                                           PUBLIC COMMENTS
(Track A, 2:33:35)
Larry Ellertson, former Utah County Commissioner, came forward to express concerns about the state of government
at all levels – federal, state, county, and city. He thanked the Board for the opportunity to express public comments
and explained he generally sleeps very well, but not so much lately. Mr. Ellertson woke up this morning and decided
to share some notes to clear his mind; his intent is not to offend anyone and he apologizes in advance if that occurs.
Mr. Ellertson referred to an editorial in the February 11, 2018 ‘The Daily Herald’ newspaper which addressed the
current three-member Board organization for Utah County government. He noted it is an important issue and should
involve a process to make it work; it should not necessarily be an easy process. Mr. Ellertson mentioned the state
legislature is looking at ways to make things easy, but he is not sure that is the proper thing to do. Mr. Ellertson said,
“It needs to be well thought-out.” Mr. Ellertson believes at-large positions promote broadly based representation
within government. When a vote is specified by district, it begins to fracture the general concern and opportunity to
represent all citizens in the county. Mr. Ellertson pointed out he enjoyed that opportunity and felt that the Board
worked hard at it. Once someone is elected by only a small group of people, the person tends to answer only to those
people. Mr. Ellertson advised caution in such action and reiterated at-large representation is much better than re-
districting.

Larry Ellertson said he enjoyed working and being committed to all county issues, and believes the people he worked
with throughout the county recognized his genuine concern for them. He added that does not mean everyone was
happy, but could understand that the commissioners were trying to represent all of them. Being a commissioner is a
full-time job. The newspaper editorial addressed county growth and the need to enlarge the Board, but the county is
not run by three people. There are ten elected officials, and regular meetings with two dozen additional people who
represent all areas in the county, as well as regional planning and other boards and committees providing input through
that process. Legislative standpoints ultimately belong to the commissioners, but there are many good, smart
employees as well. Mr. Ellertson advised against getting caught up in micromanagement, instead allowing those
employees to do their jobs and listening to them.

Mr. Ellertson talked about principles, referencing a set of rules called ‘The Word of Wisdom’ and the instruction for
‘moderation in all things.’ He explained that is not just for what we eat and drink – it includes what we do and what
we think. He suggested being more considerate and moderating some of our thinking sometimes. The current form
of county government has yielded many good things and should not be changed because of other entities. Mr. Ellertson
pointed out Salt Lake County was mentioned as a good example in the newspaper. He disagrees and has personal
experience with many who question the quality of that example, as well as the high cost. If the form of government
is not working, the people behind the government need to be willing to take a look at themselves because it is the
people who enact the form. Mr. Ellertson emphasized the need to employ principles of forgiveness and repentance.
He said, “If we make a mistake, we need to repent about it… we need to make amends and we need to be willing to
forgive. We shouldn’t fracture and tear things down; we need to work together.” Mr. Ellertson has worked with local
elected officials for more than twenty years and has spent fifty years working with people and has found it enjoyable.
He suggested there are very few people that cannot be worked with, and perhaps we need to examine our attitudes
towards people and make amends where amends need to be made. At the end of the day, Mr. Ellertson said, “We’re
all in this together, and we need to learn to work and play together for the betterment of all and not my own agenda.”
He thanked the commissioners again for their time.
