                                    CONTRACT
WITNESS THIS CONTRACT (hereinafter called Contract) made the _____day of
_______, 2021 by and between DS Accounting Services LLC, the (“Contractor”) and
Magna Township, the (“MT”):

                                        I. PURPOSE
The Contractor shall provide accounting, and any other related services to the MT
according to directions or projects made by the Township Council. The Contractor
retains the right to perform services required by the specifications by any reasonable
means and in any reasonable manner it sees fit.

                                SPECIFIC SERVICES
Daily – Provide assistance on texts, emails and telephone conversations. Attend
meetings if needed.

Monthly – Review monthly financial reports and prepare onthly financial reports for
Township meetings.

Yearly – Budget preparation.

                                          II. TERM
The term of the Contract shall commence July 1, 2021 and continue until either party
decides to terminate contract. Said Contract shall continue in full force and effect unless
and until either party gives sixty (60) days written notice of its intent to terminate.
Receipt of a termination notice by either party shall automatically terminate this Contract
upon the expiration of the applicable notice period, unless both parties agree to reinstate
this Contract. Access to the MT office will be provided.

                                III. COMPENSATION
The MT agrees to pay the Contractor at $1000.00 per month and any out of scope
services at $85.00 per hour. Also MT will reimburse for miscellaneous out-of-pocket
expensed such as postage and travel expenses

This amount is to be paid monthly and the Contractor will invoice the MT at the end of
each month and the terms of the payment will be net 15. Any delinquent amounts will be
assessed 1 ½% interest per month.

                                        IV. TAXES
The Contractor shall be deemed an independent contract employee and the contractor is
responsible of any applicable taxes or insurance.


                V. CONFORMANCE WITH LEGAL OBLIGATIONS
The Contractor agrees to abide by all applicable federal, state and local laws in the
conduct of its business and the performance of services required by the specifications
attached hereto and made part of this Contract.

                                     VII. BINDING
This Contract shall insure to and bind all parties, their successors, assigns, agents or
representatives.

                             VIII. ENTIRE AGREEMENT
This Contract contains the entire agreement between the parties. All prior negotiations
between the parties are merged in this Contract, and there are no understandings or
agreements other than those incorporated or referred to herein. This Contract may not be
modified except in writing and signed by both parties. This Contract supersedes any
prior Contracts.

Re:    OAM2021-000256– Mahogany Ridge Development Agreement Proposed Change


On May 25th, 2021 the Council approved a development agreement with Ivory Homes regarding
the Mahogany Ridge planned community. This agreement outlined the density and conditions
under which the development would be done. While the mayor has signed this agreement, Ivory
Homes has not yet signed and is requesting some changes before this is finalized.
Ivory Homes is requesting that the density numbers within the village area be increased to be in
line with the P-C code. Note that this is not an increase to the overall density number but to the
allowed density within the village district so that the units could be more concentrated. This
would result in more open space between units, but higher numbers of units in contained areas.
Ivory states that this change is being requested to allow for development as some of the areas
also fall within the overpressure zone and make design of the area more complicated. Relevant
sections from the existing DA, proposed DA and from the P-C ordinance regarding the Village
District are posted below.

                                  EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

What is going on with Magna’s Storm Water Drainage System?
Salt Lake County Public Works and Engineering (County) oversees maintenance, planning and
implementation of storm drain and flood control needs for Magna, Utah. The Metro Township of
Magna has been dealing with storm water drainage challenges for many years. Continued growth
throughout Salt Lake County has brought the usual side effect of compounding existing problems
with utilities in general and storm drainage in particular.

As open land is developed, rainfall that previously soaked into the ground now accumulates quickly
over parking lots, driveways, and rooftops. The existing underground network of storage and piping
that would normally contain this accumulation does not have the capacity to accommodate the
additional rainfall runoff. The result is increased flooding in the streets, homes, and businesses of
Magna. Left unchecked, this flooding can result in costly structural damage and block roadways for
emergency services.

In several areas of Magna, there are storm water storage ponds that do not drain properly and are
left with standing water that is prone to contamination, trash, weeds, and mosquitos. These sites not
only take up land that could be put to better use, but can potentially become a blight to the community
as public health and safety hazards.

In addition to the challenges already mentioned, there are areas of Magna with no drainage
infrastructure, and other areas that share storm drain pipeline capacity with irrigation water. There
are also portions of the storm drain system that have aged past their useful life and are no longer
functioning effectively.

Historically, much of the storm water drainage system has not been mapped. So when problems arise
finding the storm water infrastructure and understanding if and how it connects is an additional
burden.

In light of these growing challenges, Salt Lake County Public Works has undertaken an extensive field
survey and system mapping effort. Over time, this information has been collected into a single
database. This information is continually updated as additional information is collected and new
developments are constructed.

With substantial portions of the system now mapped, Salt Lake County commissioned Bowen Collins
and Associates (BC&A) to develop a Storm Water Master Plan for Magna. The intent of the master
plan is address the challenges facing the storm water drainage system and create a plan for future
development of the system.

What should be done to improve the storm water system?
As part of this master planning effort, a storm water computer model was created. This allows the
County to better understand how the storm water system is working today, and how it might work
with future improvements.

The model was used to simulate storm water response to rainfall when Magna receives a larger-than-
average rainstorm. The rainfall event selected for the evaluation is a 10-year design storm of short
duration and high intensity (i.e. a storm with a 10 percent chance of occurring in any given year).

                                   1.0 INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background and Introduction
The urbanization of the northwest portion of Salt Lake County continues to have significant impacts
on existing infrastructure, including storm water conveyance and collection facilities. Magna, a
township located near the Great Salt Lake and the Oquirrh Mountains, has seen particular growth.
The evolution from a rural to urban landscape necessitates continuous maintenance, improvement
and development of their storm drain systems (SDS). Studies conducted in 1976 and 2005 provide
significant precedence, but no comprehensive storm drain master plan has been completed for the
Magna Township.

Magna, a Metro Township in unincorporated Salt Lake County, is a member of the Greater Salt Lake
Municipal Services District (MSD). The Salt Lake County Public Works and Engineering Department
oversees the maintenance, planning and development of infrastructure needs for the MSD. Storm
drain planning, design, and construction efforts are overseen by the Engineering Division, while
maintenance and operation of storm drainage facilities are conducted under the direction of the
Operations Division.

Coon Canyon Creek drains the Oquirrh Mountains from the south and west, while four major canals
terminate or flow through Magna. These major drainage channels are illustrated in Figure 3 of
Appendix A. Much of the storm drain infrastructure operates jointly as flood control and irrigation,
including the Utah and Salt Lake Canal, the Riter Canal and the Utah and Salt Lake Tailwater Ditch.
Smaller irrigation distribution ditches and pipes are also used for storm drain runoff. The County
hopes to separate the smaller storm drain collection and irrigation distribution systems and divest
itself of the irrigation distribution system. With the urbanization of Magna the drainage system has
experienced increasing strain, while the demand for the irrigation system has diminished.

The County and Magna Township require an updated and comprehensive storm drain master plan
to identify and address storm drain deficiencies and to plan for future development.

1.2 Purpose of Work
In December 2018 Salt Lake County retained Bowen Collins and Associates (BC&A) to develop a
comprehensive storm drain master plan for the Magna Township with two primary objectives:
   1. To inventory existing drainage facilities, to evaluate the adequacy of these facilities to manage
      storm water runoff for both existing and future conditions, and to recommend improvements
      as needed.
   2. To gain a better understanding of the storm drain collection and irrigation distribution
      system interconnections. It is the County’s desire to ultimately separate the two systems and
      divest itself of the irrigation system.

The purpose of this report is to describe the methods used to accomplish these objectives and
summarize the results.

       InfoSWMM model has been provided to the County.

                            2.0 DESCRIPTION OF STUDY AREA

2.1 Location
The study area, as shown in Figure 1 of Appendix A, is located on the northwest side of Salt Lake
Valley. The area includes the Magna Township and areas of potential future development north of
2100 South. In general the storm drain facilities evaluated in this study are bounded by the township.

4100 South on the south and 7200 West on the east.

To evaluate the adequacy of Magna’s SDS, contributing drainage areas south of 2400 South were
included or accounted for in the study. This includes areas on the west between the Utah & Salt Lake
Canal (USLC) and the abandoned Kennecott railroad spur. The Coon Canyon watershed in the
Oquirrh Mountains also drains through Magna from southwest to northeast, with drainage
accumulating in the north east end of the township. It collects in a channel, flows east then north and
eventually drains to the C-7 Ditch and then the Great Salt Lake.

2.2 Elevation and Climate
Study area elevations range from approximately 4,200 to 5,800 ft with most developed residential
and commercial areas within the 4,200 to 4,600 ft range.

The climate in the Salt Lake Valley is characterized as semi-arid, with annual mean relative humidity
of 55 percent (ranging from 22 to 79 percent). Average temperatures range from 59⁰ to 88⁰ F in the
summer, 40⁰ to 65⁰ F in the spring and fall, and 22⁰ to 40⁰ F in the winter. Average annual
precipitation in the valley is approximately 15 inches, more than half of which comes from snow.

2.3 Native Soils
Native soils in the study area consist predominantly of lean clay and silt, with some pockets of silty
sand and clayey gravel possible (Geotechnical Investigation for Cyprus 8800 West Detention Basin,
AGEC, 2007). While the subsurface exploration was limited to test pits and borings near Cyprus High
School, it may be assumed similar throughout the study area.

Groundwater elevations vary across the study area both geographically and seasonally. The northern
portions of the study area have higher groundwater elevations because of their proximity to the Great
Salt Lake. Depth to water table increases to the south. Groundwater elevations tend to be higher in
the spring and summer, as a result of snowmelt, and lower in the fall and winter.

2.4 Land Use Conditions
The study area covers 4,757 acres which encompasses the Magna Township including the largely
undeveloped area north of SR-201 approaching the Great Salt Lake. Overall, the study area is
approximately 75 percent built-out.

Within the developed portion of the study area, roughly 80 percent is residential development, with
20 percent commercial and light industrial areas. Among the residential areas, there is a mix of
medium to high density developments. A few low-density residential developments are scattered
throughout the study area.

                                                     5.0 HYDROLOGIC ANALYSIS

5.1 Introduction
Evaluating a storm water system consists of understanding two key components. The first is
understanding how runoff is generated in response to precipitation (i.e. hydrologic analysis). The
second is understanding how the drainage system responds to the runoff (i.e. hydraulic analysis).
This section provides an overview of the hydrologic analysis.

5.2 Precipitation
For the purposes of this study, the worst-case condition for Magna’s storm drain system is assumed
to be a typical summer intense cloudburst event characterized by brief, high-intensity rainfall. This
assumption is consistent with previous studies for Magna and the Salt Lake Valley.

To mimic this type of rainstorm, a 10-year recurrence interval event was selected. This event
corresponds to a rainfall depth of 1.19 inches over the entire study area based on data from the
nearby Garfield Rain Gauge Station reported in the NOAA Atlas 14.

The rate at which this rainfall depth falls over the duration of a 3-hour event is assumed to follow a
modified Farmer-Fletcher temporal distribution. This is illustrated in Figure 5.1.

5.3 Study Area Response to Design Event
As the design rainfall event falls over the study area, a number of interactions occur. Precipitation is
intercepted by vegetation and structures before hitting the ground. Once reaching the ground,
precipitation may be absorbed into the ground or evaporated. There is also small local surface
ponding on both paved and unpaved areas. As rain continues to fall, excess rainfall begins to
accumulate and flow over the landscape, form into rills and flow into storm water conveyance
systems. A hydrologic model simulates these interactions to generate an estimate of the runoff


BOWEN COLLINS & ASSOCIATES
SALT LAKE COUNTY PUBLIC WORKS ENGINEERING                                                                             5.1
                                                             MAGNA STORM WATER MASTER PLAN 2021


volume and flow-rate expected from a design storm event over the study area. There are several
acceptable methods for simulating these interactions. For this study, the EPA Storm Water
Management Model (SWMM) method was selected.

SWMM is a dynamic rainfall-runoff simulation model used for single event or long-term (continuous)
simulation of runoff quantity and quality from primarily urban areas. The runoff component of
SWMM operates on a collection of subcatchment areas that receive precipitation and generate runoff
and pollutant loads. The routing portion of SWMM transports this runoff through a system of pipes,
channels, storage/treatment devices, pumps, and regulators. SWMM tracks the quantity and quality
of runoff generated within each subcatchment, and the flow rate, flow depth, and quality of water in
each pipe and channel during a simulation period comprised of multiple time steps (EPA SWMM
User’s Manual v. 5.1, 2015).

A summary of the SWMM inputs used to characterize the study area can be found in Appendix B.

5.4 Drainage Basin Delineation
To facilitate the evaluation of Magna’s storm water system, the study area has been divided into sub-
basins. These sub-basins represent an approximation of the contributing land area that drains to an
outlet point. All of the runoff generated within that basin is assumed to flow into the conveyance
facilities within that basin and, where applicable, facilities downstream of that basin.

Basin delineation is based on ground elevations, conveyance facilities (both natural and manmade),
and the type of development covering the land. Topography and aerial imagery where obtained from
the State of Utah AGRC (aerial imagery dated 2018). Topography resolution is based on 0.5 meter
LiDAR (1.64 feet, 2013). Due to the significant slope from southwest to northeast across this study
area, this publicly-available topography is sufficient to support master planning efforts.

Figure 5 in Appendix A illustrates the basin delineations. Some of these basins were further
subdivided into subcatchments for more detailed distribution and routing within the basin. These
smaller subcatchments are not shown in Figure 5, but are listed along with their parameters in the
Model Inputs table in Appendix B.

The overall study area consists of 4,758 acres divided into 90 sub-basins ranging in size from 0.84 to
251 acres. The average sub-basin size is 45 acres.

5.5 Hydrologic Computer Model Creation
The creation of the hydrologic computer model can be accomplished with various software
applications. Following discussion with County staff at the beginning of the study, the modeling
software employed for this study is InfoSWWM Suite v. 14.7 produced by Innovyze. Model data can
be exported to a free platform such as EPA SWMM; however, much of the functionality of the
InfoSWMM model is not available in EPA SWMM.

The model parameters discussed in this section were entered into the computer model for each sub-
basin and subcatchment. A modified version of the EPA SWMM 5 Engine is used by the software to
calculate the runoff from each of the drainage areas.

The continuity error of a computer model is a good indicator of the model’s stability and the
reliability of the model results based on the input information provided via model inputs. The
continuity error for this model is less than 1.3 percent.

5.6 Hydrologic Computer Model Calibration
Model calibration is the process of adjusting the initial model assumptions to generate results that
consistently match observed field conditions. Calibration of storm water models is problematic,
since it is difficult to monitor storm water flows for a long enough period to capture rainfall and
runoff events comparable to those with 10-year and 100-year probability of occurrence. Runoff
response to rainfall is not linear, so small storm events cannot typically be used to calibrate models
for larger events.

Fortunately studies have been conducted for the Salt Lake Valley in the past to establish typical runoff
response to 10-year and larger rainfall events. Model results are compared to previous studies’
outcomes, typical expectations for urban runoff in the Salt Lake Valley, and staff knowledge of where
flooding has occurred in the past. In each comparison, these model results compared well and appear
to be reasonable in all aspects. Through the application of conservative buffers and practical factors
of safety, the results can be used for planning purposes and provide the best available information
for the design of future developments or capital improvement projects.

As the model is further refined in the future, the County may want to consider installing flow meters
at key locations (such as the Coon Canyon Creek trunkline in 8000 West) to measure runoff response
and a weather station to measure rainfall intensity and volume. These would need to remain
installed and be monitored to capture several rain events during various seasons throughout
multiple years. These would allow for further calibration of runoff response to rainfall.

5.7 Projected Future Land Use Conditions
As the study area is developed, the unpaved pervious areas that now absorb rainfall will give way to
impervious rooftops, roadways, and parking lots that will increase runoff and the potential for
flooding.

The model of the existing study area has established current runoff rates for both the developed and
undeveloped areas as they are today. As development or redevelopment occurs it is recommended
that the County work with developers to consider improvements on a case-by-case basis and restrict
runoff rates and volumes through onsite mitigation measures using proven storm water best
management practices (BMPs). The County may also consider requiring a reduction of runoff from
redevelopment areas with high existing runoff rates. This type of policy reduces the potential for
adding burden on the existing storm water drainage system. Additional discussion of general
guidelines is included at the end of this section.

Historically, this approach has led to maintenance challenges as numerous small, single-site storm
water detention or retention facilities are constructed to varying degrees of effectiveness and design
quality. However, a policy of restricting runoff increase does not necessarily require dispersed storm
water facilities. Well-planned and effective regional facilities can be constructed in central locations
within the natural drainage pathways. The cost can be distributed to benefitting properties through
latecomer agreements which will allow for their construction with the first development while later
developments pay their fair share of the cost to first-in developer.

There are other benefits too. Regional detention, for example, can be large enough to serve as multi-
use park and stormwater facilities and is typically easier to maintain with large equipment.

5.7.1 General Guidelines for New Development
As discussed here, limitations on runoff for new development should be considered on a case-by-
case basis for Magna. Downstream receiving facilities in Magna such as the USLC Tailwater Ditch, are

already at or above capacity to convey runoff from a 10-year design storm. In general, there is not
capacity for additional flow or volume in these facilities.
In the past, the County has established general guidelines for limitations on runoff from new
development. For example, regional studies such as the 2002 Southwest Canal and Creek Study
recommended limiting storm water discharge in areas of planned development to 0.2 cfs per acre,
and limiting storm water discharge in areas not anticipated for development to predevelopment
runoff levels. These regional studies are currently being updated. In general, study updates reflect
that development has been more dense and more expansive than anticipated 20 years ago, with a
decrease in average lot sizes and extension of development into foothill and other areas not
previously envisioned. As a result, cumulative peak runoff rates and volumes from developing areas
have generally been found to be higher than expected.
In addition, recommendations to limit runoff to predevelopment conditions are subject to
interpretation and often misconstrued to allow new development runoff significantly higher that
historic native conditions (e.g. by using the most recent land use, such as plowed farmland or poor
rangeland, as the predevelopment condition rather than the historic natural watershed condition).
Based on historic flow records for large watersheds in the Salt Lake Valley, native watershed runoff
rates are estimated to be in the range of 0.02 to 0.05 cfs per acre. This is a reasonable guideline to
compare with estimates of predeveloped runoff conditions, and a reasonable guideline for general
runoff limitations. It should be noted that runoff limitations should apply to both 10-year and 100-
year events.
In general the direction of the State of Utah, who administers the Utah Pollutant Discharge
Elimination System (with primacy granted by the U.S. EPA), is increasingly to limit runoff peak
discharge, volume and water quality to predevelopment (i.e. historic native watershed) conditions.
The intent of this is to limit negative impacts to downstream streams, lakes and rivers. In keeping
with this, drainage from new development in Magna should consider peak discharge, volume and
water quality associated with storm water runoff.

                                  6.0 Hydraulic Analysis

6.1 Introduction
As mentioned in Section 5 of this report, evaluating a storm water system consists of understanding
two key components. The first is understanding how the land responds to precipitation, or the
hydrologic analysis. The second is to understand how the drainage system responds to runoff, or the
hydraulic analysis. This section provides an overview of the hydraulic analysis of Magna’s storm
drainage system.

6.2 Hydraulic Modeling Creation
Storm water runoff from the study area is routed through a network of roadside gutters, open
channels, and underground pipes. Periodically, this runoff accumulates or ponds in basins or in the
streets. The intent of the hydraulic model is to mimic the flow conditions in this network during the
design rainfall event and identify areas that may need to be improved.

The model is limited to the primary drainage corridors. All of the runoff from the area surrounding
these drainage corridors is assumed to make it to the trunk lines. In reality, this is not likely to be
the case in all areas. There may be flooding in some areas due to lacking infrastructure; however,
once these smaller issues are resolved and the flow is completely routed to the main corridor, the
runoff will have already been accounted for in the trunk lines using this approach.

The creation of the hydraulic model begins with the drainage facility inventory. The best available
data on the storm water network has been entered into the computer modeling software InfoSWMM
Suite v. 14.7. This software was selected because of its ability to evaluate flooded backwater
conditions in a storm water system. A digital copy of this model has been provided to the County for
use in planning for and evaluating future developments.

6.3 Hydraulic Modeling Calibration
As more of the storm water system is surveyed and uncovered, it is recommended that the hydraulic
model be updated with the best available data. This will improve the accuracy of the model in
reflecting actual field conditions.

6.4 Hydraulic Modeling Results: Conveyance
As the runoff is routed through the pipes in the model, water is allowed to pond on the ground surface
if there is not enough capacity in the pipe to convey the flow. As the pipe drains and capacity becomes
available, the ponded water is allowed to enter the piped system.

The model reports remaining pipe capacity as peak flow passes through and the total volume of
ponded water in sections of inadequate pipe capacity.

For the purpose of this system evaluation, a threshold for pipelines needing improvements was set
based on significant upstream flooding. Significant flooding was considered to be a total ponded
volume of 0.1 acre-ft (or one tenth of an acre covered in 1 foot of water). Figure 6.1 shows an example
of the approximate volume of flooding equivalent to 0.1 acre-ft.

6.6 Hydraulic Modeling Results: Discharge Conditions
All of the areas west of 8400 West ultimately discharge to the Tailwater Ditch. Most of the remaining
study area discharges to the Riter Canal. The water surface elevation of the ditch and canal were
assumed to be below the storm water system outfall pipe inverts. This assumption allows the system
failures to manifest according to weaknesses in the system and not from backwater effects on
submerged outfalls.

The hydraulic capacity of the Tailwater Ditch was studied and modeled previously as part of the USLC
Tailwater Ditch Study completed by BC&A in 2008. The study found that the Tailwater Ditch was
over-capacity at the time and contributing runoff from development has only increased since then.

The recommended improvements for the Tailwater Ditch from the previous study have been
included in the capital improvement plan of this study.

         7.0 RECOMMENDED CAPITAL IMPROVEMENTS PLAN

7.1 Introduction
The hydraulic model of the existing system aided in the identification of system deficiencies. That
model was also used to simulate improvements to the existing system to eliminate flooding through
improved detention capacity or pipeline capacity necessary to convey the peak runoff rates from the
design rainfall event.

This section provides an overview of the recommended improvements and provides Salt Lake
County’s preferred prioritization of the capital improvement projects.

The order of priority for these improvements includes several considerations. Storm water
improvements have a tendency to move problems downstream. So, for practical reasons, the
downstream projects need to occur first in order to provide the increased conveyance for later
upstream improvements.

Priority ranking also considers the potential impact and severity of flooding potential. Where
projects can realize a cost savings by coordinating with other planned projects such as road
widenings, repaving, or utility corridor improvements, these have been included in the order of
project prioritization.

7.2 Recommended Storm Water System Improvements
Figure 7 in Appendix A illustrates the overall recommended storm water system improvements. The
improvements have been grouped together by proximity. Appendix C contains project sheets for
each of the recommended projects. The sheets contain a written description, an area map, and
opinions of probable construction costs.

       Conveyance improvements generally consist of increasing the existing pipe size or installing
       new pipelines where there are gaps in the storm water system (including overlaps with the
       irrigation system). To better explain their locations and purpose, the improvements have
       been illustrated in the Recommended Capital Improvements Plan (Figure 7) found in
       Appendix A with additional supporting details provided in the project sheets in Appendix C.

       Improvement recommendations from the 2008 USLC Tailwater Ditch study have been
       incorporated into the CIP by reference. In summary, the recommendations include:
       1. Establish Ditch Maintenance Responsibility
       2. Perform Channel Maintenance
       3. Make Channel Improvements:

                  West extending to the upstream end of the Kennecott Process Water
                  Undercrossing. Replace the culvert and road crossing at 8400 West.
               b. Improve the ditch to have a consistent slope downstream of the Kennecott
                  Process Water Undercrossing to the confluence with the C-7 Ditch.
               c. Remove existing 12-foot section of 84-inch diameter concrete pipe.

       Detention basin improvements generally consist of improving existing storage basins or

       improved to detain 0.84 ac-ft and attenuate the contributing area’s peak runoff rate to 4.9 cfs.

       Improve Basin 3: This basin will be improved to function properly and eliminate standing

       system can accommodate the additional flow. Abandonment of this basin was designed and
       implemented in 2019-20.

       could continue to do so through a mutually beneficial agreement with the owner. The model
       predicts 1.6 cfs of peak flow would be added to the property during a storm event if the
       basin wasn’t eliminated, but the total volume directed to the site would remain roughly the
       same as it is today. The existing pipe crossing the road has the capacity for the additional
       flow. For the present the County has determined this basin should remain in place.

       Eliminate Basin 6: The basin collects runoff from a relatively small area. Downstream
       conveyance improvements, already identified for other reasons, will be necessary to
       accommodate the additional peak flow rates resulting from eliminating this basin. The sale

                                     8.0 CONCLUSION

The objectives of this storm water master plan are consistent with Salt Lake County’s and Magna’s
goal of growing in smart and responsible ways while creating vibrant, clean, and safe neighborhoods.

Like many communities, Magna’s storm water drainage system has significant challenges to
overcome. Overburdened storm water facilities can lead to costly damage from flooding. Standing
water can also create safety and health hazards. Poorly functioning storm water facilities can be
unsightly and a source of odors and insects.

The recommendations provided in this master plan can serve as the road map to improving Magna’s
storm water drainage over time as resources and opportunities become available.

While the recommended improvements are costly, neglecting the growing issues are likely to be even
more so. These improvements to Magna’s storm water drainage system are an investment in the
community that will bear practical returns such as improved land values, new development
possibilities, and maintenance cost savings. In addition to the practical benefits, these improvements
promote a better quality of life for those living and working in Magna.
