      A.     GENERAL PLAN AMENDMENT AND REZONE – “THOMAS REZONE”, A
             REQUEST TO REZONE 1.29 ACRES AT 3444 WEST 12350 SOUTH.
             APPLICANT, JOSH THOMAS.

Mr. Prestwich reported that the proposed rezone makes it a legislative recommendation
item. The Planning Commission makes a recommendation to the City Council who makes
the final decision. He identified the location of the subject area near 3600 West and north
of 12600 South. The applicant, Josh Thomas, is requesting R-4 zoning, and the property is
currently General Planned for Estate Density Residential, so this application is also a
General Plan Amendment that is necessary if the rezone is granted. The notice was sent to
all property owners within 1,000 feet of the exterior of the boundary of the property. In
addition to the mailings, many residents typically post agenda items on Facebook, so there
is a good chance that people know about it and are involved.

The General Plan shows what the City would like the land to be or considered to be in the
future. The zoning map shows what the land can be used for currently. City Ordinances
correspond to the Zoning Map. Because Riverton is largely built out, the General Plan and
Zoning Map mostly correspond. There are areas in the City that the City Council and
Planning Commission have recognized as having potential room for a change of use and
where the General Plan does not match.

The subject property is currently zoned R-1 and is bounded on the north by R-3, on the south
and west by major roads, and on the east by the canal. The property is currently zoned
Estate Density Residential, which is equivalent to one-half-acre lots with animal rights. The
zoning corresponds to the General Plan’s R-1 and RR-22. Historically, once the City
changes the zoning to medium density, the surrounding areas will likely have a change in
animal rights in subsequent zoning changes.

The R-1 zoning provides for approximately one-acre lots with a minimum lot width of 150feet.
It has animal rights and allows for Accessory Dwelling Units. The R-4 zone allows for four
units per acre, 10,000 square-foot minimum lot sizes, an 80-foot minimum lot width, and no
animal rights.     Accessory Dwelling Units (“ADU”) are allowed.         The surrounding
neighborhood is older and consists of rolled gutter and no sidewalks. Each lot is at least
one acre in size.

There have been several comments regarding the proposed zone change. All of the emailed
comments were forwarded to the Commission Members. Most of the comments pertained
to reasons why they were opposed to the rezone. The majority stated that it was out of
character with the neighborhood and cited the conflict of animals versus non-animals. There
was also concern with the rural and traditional feel of the current neighborhood conflicting
with the updated neighborhood as the City’s requirement would be curb, gutter, and
sidewalk, which is the new standard. There were also concerns with existing flood irrigation
systems that are in place and deficiencies in street lighting and stormwater volume.

       B.     CONDITIONAL USE, “HOWELL PRIVATE LANE” A PROPOSED PRIVATE
              LANE TO ACCESS 4.07 ACRES LOCATED AT 2356 WEST 11800 SOUTH.
              APPLICANT, MARV HOWELL.

Mr. Prestwich presented the staff report and stated that the matter was before the
Commission for a rezone in December and went to the City Council in January. The
Planning Commission recommended R-4 zoning and the City Council upheld that decision
and added a couple of conditions with the SD. It is located on the north end of the City and
is bound on the north side by Midas Creek, which is managed by Salt Lake County. The
subject property is an odd shape, and the key feature is the narrow piece that is 33-feet
wide. The question when looking at a private lane is whether the property can be served
with a public road or if it can meet the other criteria and be served by a private lane.

Within the last few years, the City Council chose to give additional tools to encourage
development including more lots on a private lane as the remaining vacant lots are typically
difficult to develop for various reasons. There is no longer a restriction as to how many lots
can be developed on a private lane other than zoning restrictions. Previously, developers
attempted to develop the property but because of the uniqueness of the property, it has been
difficult. The minimum street width is 54 feet, which includes 32 feet of asphalt and 12 feet
on each side for improvements.

Notices for rezone are sent to residents within 1,000 feet of the property. The approved
zoning shows R-4, and the SD designation shows that the two northern lots must match the
zoning across the canal, so they are one-third acre lots. They must also comply with the
Riverton City Planning Commission Meeting                                                 7
February 10, 2022


Code that specifies that the Development Agreement shall be prepared to include a shared
access requirement with the adjacent properties to the west to be executed before
subdivision approval may be granted for these properties. The owner must enter into a
Development Agreement with the property to the west for access before a subdivision can
be approved. This is because the neighboring property would have no access if the private
lane were approved.

Mr. Prestwich displayed the Concept Plan and presented the proposed subdivision layout.
It was just for information purposes, as the Commission will review the final concept plan at
the subdivision approval stage. The proposed access easement is not wide enough for cars
parked on either side and a fire truck to drive down the lane, so Staff’s motion included
widening the easement to 32 feet to allow for parking on both sides as well as the fire engine.

When evaluating a private lane, there are three required elements. The development must
not impede the necessary access from adjoining properties as required by the Master
Transportation Plan. The zoning and General Land Use Plan have been discussed and the
City has a Master Transportation Plan that species where roads will be located, how wide
they will be, and building expectations for how many cars are expected.

As Riverton is nearly built-out and as the City has built roads on 4100 West, most of the
major roads have been completed. There are some roads on the Master Transportation
Plan for local streets. In Staff’s opinion, one of the three requirements has been met. Item A
specifies that the lot of record is pre-existing and has no frontage or adequate property to
construct a public road. Mr. Prestwich stated that the subject property does not have enough
frontage and is not large enough to construct a public street on nor can it be subdivided with
a public street. In Staff’s opinion, the application meets the criteria for a public lane and
recommended approval. It was noted that the development would likely have an HOA that
would maintain the private road and in subsequent approvals, it would be noted that that
should be accommodated.

Mr. Prestwich described how the right-of-way is designed. The ordinance specifies that one
or two lots must have 20 feet of paved surface. Three lots or more must have at least 25
feet of paved surface. In addition, where a development includes more than four lots or the
lane exceeds 150-feet in length, additional right-of-way width including sidewalk on at least
one side, may be required by the City up to the minimum pavement and sidewalk width of a
standard public residential right-of-way as determined in the Conditional Use Permit.
Mr. Prestwich stated that as the road gets longer and more lots are developed, there is an
increased impact. It comes down to the desired look of the neighborhood. The Code also
specifies that the City cannot require more than the standard. As 54 feet is the standard,
that would be the maximum required.

Mr. Prestwich explained that with administrative action items, Staff makes conditions and
includes them in the application. As he goes through the discussion, he will explain them
and identify which are standard and why they are included. It is also at the discretion of the
Commission to make any changes to the conditions that they see fit as long as they do not
interfere with the Code. The conditions were reviewed and discussed.

     C.    ZONE TEXT CHANGE, “ACCESSORY STRUCTURES” PROPOSED
           CHANGES TO RIVERTON CITY CODE 18.225. APPICANT, RIVERTON
           CITY.

Mr. Prestwich reported that “Accessory Structures” refers to all structures on a residential
zone that is not the primary residence. The most common example would be a shed. In
Riverton City where there are larger lots, there are large sheds, barns, and garages. With
the Council’s action to adopt an ADU Standard, people can build a ‘tiny house’ or an
accessory dwelling in the back yard. ‘Accessory Structures’ is probably the most frequently
changed ordinance in the City because of its impact on the neighbors. There are items that
the Council asked to be changed and on a previous ordinance change, there was some
ambiguity created that they would like to clear up.

The maximum height allowed in the Code is 20 feet unless the main dwelling is taller than
that. In such a case, it could be as high as the main house. That Code is proposed to be
changed to be a maximum height of 25 feet. If the height exceeds 20 feet, the setback
increases to 15 feet to protect the neighbors. Many of the one-quarter acre lots would not
be able to build a dwelling unit taller than 20 feet because of the setback requirements.
Mr. Carter added that the existing language will remain unchanged since the proposed
modification is only changing the maximum height to 10,000 square-foot lots. He preferred
something simpler. Twenty-five feet seemed like a reasonable number, but he questioned
why it would need to fluctuate based on the size of the main home.

The other change pertained to ADUs. There was a change in the section above that stated
that it did not need to be limited in size. For a time, it was limited to 50% of the main dwelling
but when that change occurred, they did not realize that it was in this section as well. It was
being stricken from the section to eliminate it. Because of the ambiguity, it has been
unenforceable. The hope was that cleaning up the language it can be enforced. Staff
recommended approval.

Chair Rushton asked about the restriction on ADUs being 50% and if by removing that
language, he could convert a large barn to an ADU. Mr. Prestwich confirmed that was the
case. He asked if he would be able to have an additional ADU if he has a one-third acre lot.
Mr. Prestwich confirmed that that was the case. He explained that because the City began
as rural with large lots and sometimes smaller homes, there is a lot of land that is
developable to homeowners. The State specifies that there must be more density and
‘middle-housing’. The City decided to allow ADUs to accommodate the State’s request.
One stipulation the Council put in place was that the property be owner-occupied.

In response to a scenario proposed by Commissioner Matheson regarding a child living on
the property, Mr. Carter stated that a child must be listed on the title of the property.

Commissioner Park asked for clarification on the number of ADUs allowed with respect to
lot size. Mr. Prestwich stated that there must be at least 10,000 square feet to do a detached
ADU. Only one ADU is allowed regardless of lot size. Mr. Carter added that a single-family
residential unit is defined as no more than four people who are unrelated by blood or
marriage residing in the same property. For example, four unrelated young men going to
college can live on the property and be considered a single-family residential use. One of
the concerns was how adding an ADU affects the definition of a residential unit. The Code
specifies that an ADU shall not affect the number of persons by blood or marriage who can
live on the property. Ideally, it is used as a mother-in-law unit.

Chair Rushton stated that anyone applying for an ADU must come before the Planning
Commission where specific questions can be raised.
