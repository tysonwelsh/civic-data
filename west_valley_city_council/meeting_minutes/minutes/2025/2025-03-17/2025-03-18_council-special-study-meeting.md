  Ifo Pili stated that they were hoping to receive as many questions and feedback as
  possible, particularly after sending out the program modifications for review. It was
  mentioned that several program modifications had been worked on and categorized,
  although not all were presented during the meeting. The General Plan Update,
  which Steve Pastorik is working on a grant for, was briefly discussed, with the
  understanding that it was still in progress and had different funding mechanisms.
  The Fire Department had requested 9 new firefighters and a new fire station, but it
  was noted that the Chief planned to apply for the SAFER grant to cover firefighter
  salaries for the next two years, which was not included in the current proposal.
  Personnel needs for the police department were addressed, focusing on the addition
  of 4 new officers over 3 years. Ifo stated that over the next month, the budget team
  planned to refine the proposal, incorporating feedback from the Council.

  Councilmember Huynh suggested hiring full time employees instead of
  subcontracting for park maintenance. Councilmember Harmon replied that 4
  employees couldn’t handle all the maintenance. Mayor Lang agreed and stated that
  she believes this will be a huge benefit and will increase appearance throughout the
  community.

  Councilmember Huynh expressed support for the hiring of new Police Officers but
  emphasized the need to see a clear, long-term plan. He requested a detailed
  projection for the next 2, 4, 6, and even 10 years, particularly regarding the hiring
  and use of 12 police officers. He wanted to understand what the department would
  do over the next several years and how they would achieve these goals.
  Councilmember Huynh referenced past issues where police officers were hired,
  trained, and then left after a short period, often for better-paying positions
  elsewhere. He noted that this resulted in wasted resources spent on training. He
  expressed a desire to see a clear goal and plan in place to avoid this situation moving
  forward.

       Ifo mentioned that there is a significant amount of data that can he provided in a
       quarterly report. When reviewing requests for additional officers, he pointed out
       that there are many variables to consider. Although call volumes have been
       increasing, they are relatively flat overall. However, when comparing the cost of
       service across cities of similar size, the data suggests that the department needs
       more officers, even after removing Salt Lake City as an outlier. The analysis
       indicated a need for 50 additional officers, excluding Salt Lake City and Provo,
       which have unique situations. He emphasized the importance of considering the
       department's specialties, such as SWAT and K9, when evaluating staffing needs.
       Ifo mentioned that Harold Moleni had been assigned to conduct an analysis on cost
       for service, which further supports the need for additional officers. He also
       discussed the large overtime budget, which often exceeds expectations. He
       acknowledged that while call volume may remain flat, the nature of police work
       has evolved significantly, with officers now taking on more responsibilities, such
       as those typically handled by social workers, and dealing with more paperwork.
       Additionally, maintaining CALEA certification is a challenging and resource-
       intensive process. Ifo Pili concluded by stating that the department could provide a
       report outlining their goals and achievements for further clarification.

  Councilmember Nordfelt stated that he did not see an issue with having a
  discrepancy between policy and code. He acknowledged that the department's
  practices might be more lenient than the code,but emphasized that the code
  provided necessary tools when enforcement was required. He clarified that he did
  not believe in proactively searching people's backyards for inoperable vehicles, but
  if such a situation became a nuisance and complaints were made, the code could be
  used to address the issue. Ifo replied that the department was seeking more

  flexibility in handling situations like inoperable vehicles. He explained that the
  current direction given to officers was that they could not act, even if there were
  complaints and the issue was visible from a neighbor’s yard. As a result, officers
  could only inform residents that they were unable to do anything because of the
  policy in place. Ifo pointed out that the policy restricts their ability to exercise
  judgment in these cases, even though the code allows for enforcement. He
  suggested that if the policy could be adjusted to provide officers with the discretion
  to act when it made sense, it would align better with the code. He emphasized that
  the policy currently contradicts the code, as it prevents enforcement of what the
  code considers a violation.

  Councilmember Huynh raised a concern regarding the enforcement of issues in
  backyards. He highlighted a past issue where complaints between neighbors led to
  an enforcement officer issuing a ticket for an issue visible from another yard. This
  situation escalated into a dispute, even involving the church, which became
  embroiled in the argument. Councilmember Huynh expressed that this kind of
  conflict is not ideal, as it creates unnecessary tension between neighbors and
  involves external parties, like the church, in what should be a straightforward
  enforcement issue. He stressed that the city should avoid becoming a mediator in
  personal disputes and should not allow such issues to escalate to that level. He
  emphasized that enforcement should not create such conflicts.

  Councilmember Harmon shared his concerns about the potential misuse of the code
  enforcement process, particularly when neighbors use it as a means of retaliation.
  He explained that some residents may report their neighbors for issues like vehicles
  in disrepair out of spite, leveraging the code as a tool to get back at others.
  Councilmember Harmon acknowledged the difficulty of entering backyards and
  said he didn’t like the idea of using backyard inspections as a way for neighbors to
  seek revenge. He suggested that enforcement should only happen when issues are
  visible from public spaces, like the street or a patio. He also pointed out that if a
  neighbor has a privacy fence that makes it hard to see over, the city shouldn’t get
  involved. However, if a problem is clearly visible, such as unsightly vehicles
  without coverings or poorly maintained yards, he believed the city should step in.
  Councilmember Harmon emphasized the balance between respecting privacy and
  addressing real concerns, suggesting that enforcement should focus on visible
  nuisances rather than entering private yards unnecessarily.

  Cindy Wood agreed with Councilmember Harmon’s perspective, sharing that she

  he highlighted that even if a violation is only visible over a fence, it would still be

  Council agreed.

  Steve pointed out the main difference regarding landscaping enforcement.
  Currently, the policy only enforces grass or weeds that are over 6 inches in height.
  However, the code is more specific and sets a minimum standard for landscaping,
  requiring things like trees and a certain amount of live plant material. This creates
  a discrepancy between the enforcement policy and the code.

  Councilmember Harmon discussed the issue of enforcing landscaping codes,
  particularly around weeds. He mentioned that while he quickly removes weeds in
  his own lawn, some people’s lawns are primarily weeds instead of grass. The
  question arose about how to enforce the code in these cases—whether it would
  require them to remove all the weeds and plant grass. He highlighted that this is
  why the policy shifted to focusing on maintaining the lawn, regardless of whether
  it’s grass or weeds, as long as it's kept under 6 inches and green.

  Steve brought up the issue of "decorative dirt," noting that under current policy, it’s
  technically permissible for a yard to consist entirely of dirt and still be considered
  in compliance with the code. This raised concerns about enforcement, as it could
  be difficult to distinguish between acceptable decorative dirt and an unkempt yard.
  He added that while the policy allows for decorative dirt, the lack of clear guidelines
  on how it should be used may lead to issues. He also mentioned that some people
  have used this to avoid having to plant grass, which could potentially lead to
  inconsistencies in how yard maintenance is enforced across the city.

  Councilmember Harmon asked for Tumi’s thoughts regarding administrative
  citations. Tumi discussed the process of issuing citations for property violations,
  particularly with issues like tall weeds. He explained the multi-step process:
      1. Initial Warning: When a violation is observed, a warning is issued, giving
          the property owner two weeks to resolve the issue.
      2. Notice of Violation: If the issue isn’t resolved, a formal notice of violation
          is given, allowing an additional two weeks to fix the problem.
      3. Court Action: If the violation persists, it escalates to court, which can take
          about 1 to 1.5 months. After that, the property owner can appeal, further
          delaying resolution.
      4. Fines: During this process, fines can accumulate daily for ongoing
          violations, which can lead to higher costs for the property owner over time.

  Tumi noted that using citations (tickets) tends to yield faster results, with people
  being more willing to comply quickly compared to waiting for the violation process
  to play out over a long period. He acknowledged that while warnings are always
  the first step, citations are an important tool in ensuring compliance, particularly in
  situations where timely action is necessary (like for safety concerns such as weeds
  before a holiday like the 4th of July).

  Mayor and Council agreed to allow administrative citations.
