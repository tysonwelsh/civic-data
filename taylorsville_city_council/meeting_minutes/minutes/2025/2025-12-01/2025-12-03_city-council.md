Sergeant Fox from West Valley Police, had cooked Thanksgiving breakfast and dinner for years
for public servants working on the holiday. She emphasized this wasn't a budget item but done
"out of the goodness of their hearts."

3. REPORTS

3.1 Wasatch Front Waste & Recycling District — Evan Tyrrell

Evan Tyrrell, the new general manager of Wasatch Front Waste and Recycling District who had
been with WFWRD for "a little over 3 months," presented an extensive report on the District’s
services and initiatives. He began by explaining their base residential service of $26 per month
included weekly curbside trash and recycling, the SCRP (Seasonal Container Reservation)
program, landfill voucher program, central glass drop-off locations, seasonal services including
the recent leaf bag collection and upcoming Christmas tree collection in January 2026, and various
educational outreach programs.

Mr. Tyrrell highlighted that Taylorsville was their second-highest household service area with
13,789 homes serviced as of September 30th. He detailed the tonnage hauled from Taylorsville
year-to-date: 14,000 tons of curbside waste (equivalent to 28 million pounds of trash), noting this
was down from 15,000 tons last year. He explained recycling was split between the Waste
Management and Rocky Mountain Recycling Materials Recovery Facility based on geographic
area and cost considerations.

When discussing recycling contamination rates, Chair Harker asked about the reported 17.08%
decrease, questioning whether residents were "recycling cleaner" or doing worse. Mr. Tyrrell
acknowledged an error in his presentation slides but confirmed all communities including
Taylorsville had seen a decrease in contamination, with Taylorsville dropping from about 19% to
17%. Council Member Cochran asked when stamping tops of recycling cans began, with
discussion revealing newer cans had instructional labels while older cans did not.

Mr. Tyrrell presented new educational materials including a green waste can guide finalized a
month ago and a recycle can guide completed the previous week, emphasizing the importance of
not bagging recyclables or green waste. He explained that the tiered SCRP program had reduced
repeat users from 34% in 2023 and 2024 to 11% in 2025, with plans to increase service capacity
to better meet demand. He noted Taylorsville had received nearly 1,200 SCRP containers in 2025,
serving about 9% of homes and hauling 1,130 tons of material.

Mr. Tyrrell then announced the transition to monthly billing beginning February 2026, explaining
it would help customers budget better and provide more consistent cash flow for the district. He
detailed new fees for 2026 including a late set-out return pickup fee to deter missed pickups and
recover costs, and a recycling contamination fee after three documented recurrences. He noted
contamination fees to just one facility had cost about $64,000 in extra tipping fees year-to-date.

When Chair Harker asked when next year's SCRP schedule would be available, he indicated March
or April at the latest, explaining they were still strategizing recruitment and planning for the

increased container allocations.

5. PLANNING MATTERS

5.1 Discussion Only Regarding a Proposed General Plan Map Amendment,
Zoning Map Amendment and Zoning Text Amendment for Approximately
13.248 Acres of Property at 4271 South, 4225 South, and 4273 South 2700 West
from Employment Center/Employment Mix and Professional Office to
Residential High Intensity and Site-Specific Development Residential (SSD-R)
— Terryne Bergeson

Staff planner Terryne Bergeson presented the applications submitted by Casey Forbush and Chase
Andrizzi of DAI Utah, with Joe Salisbury present as their representative. She explained this was
the first phase requiring city council approval for regulatory standards before development could
proceed. The properties, located behind the state office complex as part of the Beltway West
subdivision approved in 2021, were originally intended for office development but now proposed
for 222 townhome units.

Ms. Bergeson explained that the current future land use map designated the properties as
employment mix and employment center, allowing for 2-story office buildings with potential for
up to 10 stories after zoning review. The applicants requested changing to residential use with
townhomes at 16-17 units per acre, higher than the current code maximum of 16 units per acre.
The project would provide missing middle housing, with townhomes currently representing only
2% of Taylorsville's housing stock compared to 8.5% countywide.

The primary discussion centered on a proposed median for the new public road entrance from 2700
West. Planning Commissioners had requested a "sense of arrival," leading applicants to propose
the landscaped median with artistic lighting. Staff recommended against the median due to long-
term maintenance concerns for snow plowing and repaving, though acknowledged the lighting and
landscaping could achieve the desired effect without the median. Ms. Bergeson pointed out that
the city would not be responsible for maintaining the median itself—just the surrounding
roadways.

Chair Harker loved the look of the median, pointing out that it was a unique feature in the city.
Council Members Cochran and Barbieri spoke in favor of both the median and the signage
suggestions.

Chair Harker asked City Engineer Ben White if the 20°’ roadway around the median would
accommodate snowplows. However, Mr. White responded from the audience and his answer was
not picked up by the microphone.

Chair Harker invited the applicant’s representative to address the City Council if he wished to.

Joe Salisbury from DAT addressed the council, emphasizing that the median served dual purposes:
it created a sense of arrival and provided safety for children and pedestrians in the area by acting
as a traffic calming device. He described the entire project as very upscale, attracting "renters by
choice" with amenities like valet waste service and maintenance-free living. Salisbury stated the
$100,000+ median investment would "set a precedent" and do what had not yet been done in
Taylorsville.

Council Member Burgess remained the sole dissenter, expressing concern about future
maintenance costs to the city, though he acknowledged that it would slow traffic. Chair Harker
pointed out that median maintenance would remain the property owner's responsibility, not the

city's.

The applicant also presented a solution for shared signage with the existing Beltway West
development, showing a monument sign design that both current property owners had agreed upon.

Ms. Bergeson said she had prepared a recommended motion, although it would need to be adjusted
if they wished to approve the median. Council Member Cochran asked city attorney Tracy Cowdell
if they could still make a motion when formal action had not been agendized. Mr. Cowdell
responded that it was acceptable to move to return the matter for formal action at a future meeting.

Chair Harker polled each council member. Four members (Harker, Cochran, Barbieri, and
Knudsen) supported including the median, while Council Member Burgess opposed it but
supported everything else. The council agreed to return December 17th for formal action on all
amendments as well as a development agreement.

7.1 Discussion Regarding the Frequency of Reports/Presentations to the City

Council by Outside Entities Providing Services to Taylorsville

During the November 19" meeting the Council decided to schedule this discussion regarding the
frequency they wished to hear presentations from various committees and contractors in the
coming year. Since then, the city recorder had sent each of them a list of these organizations and a
tentative schedule of reports based on what had been done in 2025 and earlier. After some
discussion, the council decided that semi-annual reports from Unified Fire, Animal Services, and
Salt Lake County Public Works were sufficient. They had agreed earlier in the month to reduce
WFWRD reports from quarterly to semi-annual as well.

Regarding internal volunteer committees, there was a fairly extensive discussion regarding those
periodic reports. Mayor Overson pointed out that though the council representatives might be quite
familiar with committee activities, the average resident was not. These reports were an opportunity
for community members to learn what each committee was doing. It was determined that each
committee would be asked to report twice per year. The victim advocates would report as required
in any grant agreements.
