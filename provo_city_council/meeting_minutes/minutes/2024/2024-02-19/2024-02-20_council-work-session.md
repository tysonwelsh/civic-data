Item 1: Discussion Regarding Ordinance Text Amendment for Transferable
Development Rights (TDR) Overlay Zone (Citywide Application,
PLOTA20230219) 0:06:00

Presentation
Bill Peperone, Development Services Director, presented the proposal for the adoption of a
Transferable Development Rights (TDR) Overlay Zone. He explained that the ordinance aimed
to either solve an existing problem or create a new opportunity, particularly regarding vacant,
geologically sensitive lots. Director Peperone outlined that lots would be classified into three
tiers, with density calculations varying for each. A total of 168 dwelling units would be
categorized, with 392 dwelling units transferred to West Provo to prevent infrastructure strain.
He clarified that the creation of these overlay zones would involve a zone change.

Discussion

Councilor Becky Bogdin expressed concerns about smaller homes on the West side of Provo,
particularly the availability of Accessory Dwelling Units (ADUs) and potential duplex
conversions. She suggested that smaller homes might remain on the East side. Director Peperone
addressed Councilor Bogdin’s concerns, citing costs associated with land, infrastructure, and
development. He mentioned that developments on the West side would likely involve the Provo
Housing Authority. Despite available 6,000-8,000 square foot lots, Director Peperone anticipated
larger homes, reducing the likelihood of an ADU problem. He also noted two significant
upcoming projects.Councilor Bogdin further expressed concern over potential future impacts and
trends of pushing developments to the West side, referencing rising home prices over the last
three years.

Councilor Rachel Whipple asked if the TDR zone would apply solely to the West side. Director
Peperone confirmed that the ordinance allows flexibility, stating that control over zone
placement is maintained unless specified by the ordinance itself. Brian Jones, City Attorney,
added that the ordinance only creates the zone but does not apply it to any area unless requested.

Councilor George Handley (by phone) asked Director Peperone to explain the logic behind the
three-tier classification. Gordon Haight, Public Works Director, explained that tiers were
developed in consultation with geotechnical engineers, prioritizing lots most affected by
geological risks, such as landslides and rockfall.

Councilor Craig Christensen asked about the economic impact on sellers of geologically
sensitive lots. Director Peperone responded that the declining value of these hard-to-develop lots
made the ordinance appealing to developers, based on positive feedback from the market.

Councilor Rachel Whipple requested copies of any changes made to the ordinance, and Director
Peperone confirmed that it was a work in progress.

Item 3: Discussion Regarding a Resolution Approving the Transfer of $90,658
from the Legacy CIP to the General Fund for License Plate Readers (24-006)
0:18:59

Presentation
Sandy Bussio, Parking Program Manager, presented a resolution requesting the transfer of
$90,658 from the Legacy CIP to the General Fund to cover the cost of two Automatic License
Plate Readers (ALPRs) and tablet computers for the Parking Division. These funds would be
used to outfit two unused vehicles with the ALPRs, which are needed for parking enforcement
across various areas in the city.

Discussion
Sandy Bussio reminded the Council that this expenditure had been discussed and agreed upon
during the previous meeting, as a step to address vehicle updates. The ALPRs would replace one
current vehicle system and add an additional unit, bringing the total number of ALPR-equipped
vehicles to five. The ALPRs are primarily used in residential parking areas, timed parking
enforcement, and areas like Center Street and Slate Canyon. Bussio explained that the system
provides valuable benefits, including identifying stolen vehicles and eliminating the need for
chalking tires, thus saving time and increasing staff efficiency.

Councilor Becky Bogdin asked for clarification on the function of the ALPRs. Bussio responded
that the ALPRs assist in tracking parked vehicles, notifying if a vehicle is stolen, and providing
daily monitoring without the need for manual tire chalking. Bussio mentioned that without these
tools, the Parking Division would need to hire three additional staff members, particularly for
challenging shifts, like overnight hours from 10 PM to 7 AM.

Councilor Craig Christensen asked about potential staffing savings and was informed that the
ALPRs significantly reduce the need for additional manpower. Bussio provided examples of
current parking challenges in areas like downtown and University Garden, where these tools are
especially helpful.

Councilor Gary Garrett asked if ALPR-equipped vehicles are authorized to issue citations.
Bussio confirmed that parking enforcement staff are responsible for issuing tickets, and clarified

Item 4: Discussion on Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Requirements (24-025)
0:28:30

Presentation

Abi Macabee, Council Office Intern, presented a discussion on electric vehicle (EV)
infrastructure, focusing on the background, purpose, costs, demand in Utah, and state and city
goals. She clarified that this was not a proposal but an informational presentation. Inspired by
Salt Lake City’s efforts to reduce gas emissions by 80% by 2040, Macabee discussed a new
ordinance requiring one EV charging station per 25 parking spaces in new construction or major
renovations. She emphasized the cost benefits of preparing for EV infrastructure during new
construction ($1,000) versus retrofitting ($3,600). Citing data from the Utah State Tax
Commission, Macabee noted that while EVs currently represent less than 1% of vehicles in Utah,
the U.S. Department of Energy projects that 30% of vehicles will be electric by 2030.

Macabee highlighted that 6 out of 10 Americans hesitate to buy EVs due to a lack of charging
stations. She acknowledged concerns about whether current demand justifies such infrastructure
requirements and the potential impact on rent prices. However, she referred to a manager from
Salt Lake City who reported no significant issues with implementing EV-ready requirements.
Macabee also discussed other cities with similar demographics to Provo that have adopted
similar measures. Additionally, she mentioned state legislation supporting the electrification of
transportation, including Utah’s allocation of $4.9 million to incentivize businesses and
organizations.

Power Department Opinion
Travis Ball, a representative from the Power Department expressed support for the idea of
planning for EV infrastructure but questioned the need for mandates, citing the natural market
progression toward EV adoption. They expressed concern that mandates could unnecessarily
increase housing costs.

Discussion
Councilor Rachel Whipple inquired about potential supply chain issues. Travis Ball, Power
Department representative, responded that infrastructure upgrades, particularly transformers,
could pose challenges for developers.

Councilor George Handley shared his thoughts, noting that while resistance to new development
requirements is typical, it might stem from misunderstandings. He emphasized the importance of
including at least Level I EV charging infrastructure in new developments and voiced skepticism
about claims of unaffordable costs. He stressed the long-term implications of failing to install EV
infrastructure in new buildings, calling it a "60-year decision." Handley suggested further

exploration of policy solutions, including cost and feasibility, to ensure the city sets reasonable
and meaningful EV infrastructure goals.

Councilor Gary Garrett supported Handley’s approach, advocating for incentives or partial
mandates. He inquired whether developers were voluntarily installing EV stations. Bill Peperone,
Development Services Director, replied that developers could be encouraged through code
amendments, rather than mandates. Director Peperone suggested staff return with a code
amendment reflecting Council priorities.

Councilor Craig Christensen, speaking, expressed discomfort with mandates and preferred a
market-driven approach, favoring incentives.

Motions

Item 6: Discussion on Fiscal Year 2025 General Fund Revenue Estimates and
City Debt (24-007) 1:20:34

Kelsey Zarbock, Budget Officer, presented on the FY25 General Fund revenue estimates,
highlighting significant changes in revenue streams. She noted that tax revenues, particularly
sales tax, were showing the most fluctuation. The revenue from scooters was uncertain for this
year, while Fire Department revenues were up due to expected charges to the airport for fire
department overtime. Police revenues remained flat, and intergovernmental revenues increased
slightly, possibly due to state liquor taxes. Zarbock reported an anticipated deficit of $850,000
but offset by $300,000 in expected revenues, resulting in a net deficit of approximately
$556,236. She stressed that these estimates are fluid and could change rapidly.

Administrative Services Director John Borget commented that overall city revenues were around
$80 million, noting that while revenue growth was leveling out, the situation was not dire, but
challenging due to a slower growth rate. He explained to Councilor Becky Bogdin how state
taxes are distributed, emphasizing that although Utah generally outperforms the national
economy, the state and city were not experiencing the same level of growth as in previous years.
Sales tax was identified as particularly volatile. Bogdin inquired whether the upcoming Target
store opening would increase tax revenue, to which Director Borget expressed hope for a
positive impact, though not a solution to all fiscal challenges.

Dan Follett, Finance Director, addressed the Council regarding city debt. He explained that the
city's debt was primarily fixed-rate, offering stability, and that the interest rates were generally
favorable. Bogdin asked about the airport bond and whether it was tied to an interfund loan with

Item 7: A discussion regarding workforce retention and pay grades (24-007)
1:48:53

Discussion on Workforce Retention and Pay Grades (24-007)

Presentation

Daniel Softley, Director of Human Resources, presented on workforce retention and pay grades.
He began by emphasizing the exceptional talent and expertise among Provo City employees and
discussed the importance of pay grades. Job classifications are used to compare Provo’s positions
with those of other agencies during market studies. These classifications are available in the city
code and published online. A key aspect of the HR department’s work is the Annual Pay Grade
Study, which ensures that Provo’s pay grades remain competitive with similar public sector
positions.

Discussion

Councilor Rachel Whipple inquired about comparing analyst positions. Director Softley
explained how Provo’s classifications differ from those at the state level. Regarding EMTs, he
noted that in cases where there is insufficient local data for market comparisons, the state data is
used. Councilor Craig Christensen asked if pay and salary information must be posted publicly.
Director Softley responded that transparency is a core value, and publicizing pay information not
only adheres to transparency but also reduces the number of inquiries.

Director Softley also mentioned TechNet, an independent third-party organization that
benchmarks pay data across positions. Provo uses this data to ensure competitiveness, but
Director Softley also compares pay data from organizations not included in TechNet to provide a
comprehensive analysis. He tracks turnover, noting potential trouble areas such as positions with
consistent vacancies.

Councilor Becky Bogdin asked about the Transparency Utah platform. Director Softley
confirmed that Provo City complies with this requirement and added that he performs additional


Provo City Municipal Council Work Meeting – February 20, 2024                                 Page 7 of 8
research to ensure accuracy. He then explained the difference between cost of living adjustments
(COLA) and benefits, clarifying that at Provo City, COLA applies strictly to salary, while
benefits, including medical, are considered separately. Director Softley also noted that
department heads now have the authority to adjust employees’ pay steps independently, a
process introduced about a year ago.

Director Softley continued by discussing employee benefits, explaining that Provo City
transitioned from a low-deductible health plan to a high-deductible plan, educating employees on
managing medical expenses. This change allowed the city to contribute extra Health Savings
Account (HSA) dollars. Council Chair Katrice MacKay asked about opting out of Social
Security, and Director Softley explained that certain government agencies, such as public safety,
have their own retirement plans. Brian Jones, City Attorney, noted that in these cases, the city
contributes the 6.2% Social Security tax directly to employees' retirement.

Councilor Bogdin also asked about sick leave and vacation policies. Director Softley
summarized current leave policies and highlighted a recent change to bereavement leave, which
now allows employees to use three days of bereavement leave for an immediate family
member’s passing without affecting their sick leave.

Director Softley concluded by sharing retention statistics. The HR department conducts exit
interviews, speaks with supervisors, and tracks employee turnover, particularly when employees
leave for similar positions elsewhere, in order to understand and address the reasons behind their
departure.
