39          Rules of Order and Procedure Discussion
40
41   Kevin Hicks, City Manager, acknowledged that individual meetings were held with each
42   Councilmember over the past week to review edits. He said this work session discussion was
43   meant to provide an opportunity for additional dialogue. He stated that the plan was to bring the
44   document before the Council at the next meeting in August for approval.
45
46   Councilmember Buroker proposed modifying the wording from "public comments for 15
47   minutes" to "at least 15 minutes" to provide flexibility, or alternatively, removing the time limit
48   entirely.
49
50   Ryan Carter, City Attorney, stated that the Riverton City Council follows Robert's Rules of Order,
51   which includes a motion to suspend the rules under special circumstances, thereby allowing for
     Page | 2 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     July 16, 2024




 1   more than 15 minutes of public comments. He noted that this existing procedure already
 2   provides the needed flexibility. However, he also expressed openness to making the change if
 3   the Council prefers.
 4
 5   Mayor Staggs responded by noting that the discretion to extend the comment period lies with
 6   the chair. He explained that typically, if a large crowd is anticipated, comment cards are made
 7   available. This helps manage the number of speakers by requiring them to be called upon,
 8   which often results in fewer comments. He also mentioned that if a Councilmember senses
 9   significant interest from the public, they can suggest having a few spokespersons speak on
10   behalf of larger groups, allowing more time for those speakers while preventing repetitive
11   comments. This approach provides flexibility within the established rules.
12
13   Councilmember McCay expressed concern that a 15-minute limit for public comments might be
14   too short, especially for significant or contentious issues. She noted that with a three-minute
15   limit per speaker, only five residents could speak within that time frame, which may not be
16   sufficient for important topics. She cited the example of the recycling issue, suggesting that had
17   residents chosen to speak at the meeting instead of sending emails, the 15-minute limit would
18   have been inadequate. She supported increasing the public comment period to 30 minutes, with
19   the understanding that it could be reduced if necessary, to ensure residents feel heard.
20
21   Councilmembers discussed and agreed to remove the 15 minute time limit from the Rules.
22
23   Councilmember McCay raised a point about retaining the last item in the Rules which addresses
24   the involvement of the city manager or department heads in local elections. She suggested that
25   this section could be condensed but emphasized the importance of keeping a portion of it,
26   especially the sentence stating that Councilmembers should not request these officials to
27   participate in campaigns.
28
29   Mr. Carter noted that while City employees are free to engage in political activities during their
30   personal time, without using City resources, the City does not have the authority to prohibit such
31   involvement entirely. He acknowledged that while it might be permissible for the Council to
32   express a commitment to not expecting employees to participate in their campaigns, enforcing a
33   prohibition against employees' political involvement outside of work could expose the City to
34   legal risks.
35
36   Councilmembers Haymond and Buroker did not want to retain the last paragraph based on the
37   feedback from the City Attorney.

39            Cargo Container Discussion
40
41   Mayor Staggs described a previous situation where complaints from residents led to an
42   ordinance restricting the placement of cargo containers. However, some residents now view
43   cargo containers as a cost-effective storage solution, especially if placed behind fences in their
44   backyards. He suggested that a solution might involve revising the ordinance to allow cargo
45   containers if certain conditions or materials are used to improve their appearance.
46
47   Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, explained that while the ordinance prohibits
48   the use of cargo containers as standalone structures, they can be used if they are integrated
49   into accessory buildings that meet specific design standards. The ordinance currently requires
50   accessory structures to match the materials used on the main dwelling, which means cargo
51   containers, with their typical corrugated metal exteriors, are not allowed as external materials.
     Page | 3 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     July 16, 2024




 1   However, if a cargo container is used as the interior framework of an accessory building and
 2   covered with compliant materials, it can be treated as a standard accessory structure, subject to
 3   the same regulations as other buildings of similar size.
 4
 5   Mr. Lethbridge highlighted that this approach has been effective in addressing complaints and
 6   providing a cost-effective storage solution for residents. The City’s webpage provides
 7   information on how to comply with these regulations. The state recently increased the threshold
 8   for requiring a building permit from 200 to 350 square feet, which has simplified the process for
 9   using cargo containers as accessory structures by eliminating the need for a permanent
10   foundation.
11
12   Dana Long, resident, provided feedback on the use of cargo containers, particularly in
13   agricultural settings. She expressed concerns about the practicality and cost-effectiveness of
14   applying siding to cargo containers, noting that:
15
16            1.        Material Durability: The material used for siding is of a lower grade and is
17                      susceptible to damage from livestock, such as horses, which can easily eat
18                      through or scratch it.
19            2.        Cost of Siding: The cost of applying siding, estimated at around $4,000, is not
20                      considered cost-effective, especially for those with larger properties.
21            3.        Water Flow and Versatility: The design of the boundary on the bottom of the
22                      container can obstruct water flow, which is problematic for properties that use
23                      flood irrigation. She highlighted that cargo containers' current design allows for
24                      water to flow underneath, which helps with irrigation and prevents water damage.
25            4.        Safety and Environmental Concerns: Cargo containers provide a safe and fire-
26                      resistant option for storing materials and protecting livestock. The containment of
27                      fire within a cargo container prevents it from spreading to other structures and
28                      potentially harming animals.
29
30   Wayne Withers, resident, shared his experience with cargo containers on his two-acre lot. He
31   highlighted the following points:
32
33            1.        Cost of Removal: He faced significant expenses, totaling $9,000 for a trailer to
34                      move the contents of the container and an additional $2,000 to have the
35                      container hauled away. This cost was incurred after receiving a 30-day notice to
36                      remove the container.
37            2.        Inconsistent Enforcement: He noted that a neighbor with a similar container on
38                      the same street had not been contacted about their container, despite its
39                      presence being visible from the street. He felt that this inconsistency in
40                      enforcement was unfair.
41            3.        Visibility and Complaints: His container had been positioned out of view behind
42                      trees and had not generated complaints. He questioned the fairness of the
43                      regulations, especially since his container had been in place for years without
44                      issues.
45
46   Councilmember McCay expressed growing concern about the regulation of cargo containers,
47   particularly in relation to larger lots and properties with animal rights. She acknowledged that
48   containers should not be placed in front of houses but could be suitable for larger lots. She
49   suggested that flexibility in the policy could address concerns about aesthetics and practicality,
50   such as allowing containers if they are properly screened or surrounded by other materials. She
51   also anticipated challenges for code enforcement if the regulations were too strict, particularly
     Page | 4 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     July 16, 2024




 1   given the number of containers already in use and the potential burden on residents to comply
 2   with stringent aesthetic requirements. She recommended exploring policies used by other cities
 3   and potentially incorporating requirements that could accommodate the practical benefits of
 4   containers while addressing aesthetic concerns. This might include options for screening or
 5   surrounding containers in a way that aligns with the look of the property.
 6
 7   Mr. Lethbridge agreed to look into how other cities handle similar situations and bring this
 8   information back to the Council in August.

29            Ordinance No. 24-13 – School Code Text, Amending Section 18-195-060(3),
30            regulating the requirements for private or quasi-public schools, Amendments
31            proposed by Riverton City
32
33   Mr. Lethbridge discussed a text amendment concerning private or quasi-public schools outside
34   of the school district's jurisdiction. The current code requires a minimum property size of three
35   acres for such applications, a rule established years ago due to traffic and circulation challenges
36   with a previous large-scale school project. The proposed amendment seeks to reduce this
37   minimum to one acre. This proposal emerged from the Planning Commission's detailed
38   examination and acknowledgment of property interest within the City.
39
40   The amendment specifies that while one acre is the new minimum, more acreage may be
41   required for larger facilities. It maintains that private or quasi-public schools would need a
42   conditional use permit in residential zones and would only be allowed on collector or arterial
43   roadways as defined by the City's transportation master plan. This measure aims to prevent
44   these schools from impacting residential neighborhoods by ensuring they are situated on roads
45   designed to handle additional traffic.
46
47   Further, the amendment allows the City to impose additional restrictions based on traffic, noise,
48   parking, emergency response, stormwater, and the school's impact on the surrounding
49   neighborhood. The Planning Commission's thorough review led to these stipulations, ensuring
50   the City's infrastructure and residents' needs are adequately protected while providing
51   opportunities for private and quasi-public schools. The Planning Commission recommended
     Page | 9 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     July 16, 2024




 1   approval of these amendments.
 2   Mayor Staggs called for public comments; there being none, he closed the Public Hearing.

48            Ordinance No. 24-15 – Amending Riverton City Code Title 8, Adding Chapter 45
49            (Prohibited Camping)
50
51   Mr. Carter noted that the ordinance had passed through rigorous legal scrutiny, giving
     Page | 10 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     July 16, 2024




 1   confidence in its language and its ability to withstand future challenges. He explained that the
 2   ordinance prohibits overnight camping on public property, such as parks, while allowing
 3   camping on private property. This measure aims to prevent homeless people from
 4   congregating in public spaces, addressing part of the broader homeless issue. Riverton City
 5   has already encountered instances of homeless individuals using public restrooms in parks,
 6   and the ordinance would provide law enforcement with the necessary legal foundation to
 7   address these situations effectively.
 8
 9   Councilmember Pierucci expressed appreciation for the ordinance draft, acknowledging the
10   efforts to address the issue of camping on public property in a humane manner. He
11   emphasized that public spaces should be open, safe, clean, and accessible for their intended
12   uses. He noted that, in his district, there have been recent challenges with encampments
13   along the Creek Trail, which have affected neighbors.
14
15   Councilmember McCay raised a concern about the provision of the ordinance, which states
16   that the City shall inform a local agency providing social services to homeless individuals
17   about the campsite location. She expressed doubt about whether these agencies would have
18   the resources to respond to such notifications effectively. She suggested that instead of
19   relying on social service agencies to come out to the campsite, it might be more practical to
20   provide the individuals with resources directly or offer transportation to a homeless shelter.
21
22   Chief Shane Taylor, Riverton Police Department, acknowledged the increase in homeless
23   populations but noted that the City has not been overwhelmed by the issue. He expressed
24   support for the ordinance, emphasizing that it provides necessary enforcement tools. He also
25   highlighted that, in the worst-case scenario, the class B misdemeanor provision could be used
26   to address violations, potentially leading to further support and resources for affected
27   individuals.
