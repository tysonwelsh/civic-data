3. Kenison Subdivision; 6634 South 2700 West; Major Subdivision Plat (2 lots on .879
acres); RR-.5C Zone; Ivan Kenison (applicant) [#31809; parcel 21-21-327-014]
Ivan Kenison, applicant, stated that the property had been in the family for many years and was
originally used for agricultural purposes. There is a home on the front of the property and now they
need to build a house on the back of the property for their son so he can be nearby.
Larry Gardner said Brower Circle is a public street that was dedicated in the 1970’s. Other properties
that access Brower Circle are a daycare center and homes. The new lot will have its access from
Brower Circle. The original property was one acre in area, but with the widening of 2700 West the city
took .12 of a acre. The proposed lots are less than the 20,000 square feet required, but Mr. Kenison
received a variance from the Board of Adjustment that approved the smaller lot sizes. The layout meets
public access, frontage, and utility requirements. The reason this is considered a major subdivision is
that a sidewalk is required to be installed and dedicated on the south side of Brower Circle.
Based on the analysis and findings contained in the staff report, staff recommended that the Planning
Commission approve the Final Subdivision Plat for Kenison two lot subdivision located at 6634 South
2700 West.

4. The Venue Event Center; 1609 West 9000 South; Conditional Use Permit for a Reception

Center; P-C Zone; Angel Aguilar (applicant) [#32092; parcel 27-03-401-047]
Angel Aguilar, applicant, gave an overview of the request for a reception center. He has been in the
business since he was 12 years old, working as a DJ. He has worked in many venues in the Salt Lake
Valley and now his family would like to have their own event space. The Venue can help other
businesses in the area by subcontracting servers, catering of all types, drink services, bartending
services, and security. They will need one security officer for every 50 people in attendance. Capacity
of the space is 200 people, which they would probably never reach. Of all the spaces they have looked
at, he likes this one the most since it is not close to residential uses. The biggest concern most people
have is with loud music, so being away from residential is the best situation.
Ammon Allen asked what measures they will take to mitigate noise to the neighboring businesses.
Angel Aguilar stated that they will create a sound wall next to the restaurant, which stays open until 10
p.m. The other neighbor is a pet groomer that closes at 5 p.m. and is not open on the weekend.
Megan Jensen said proposed hours of operation are Sunday through Thursday from 11:00 a.m. to
12:00 a.m. with music ending at 11:45 p.m. and on Friday and Saturday from 11:00 a.m. to 1:00 a.m.
With music ending at 12:45 a.m. Most events will be on the weekends. Maximum capacity is about
200 people and there is ample parking that is shared for River Pointe Plaza. Staff recommended some
conditions of approval to mitigate potential incidents that have occurred at similar event centers in

5. Taylor Farms Rezone; 7220 South 1300 West; Rezone 9.75 acres from A-5 (Agriculture 5-
acre lots) Zone to R-1-8 (Single-family residential 8,000 square foot minimum lots) Zone;
Utah Land Co./Robbie Thomson (applicant) [#32010; parcels 21-27-276-021, 022]
Robbie Thomson, the applicant representing the family, said the Taylor family has owned the property
for at least four generations and maybe as much as six. The Taylor’s originally had 60 acres and most
of it was sold to build the housing that currently surrounds the subject parcel. The veterinarian shop
was built in the early 1970’s and it will continue to operate. It has taken the family a long time to come
to the decision to sell the property. It is difficult to farm property that is surrounded by homes, and if it
isn’t farmed then it loses greenbelt status. The city just adopted a general plan map that designated the
property as low density. The proposed density of 3.79 units per acre meets the general plan. The
existing lots to the north average 6500 square feet, and those to the west are 4500 square feet. The
homes to the south are approximately 8,000 square feet. The proposed lots will range from 8,000 to
15,000 square feet in size and are bigger than the surrounding lots. The request meets the goals,
objectives, and policies of the general plan. The density and single-family homes meet the general
plan. When the property is built out it will create connectivity with the two stubs streets to the south,
which will provide improved emergency response times to the existing neighborhoods. There is
adequate capacity for services in terms of sewer, water, and utilities to serve the development.
Megan Jensen said the application tonight is for a rezoning of the property, and the subdivision layout
is not being approved at this time. The combined acreage for the two parcels being rezoned is 9.75
acres. The animal clinic is currently operating as a legal, non-conforming use and will continue to do
so. The land use designation is low density residential, and the concept plan shows that the R-1-8
standards could be met. The proposed zone matches or is less dense than the surrounding zones and is
consistent with the public services master plan.
Based on the analysis contained in the report, staff recommended that the Planning Commission give a
recommendation of approval (positive recommendation) to the City Council for the proposed Rezone.

Trish Hatch asked if the vet clinic property would fall under the R-1-8 standards once the clinic
decides not to continue.

Megan Jensen said that was correct.

George Winn indicated that the Planning Commission was forwarded comments from three
individuals. One was in favor of the rezoning and two were against.

Geoge Winn opened the public hearing.

Bryan Larson, West Jordan resident, was opposed to the rezoning because this is one of the few green
spaces left in West Jordan. He listed his concerns with potential environmental impacts on water
quality, air quality, wildlife habitat, storm water management, and erosion control. He wondered if the
snakes in the area were of a protected variety. West Jordan has the authority to plan, zone, and regulate
land use in promoting the community’s health, safety, and welfare, so the city should make sure that
they are considering the impacts to current and future residents. He felt the rezoning was short-sighted
and they should consider alternatives for a sustainable development that respects the land’s agricultural
heritage and promotes the well-being of future generations.

Reed Hart, West Jordan resident, understood that the land use plan calls for residential, but the R-1-8
zone does not protect the best interest of the neighborhood and allows for uses that have a negative
effect. He distributed a photo taken from his back porch that has a mountain view. The R-1-8 zone
would allow for a 35-foot-tall home at 25 feet from his property, which would block the view and
affect his property value. The proposal does not minimize the impacts to existing neighborhoods, so it
does not align with the guiding principles of the general plan. He asked for a continuance so the
developer could host a neighborhood meeting and create a development agreement that enforces
stricter regulations that are consistent with the best interests of the neighborhood.

Rob Anderson, West Jordan resident, pointed out that the neighborhood to the north is older and run
down, his neighborhood is 25-year-old and has 4 acre lots. He understood that the developer said the
lots on the north side would be 4500 square feet, which would be a high-density neighborhood with tall
homes. He was not against something happening with the land, but he was concerned with the
additional traffic. If the road is opened into his neighborhood, there will be a lot of people going to
Redwood Road and it sometimes takes him 5-10 minutes to turn left onto Redwood Road, which will
be even worse with more cars. He asked for more low density lots.

Avery, West Jordan resident, said she cherishes open space and seeing the animals in the area.
Rezoning would disrupt the tranquil lifestyle, increase property taxes, and impact wildlife habitat. She
asked that their open space and diverse ecosystem be preserved.

Mike Johns, West Jordan resident for 30 years, said he knew the Taylor’s and used their veterinary
services. He had enjoyed the buffer that the property created to the north. He said at one time the
property owners had talked about having a future park there. He asked why the city would not buy the
property for a park since there are not any in the area.

Debbie Reverman, West Jordan resident for 12 years, said it is evident that the high density
surrounding the property overloads the infrastructure. Roads are narrow, crowded, and in disrepair due
to the amount of traffic. There is a lack of green space in the area. It is critical to take into
consideration the quality of life of the residents. The animals and view of the mountains would be
taken away, which changes property value and quality of life. Wear and tear on the infrastructure is
tough on the taxpayers. Building high density in this area isn’t the way to go. She supported rezoning
of agricultural property but hoped the commission would consider her concerns.

Hailey Barton, West Jordan resident, (speaking remotely) said they chose their home specifically
because it was on a dead-end with less traffic. If the road connects, then her kids would not be able to
use the pathway to school that takes them to 1470 West. She loved the idea of green space and parks,
which are lacking in their neighborhood.

Kellie Brixey, West Jordan resident, (speaking remotely) agreed with the other comments, they also
use the secret passageway to get to school. They bought their lot in part for the quiet streets and less
traffic. She was concerned with increased homes and traffic and safety for the children. It is difficult to
get onto Redwood Road at rush hour, and this will increase commute times. She understood that the
family wanted to rezone but asked that it be postponed to consider other options like a park.

George Reynolds, West Jordan resident, (speaking remotely) said his major concern is the connection
to the existing subdivisions and the speeding that would take place with another way to get to
Redwood Road, like what happens on Bora Bora. If the connection is made it will lower property
values. This plan will damage the rest of the neighborhood by connecting the roads instead of building
a standalone cul-de-sac.

Jeremy Brewster, West Jordan resident, thought it was a good idea to develop the property, but he was
concerned with the traffic if the road is connected from 1300 West to Redwood Road. There are
multiple through streets that already provide that access, and they don’t need another one. It is already
hard to get out of Erickson Park Drive onto Redwood Road. He shared the concern for the safety of
kids and pets in the neighborhood.

Sam Craw, West Jordan resident, said his biggest concern is traffic. They have a great spot and great
neighbors. He said the Taylor’s have the right to try to do what they want with the property, but the
two dead-end streets connecting from 1300 West will create a nightmare situation with traffic. If the
only access to the development was from 1300 West, then there would not be a problem.

Further public comment was closed at this point for this item.

Robbie Thomson clarified the comments about high density. Their development is not high density,
and the lots will be 8,000 square feet minimum, which is substantially larger than many of the
surrounding lots. He is familiar with the same stated concerns that most rezoning applications receive,
which are safety of children, increased traffic, and home values. He said that new home construction
will actually lift the value of the existing housing. Regarding the two stub roads, cities try to create
connectivity for emergency purposes, and there are maximum lengths that a cul-de-sac can have. He
would be happy to put in a cul-de-sac, but the connection is required. He did not know why a person
would want to come off of 1300 West and snake through a neighborhood to get to Redwood Road
because it would take longer. He understood that it is hard when you have lived by a dead-end that is

Regarding increased property taxes, those are not a function of new housing. The reason houses are so

Trish Hatch explained that the commission has strict guidelines. The property owner has the right to

develop their land. The general plan was reviewed last year, including the designation for this property.

There were open meetings where residents could provide input on the land use map. She felt the

proposal was the best use of the land. A park would be nice, but the cost of that would be the

responsibility of a private owner or the city, and she did not think that was feasible. She stated that she

fits with the idea of what the city had in mind all along.

Jay Thomas agreed with the comments from Commissioner Hatch. The vote tonight is a

recommendation to the City Council for the rezoning only, who will then make that legislative

decision. The public will be able to speak again at that meeting. He has used the veterinary office over
the years, and that will remain. He would also love open space, but that is probably not an option. He
felt that the best of the possible options is single family homes with R-1-8 zoning, which meets the
master plan and is consistent with the neighborhood. He stated that traffic and layout would be
discussed at the time of the subdivision plat.

Ammon Allen said the rezoning request matches the master plan and every criterion. Most of the

concerns raised were more toward the plat design and hopefully there will be more information

provided regarding a traffic study and any environmental matters at that time. He encouraged the
developer to partner with the community as much as possible and talk with one another in a better
manner.

George Winn said he appreciated both the commission and the community who spoke today. He noted

that this is private property, and the owner can do what is allowed within the established codes and

guidelines. He personally faced this exact situation in at least three places where he had a beautiful

view and animals. Development happens, and it can be a bit naive to think that you can live in a

growing city and state and not face these issues. The city codes, ordinances, and building regulations

try to balance the needs of private property owners.

Duncan Murray said regarding the comment about a development agreement, that recent changes to

state law do not allow a city to require one unless it is a specialized zone, or unless the applicant has

requested it, or for another legal reason.

(Agriculture 5-acre minimum lots) Zone to R-1-8 (Single-family Residential 8,000

requirements of approval. The motion was seconded by Trish Hatch and passed 7-
0 in favor.
