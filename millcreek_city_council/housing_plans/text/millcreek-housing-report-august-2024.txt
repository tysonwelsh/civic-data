  Executive Summary


  Millcreek, located in the northeast quadrant of Salt Lake County, is the twelfth-largest city in Utah by
  population. Since incorporating as a city in 2016, Millcreek has achieved several milestones including the
  opening of its new City Hall and Millcreek Common ─ a city center complete with an ice rink and Utah’s
  tallest climbing wall. Millcreek’s ongoing community development, combined with its charming mix of older
  and newer homes, surrounding mountains and natural amenities, and central metropolitan location make
  the City an attractive place to reside.

  However, the City faces challenges of housing cost burden and affordability, especially since the rapid rise
  in home prices since 2018. While the majority of Millcreek’s rental units are priced below 100 percent of
  Salt Lake County’s area median income, 45.7 percent of renters in the City are cost burdened. With
  mortgage rates exceeding seven percent as of April 2024, many homeowners are “locked-in” at lower rates
  with little incentive to move. While for-sale inventory has grown, turnover remains low and prices remain
  high.

  This Housing Report examines Millcreek’s current and projected socioeconomic and housing needs,
  including housing affordability now and in the future. Financial tools and mechanisms to promote housing
  affordability in light of projected growth are also discussed.

  Growth
  As of 2024, Millcreek has an estimated population of 64,913 persons and anticipates an additional 4,179
  persons by 2030 to reach a population of 69,093. Millcreek has an estimated 26,187 households in 2024.
  Based on recent permit trends, an additional 2,062 households are expected by 2030 to reach 28,250.

  Socioeconomic and Household Conditions
  Millcreek has a similar working-age population as Salt Lake County (60.7 percent of City versus 59.8 percent
  of County). However, the City differs from the County with a smaller percentage of children under 18 living
  at home (24.7 percent of City households versus 31.6 percent of County) and more seniors1 (28.5 percent
  of households versus 23.3 percent). Overall, Millcreek’s median household income ($88,186) is slightly
  lower than that of Salt Lake County ($90,011), though incomes vary widely across the City’s neighborhoods.

  Housing Inventory
  Millcreek housing is comprised of 56 percent single-family homes, 43 percent multi-family residences,
  about one percent accessory dwelling units, and less than one percent mobile homes. The average single-
  family home in Millcreek is similar in size (2,704 ft2) to that of the County (2,687 ft2), but the City’s multi-
  family units are slightly larger (1,105 ft2) than in Salt Lake County overall (1,002 ft2). Millcreek has a greater
  share of 2-bedroom units and relatively fewer units with 1 bedroom and 5 or more bedrooms.

  Though not incorporated until 2016, much of Millcreek’s housing growth occurred decades ago, with more
  than one-third of homes built before 1960. Most of Millcreek’s single-family and condo units that 100
  percent of area median income ("AMI”) earners can afford were built in the 1970s.

  Home Values and the For-Sale Market
  Just under half (47 percent) of Millcreek homes are assessed below $500,000, yet the median listing price
  was $665,000 in March 2024. Due to high mortgage rates and consequently decreased affordability, sales
  have slowed over the past three years.

  LIHTC Expiration and Redevelopment
  Millcreek lost 130 Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) units during the period 2023 to 2024 after the
  expiration of tax credits at Holladay Hills, bringing total LIHTC units down to 777 units. In addition, 136 units
  have affordability terms expiring after this year, and 16 more units after 2026. However, tax credits and
  affordability terms may or may not be renewed at the choice of the owners. The Community Development
  Corporation of Utah has recently received permits for 214 LIHTC units across two facilities, offsetting recent
  and potential further losses of subsidized units.

  Housing Affordability Gap Analysis
  Millcreek City’s housing stock includes 10,098 units affordable for low-to-moderate-income households ─
  households earning less than 80 percent of the Salt Lake County Area Median Income (AMI). With 12,087
  low-to-moderate-income households in Millcreek,2 the City has nearly met its affordable housing needs.
  Not surprisingly, the greatest shortage (2,822 units) occurs for extremely low-income households (roughly
  30 percent of AMI). High demand for low-cost units increases the cost burden for extremely-low-income
  households, as units affordable to these residents may be occupied by relatively higher-income households.

  Future Special Housing Needs
  Millcreek has an estimated capacity of more than 1,591 beds across various residential care facilities
  offering services ranging from all-ages rehabilitation to senior nursing. With a group quarters population of
  934 according to the 2020 Decennial Census, Millcreek has the built capacity to serve special needs groups.
  Still, careful planning is needed to balance the services offered at residential care facilities with the services
  demanded by special care needs groups.

  relatively smaller (23.1 percent) compared to that of the County (28.8 percent), while its population aged
  65 and older is larger (16.2 percent) than that of the County (11.3 percent).

  Housing Inventory

  Units by Type

  A slight majority (52 percent) of units in Millcreek are single-family detached homes. Just under five percent
  of Millcreek units are attached townhomes, bringing the total for single-family residences to 56 percent.
  Approximately 43 percent of homes are multi-family units (including duplexes, condos, and apartments).
  Apartments ─ multifamily rental units in complexes of three or more units, with all units generally owned
  by a single entity ─ are the most common type of multi-family housing in Millcreek, comprising 28 percent
  of the total housing stock. Condos ─ multifamily units in complexes of three or more units, usually owner-
  occupied, with each unit under separate ownership ─ comprise 10 percent of total units. Roughly five
  percent of total units are duplexes ─ twin homes that may consist of one owner and one renter household,
  two owner households, or two renter households. Accessory dwelling units (ADUs) remain a small portion
  (less than one percent) of the housing stock. Millcreek has one mobile home park with 29 units.

  Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) Facilities

  Low-Income Housing Tax Credits (LIHTC) are the primary source of federal funding for low-income housing.
  The Utah Housing Corporation (UHC) awards this federal funding to private and non-profit residential
  developers through competitive and non-competitive processes. As a stipulation of funding, UHC mandates
  that affordable units remain in place for low-income households for a specified number of years, currently
  set at 50 years. This “affordability term” has varied several times over the past few decades, but changes
  only apply to new developments and do not apply retroactively to existing units.

  As recently as 2023, Millcreek had 907 low-income units in LIHTC developments. As the affordability term
  expired at Holladay Hills in 2023 and 2024, the owner opted not to renew the tax credits, resulting in the
  loss of 130 low-income units, bringing Millcreek’s total low-income housing down to 777 units.

  Many factors influence LIHTC development owners in the decision of whether to renew the tax credits at
  the end of the affordability term. If an owner opts to renew, the affordability term will be extended to
  whatever length is currently set by UHC (currently, 50 years).4 If they opt not to renew, the outcome of the
  units is still uncertain. Units could remain affordable at the choice of the owner, could be renovated with
  prices set at market rate, or the property could be entirely redeveloped into a new use.

  One factor is the quality of the units and the potential market-rate rent. If the units are already high-quality
  or could be easily renovated to garner rents that exceed the value of new tax credits, owners may be
  incentivized to not renew. One proxy for the quality of units is the effective year built, with newer units
  generally being able to garner higher rents on the market, though this varies widely.

  Another factor is the redevelopment potential of the property, which is complex and cannot be understood
  through one measure. Factors that influence redevelopment potential are parcel size (acreage), zoning,
  and land value. Generally, larger parcels have higher redevelopment potential due to the greater number
  of potential uses. Additionally, zoning influences redevelopment potential by legally governing the possible
  land uses on a site.

  Land value influences redevelopment potential insofar that it reflects the profit potential of a site.
  Properties with high land values attract new development by means of superior location, transportation
  accessibility, geographic features, and/or nearby agglomerations and amenities. Redeveloping a property
  for a different or more intensive use often necessitates displacement of existing uses, whether by
  demolition or renovation. For properties with high improvement values, this potential loss is a significant
  disincentive to redevelopment. On the other hand, when the improvement value of a parcel approaches
  or falls below its land value (i.e., the improvement value is less than 100 percent of land value), potential
  gains from redevelopment often exceed potential losses stemming from the displacement of existing uses.
  For this reason, the ratio of improvement value to land value is a useful indicator helping determine the
  overall redevelopment potential of a property. Lower ratios (expressed as a percent in the rightmost
  column in Table 27 on the following page) indicate higher likelihood of redevelopment.

  to develop affordable housing.”5 Non-profit organizations (such as Community Development Corporation

  Financial Tools and Affordability Mechanisms

  First Home Investment Zones (FHIZ)
  SB268, passed by the Utah Legislature in 2024, allows cities to use tax increment to create a town center,
  with owner-occupied units, in areas not covered by HTRZs. There must be a minimum of ten acres and a
  maximum of 100 acres in a FHIZ. The approval process is similar to that of HTRZs, with HTRZ committee
  approval required.

  There is a per-acre minimum residential density requirement of 30 units per acre, and 51 percent of the
  developable acres in the FHIZ zone must be residential development. However, up to half of these homes
  can be outside the FHIZ zone. Homes within the zone must be 25 percent owner occupied and homes
  outside must be 100 percent owner occupied.

  At least 12 percent of homes inside the FHIZ zone, and at least 20 percent of homes outside the zone must
  be affordable. Owner-occupied homes are defined to be affordable at 80 percent of the county median
  sales price; rental homes are affordable at 80 percent AMI.
  New homes outside the FHIZ zone, but within the proposing city (“extraterritorial homes”), can count
  towards the requirement of 30 units per acre if they are owner-occupied for at least 25 years and meet
  other requirements: minimum of six units per acre, single-family owner-occupied, and 80 percent detached
  units.

  If a FHIZ is approved, the municipality can receive up to 60 percent of property tax increment capture from
  all taxing entities inside the zone for 25 out of 45 years, with a maximum of three tax increment phases.
  Increment can be used for project and system infrastructure costs for the benefit of the FHIZ and related
  homes outside the zone.

  Community Reinvestment Areas (CRA)
  Millcreek currently has six CRA areas. In each of these areas, ten percent of tax increment revenue to the
  Agency that exceeds $100,000 in a given year must be set aside for affordable housing. These funds can
  be used anywhere in the City or transferred to housing agencies.
  A CRA is a defined area, created under Utah Code 17C-5-104, that allows for the tax increment generated
  by new development, over a specified period of time, to be set aside to the City’s redevelopment agency
  for specific purposes within the CRA, including the creation of affordable housing. As the City creates
  additional CRA areas, it will also generate additional housing funds.

  Deed Restrictions
  Using a combination of incentives and deed restrictions, Millcreek could extend the affordability of expiring
  LIHTC units. However, this strategy is not without costs and requires cooperation from a willing landowner.

  Deed restrictions are recorded covenants against a property that “run with the land,” remaining in effect
  upon repeated sale or transfer. Cities across Utah use deed restrictions to maintain affordable rents, limit
  property value growth, restrict ownership to certain levels of income, and control property uses.8 A City
  may record a deed restriction on land it owns, or through an agreement with a private landowner.

  A deed restriction is a non-legislative requirement on landowners without the creation of new laws or
  changes to code. To record a deed restriction on private property, restrictions need to be counterbalanced
  with incentives for the landowner. Alternatively, the City could record deed restrictions against land it
  purchases or currently owns without the need for incentives. However, doing so could lower the asset value
  by reducing its income and/or profit potential. Once a deed restriction is recorded, the party filing the
  covenant (i.e., the City) bears the burden of enforcement. This involves dedicating personnel to track
  compliance and taking action against noncompliant landowners.

  Deed restriction is a tool to ensure the affordability of a residential property in perpetuity, but it does not
  come without costs, which remain the cost of incentivizing acceptance of the covenant or loss of asset
  value, cost of tracking compliance, and cost of legal enforcement.

  Home Ownership Promotion Zones (HOPZ)
  HOPZs were enacted by the Utah Legislature in its 2024 session in SB168. The basic requirements for a
  HOPZ are as follows:
     • Can be established directly by a municipality;
     • Must be 10 acres or less;
     • Must be zoned for at least 6 units per acre;
     • 60 percent of the housing units must be affordable (less than 80 percent of the median county
         home price);
     • Housing must be deed-restricted for at least five years;
     • And more provisions are in the bill – details; and
     • If created, the municipality can receive 60 percent of the tax increment for 15 years.

  Appendix B: Adopted HB-462 Strategies


  In recent years, Millcreek has adopted nine strategies to meet moderate-income housing (MIH) needs and
  requirements, with implementation actions supporting each strategy. This appendix outlines these adopted
  strategies and implementation actions.

  Strategy 1 (A)

  Rezone for densities necessary to facilitate the production of moderate income housing.

                                           Implementation Action

       1. Millcreek has adopted a Mixed-Development Zone that has no maximum residential density
          requirement which has helped Millcreek reach its MIH goals.

       2. Re-visit the Future Land Use Map and Land Use Framework Chapter of the General Plan and include
          moderate income housing development as a “use” and “key characteristic” for all categories well
          served by transit and near activity centers by 2027.

       3. Review and amend requirements of the Mixed-Development and Commercial Zones during the
          2023 zoning code update to entice qualifying moderate income housing.
              a. Reducing the minimum Off-Street Parking requirements.
              b. Waiving commercial use requirements for qualifying MIH projects in the MD and C zones.
              c. Create further reductions in zoning requirements for moderate income developments
                 geared towards affordable senior housing.




  Strategy 2 (E)

  Create and allow for, and reduce regulations related to, internal or detached accessory dwelling units in
  residential zones.

                                           Implementation Action

       1. Carefully monitor and track ADU building permit applications. Explore barriers that keep
          homeowners from constructing detached ADUs.

       2. Continue monitoring ADU applications and explore, where feasible, opportunities for reducing ADU
          regulations by 2027.
