         Fire Three Man vs. Four Man Service

Mayor Staggs discussed fire engine staffing levels, specifically the comparison between three-
person and four-person crews. He clarified that the discussion is based on budgetary
considerations and not a reflection on service quality. He emphasized the City's strong
relationship with the Unified Fire Authority (UFA), expressing appreciation for their
professionalism and service.

Kevin Hicks, City Manager, discussed the need to reach a consensus on the direction for the
upcoming fiscal year (2025-26) to allow UFA ample time to adjust if any changes are needed for
the next budget cycle.

Councilmember Buroker expressed appreciation for working with UFA, noting their transparency
and willingness to answer questions. She emphasized the importance of engaging the entire
Council in the decision regarding three-person versus four-person staffing, as it directly impacts
residents. She requested historical context on the transition from three-person to four-person
staffing across UFA.

Fire Chief Dominic Burchett, Unified Fire Authority, explained that in 2001, NFPA established
standard 1710, which outlined best practices for fire operations. Between 2001 and 2006, most
UFA apparatus operated with four-person crews, but when UFA began its own ambulance
service in 2006, staffing was adjusted to three-person crews on heavy apparatus. In 2015-2016,
discussions surrounding Draper’s departure from UFA created an opportunity to restructure
staffing, leading to a shift of ambulance personnel to heavy apparatus, bringing most stations
back to four-person crews by around 2017. Regarding Riverton, staffing decisions were made at
the UFSA board level, with Riverton’s stations adopting the four-person model. He noted that
staffing levels are determined by each UFA member community and stated that UFSA intends
to move all apparatus to four-person staffing, with only two low-call-volume stations still
operating with three-person crews.

Chief Burchett explained the impact of staffing changes on Riverton’s fire services, emphasizing
the importance of Station 124 and Station 121 in housing specialty units, including a hazmat
company and a heavy rescue company. He noted that due to UFA’s funding model, Riverton
currently receives the staffing benefit of 12 firefighters per day for the cost of eight. These
specialty units require six personnel per station, as mandated by OSHA and NFPA standards,
and are supported by an ambulance. If Riverton were to reduce staffing to three-person crews,

these specialty units and an ambulance would be relocated outside the City, resulting in a 33%
reduction in daily staffing. He highlighted that this change would increase out-of-service time, as
three-person crews responding to critical medical calls often lose a paramedic to patient
transport, leaving the heavy apparatus temporarily unavailable. He reiterated that the four-
person model ensures faster response times, greater operational efficiency, and more
personnel available to handle fire and medical emergencies.

Councilmember Pierucci acknowledged the financial realities the City was facing, noting the
current and projected general fund transfers to support fire services. He recognized the
importance of both budget considerations and public safety impacts in the Council’s decision-
making process. He asked for an estimate of how often the City’s specialty units were deployed
outside Riverton for emergency responses.

In response, Chief Burchett stated that it would be difficult to estimate how often specialty units
responded outside Riverton but noted that the heavy rescue and hazmat programs were shared
between two stations each. The Riverton stations primarily supported Herriman, Riverton, and
Eagle Mountain, while other stations in Midvale and Taylorsville handled regional responses in
the northern service area.

Councilmember Pierucci noted that the City's population and demographics had changed
significantly since the days of a three-person crew, citing the development of Mountain View
Village and rapid growth on the west side. He questioned whether the financial benefits of a
staffing change would outweigh potential impacts on public safety.

Mr. Hicks clarified that the City does not transfer sales tax revenue for fire services. Instead,
funding comes from fire impact fees collected from new developments, which are required by
law to pass through the general fund before being transferred to the fire fund. These funds are
entirely transferred each year with no remaining balance.

Regarding the $250,000 set aside for capital outlay each budget cycle, Mr. Hicks explained that
while this is a best-practice approach, the actual expenditures vary. Fire stations in the City
range in age from approximately 10 to 40 years, necessitating ongoing improvements. Last
year, around $160,000 was spent, while this year’s budget includes $210,000 for station
improvements. A request for next year’s funding is currently under review, and while some
projects may be deferred, an estimated $200,000 in recommended improvements is expected.

Councilmember McCay reiterated her satisfaction with the service provided by UFA and
inquired about the possibility of having one station staffed with four personnel while another
operates with three. Chief Burchett responded that, without deeper analysis, the most likely
configuration would be keeping the ladder and heavy rescue company at Station 121 while
reducing Station 124 to a three-person engine.

Mayor Staggs noted that Cottonwood Heights has a similar setup, with one four-person and one
three-person station, based on call volume. He requested more detailed call data for each
station rather than the aggregated annual totals to help assess whether a split configuration
would be feasible. The data would help determine if a reduction in staffing at a lower-call-
volume station could allow the City to maintain two ambulances while staying within budget
constraints.

Councilmember McDougal acknowledged the rising costs and the Council’s efforts to minimize
financial impact on residents. However, he emphasized that public safety—fire and police—are

Discussion/Action Items

         Fire Three Man vs. Four Man Service

Councilmember Haymond emphasized the importance of planning for the City's future financial
needs rather than focusing on cost-cutting, particularly when it comes to essential services like
public safety. He noted that while rising costs are a concern, reducing funding for critical
services could have severe consequences. Instead of looking for cuts, he suggested focusing
on how to sustainably fund the City's growth and ensure necessary services remain adequately
supported.

Mayor Staggs acknowledged learning valuable information during the discussion and
emphasized the importance of maintaining public safety as the City grows. While previously
considering reducing staffing at some stations, he now opposed the idea but remained open to
lowering Station 124’s crew to three if ambulance service remained unaffected. He requested
call volume data to assess the impact and sought input from the Unified Fire Authority on
whether future growth would require additional ambulances, engines, or other resources.

Chief Burchett explained that Riverton has experienced a 20% increase in call volume over the
past six years and is projected to see the same increase in the next five to six years. In 2024,
there were just over 2,100 calls, which is expected to rise to approximately 2,600. To maintain
adequate coverage, adding an engine company at Station 120 may be necessary. Riverton, as
a contract City rather than part of a service district, would absorb the cost, which is estimated at
$3.1 million for a four-person crew. Currently, the City has two ambulances, one at the old
station and one at Station 121. While station capacity is sufficient, rising call volumes due to
vertical growth may require additional resources in the future.

Chief Burchett clarified that the current budget with the projected 5.5% increases does not
account for the extra cost of an additional apparatus, which would be needed if call volume
increases significantly. If a new engine were to be added, the cost would depend on when it is
put into service, with prorated charges based on the remaining year. The cost breakdown for
UFA's stations includes both staffing costs (just under $2 million for a full four-person station)
and regional costs, which would be spread across all stations. The addition of Station 120 would
increase the number of stations to 26, spreading the regional costs further. However, adding
such an apparatus would likely require a tax increase, unless smaller increases were
implemented earlier to build up a fund balance. As of now, staffing levels can manage current
call volumes, but adding an engine in five years may become necessary depending on future
call volume growth.

Mr. Hicks clarified that while no official vote is immediately necessary, the Council may want to
provide Chief Burchett with enough time to plan if a transition to a three-person crew at one or
both stations is considered. If the Council proceeds with the change, a formal vote might not be
required, but staff would prefer making the decision official.

Councilmember Pierucci suggested that, in addition to addressing the increasing call volumes,
the City should explore community health and engagement strategies with local government
partners, nonprofit organizations, and religious groups. He emphasized that while assisted living
facilities contribute to high call volumes, other factors, such as gaps in community connectivity
and the tendency for people to call 911 for non-emergency medical situations, also play a role.
He expressed a desire to foster more community engagement and collaboration, reducing
Page | 11 Minutes – City Council Meeting
March 4, 2025



reliance on 911 by encouraging neighbors to help one another and build stronger support
systems.

Chief Burchett explained that UFA is actively pursuing a "community risk reduction" strategy to
help manage increasing call volumes. They are in the process of hiring a community risk
manager who will focus on creating networks and reaching out to existing programs to find ways
to reduce calls and risks. He also addressed a common concern from citizens about seeing both
a fire truck and an ambulance at every call, explaining that the department’s all-response model
requires bringing a full set of tools to every situation, as they can't predict the nature of the call
in advance. This model ensures that they are prepared for medical, fire, hazmat, or rescue
situations, with the goal of addressing the unknowns effectively.
