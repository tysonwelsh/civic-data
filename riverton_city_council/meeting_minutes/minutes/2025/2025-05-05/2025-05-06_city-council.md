Discussion Items

   Sanitation Self-Provide

Before addressing sanitation and self-hauling analysis, Mayor Staggs proposed ending curbside
recycling and noted the contract with Waste Management requires a six-month notice, meaning
the earliest cancellation would be in January. To help frame the discussion, he shared a short
video that a resident had sent, offering perspective on the topic.

Mayor Staggs suggested shifting to a regional recycling model that emphasizes aluminum and
paper rather than plastics. Data from Waste Management showed that only about 8% of the
City's waste is recycling material, and around 20% of that is contaminated and ends up in the
landfill—leaving only roughly 6% actually being recycled. He supports cutting curbside recycling
while maintaining budget neutrality and avoiding a rate increase that would be necessary to

continue the program.

Councilmember Buroker expressed agreement that it’s difficult to justify continued plastic
recycling and emphasized the importance of a strong communication plan before a final
decision is made. She stressed that eliminating curbside recycling is a politically sensitive issue,
and residents need clear, thorough education to understand the reasoning behind the change.

Councilmember McDougal voiced strong support for eliminating curbside recycling, citing
financial unsustainability and environmental inefficiency. He emphasized that continuing to fund
the program from the general fund diverts money from other priorities, and increasing fees for a
service that yields little real-world benefit is unjustifiable. He stressed that the landfill captures
methane to generate power, framing it as a responsible alternative.

Councilmember McCay offered a contrasting view, expressing support for keeping the curbside
recycling program. She advocated for an opt-in or opt-out model after the current contract
expires in 2028, allowing more committed residents to continue recycling while reducing
contamination. She noted that 72% of residents had previously expressed support for recycling
and cautioned that eliminating it might not actually save residents money if they end up needing
additional trash bins.

Councilmember Haymond emphasized that no matter what decision is made regarding
recycling, clear and extensive communication with the public is essential. He acknowledged that
for many residents, recycling has become a deeply ingrained belief system, shaped by decades
of messaging that frames it as critical to saving the planet. Because of this, any change could
feel deeply personal or even offensive to some. He reiterated that the success of any policy
change depends on how well it is communicated, calling for collaboration with the City’s
Communication Department to ensure residents are fully informed about the realities and
financial implications of maintaining the curbside recycling service.

Councilmember Pierucci voiced concern that under the current proposal, residents would
effectively lose a service (recycling pickup) but continue to pay the same rate. He characterized
this as a “hidden fee increase” and recommended exploring the financial impact of reducing the
monthly fee by a few dollars if recycling is eliminated, suggesting that the public might be more
receptive to the change if it’s paired with a cost reduction.

Kevin Hicks, City Manager, responded to the suggestion of lowering the monthly fee once
recycling is discontinued. He clarified that the Mayor’s proposed budget already avoids a fee
increase by subsidizing the sanitation fund with approximately $300,000 from the General Fund.
Because the City must give six months' notice before halting recycling, both trash and recycling
services would continue during that time, which limits the ability to immediately reduce rates.
He noted that if the Council chose to lower the monthly fee after recycling ends, it would likely
only last for about six months before needing to raise it again due to inflation and other cost
pressures beginning the following July.

Councilmember Pierucci asked how long eliminating curbside recycling would delay the need
for another sanitation fee increase. Mr. Hicks responded that, while they hadn’t done a detailed
financial projection, their rough estimate was that it would postpone further increases by about
two years. After that, additional increases would likely be needed due to inflation and rising
contract costs. Mr. Hicks also clarified that the Mayor's current budget proposal includes a
$2.50 monthly fee increase, with smaller cost-of-living adjustments expected in subsequent
years. Removing recycling would avoid that immediate increase and allow the City to delay

future ones temporarily.

Mr. Hicks explained that the Mayor’s budget includes a regional recycling option, which would
place recycling containers at each of the City’s 33 parks. The cost estimate for this program is
$50,000 for half a year, based on feedback from a hauler, though the actual cost would need to
be confirmed through a bidding process.

Mr. Hicks reported that diversion rate for recycling in early 2025 has increased to 10%, up from
8% in 2024, indicating that more residents are participating in recycling, even with the audits in
place. The City’s glass recycling program, which incurs no additional cost for the City, is based
on two pickups at designated locations. However, expanding this service would likely result in
extra charges.

Per Councilmember Buroker’s request, staff agreed to prepare a detailed communication plan
regarding recycling by the next Council meeting.

Mr. Hicks explained that, at a high level, he had compared the City’s sanitation costs to a
neighboring city that handles its own waste hauling and found their projected expenses to be
very similar—slightly lower, in fact. However, the analysis showed that the City still could not
afford to self-haul under current revenue levels and would need to raise fees for residents. The
pro forma included roughly $5.5 million in startup costs for trucks and facility upgrades, such as
a truck bay and wash station. He confirmed the $5.5 million startup cost includes financing
estimates for trucks and facility improvements, with vehicle debt structured based on the useful
life of each truck (3–7 years) and the bond for building improvements estimated at 20 years with
a $240,000 annual payment. The pro forma did not include rate increases in FY26 or FY27,
focusing instead on expense projections. He also noted that while no trucks would be replaced
in year one, best practices would suggest replacing one truck annually starting in year three, at
a cost of around $385,000–$400,000 each. A decision is needed within the next six months to
start procurement, as truck lead times are close to two years.

         Ordinance No. 25-14 – Amending Riverton City Code Section 18.135.200,
         Temporary Commercial Uses, Addressing Temporary Commercial Uses on
         Residential Property, Amendments Proposed by Riverton City

Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, explained that this amendment would allow
temporary commercial uses on vacant, undeveloped residential properties that have direct
access to major roadways, as defined in City code (e.g., Redwood Road, 12600 South). Based
on prior Council feedback, the updated draft restricts access for such uses to major roads only,
unless existing conditions prohibit it, in which case limited exceptions apply. The amendment
also includes references to the City’s temporary commercial lighting standards. The Planning
Commission recommended approval, and staff likewise recommended adoption with the
incorporated changes.

Councilmember Haymond asked whether the proposed ordinance included protections for
nearby primary residences and whether temporary commercial uses must be fully self-
contained. Mr. Lethbridge confirmed that yes, parking and access must be fully contained on the
temporary use property, with no spillover onto side streets. Councilmember Haymond also
raised concerns about fireworks stands and large-scale storage of explosives. Mr. Lethbridge
responded that while the City code does not specifically regulate fireworks storage, any
fireworks operation is subject to permitting and inspection by Unified Fire Authority (UFA), which
enforces applicable fire safety codes.

Mayor Staggs called for public comments;

Jay Salter spoke in opposition to a potential firework stand on vacant lots located off Christian
Way near Redwood Road, citing several concerns. He noted the area’s existing traffic
challenges, including limited egress options that would likely become further congested with the
addition of temporary commercial activity. He described the general state of the property—citing
makeshift fencing and poor upkeep—as a burden on surrounding neighbors, and asked the
Council to carefully consider whether allowing a commercial use at this location would be
appropriate or beneficial to the community.

Gary Exeter raised several concerns regarding the proposed ordinance allowing temporary
commercial uses on vacant residential properties. He questioned whether any studies had been

Todd Wartman shared concerns about the proposed ordinance based on past experiences with

residents from ongoing negative impacts.

Shawn Taylor expressed support for protecting private property rights and emphasized the
importance of allowing property owners to pursue opportunities on their land, provided their
actions do not negatively impact neighbors.

Mayor Staggs called for additional public comments; there being none, he closed the Public
Hearing

Mr. Lethbridge clarified that a permit is still required for temporary commercial uses on
residential properties, issued through the Planning Department and subject to additional
permitting from Unified Fire. He emphasized that permits can be revoked if violations occur, and
that the City has not seen significant crime issues associated with these uses. The intent is to
balance private property rights with community impacts, and enforcement mechanisms are in
place to protect nearby residents.

Councilmember McDougal highlighted that while the ordinance sets general rules, the permit
process includes an opportunity for site-specific concerns to be addressed. He expressed
support for the ordinance but stated a preference for maintaining the 60-day limit on permits,
citing the potential disruption such uses can cause to neighbors, and noting that most seasonal
operations—such as tree lots or fireworks stands—fit within that time frame.

Councilmember Pierucci agreed that the ordinance strikes a good balance between protecting
private property rights and ensuring community input on specific permit applications.

Councilmember Buroker acknowledged the potential benefits of the ordinance but voiced
reservations about the ability to enforce compliance, especially given the track record of the
property owner in question. She also noted that 60 days may not be enough time for code
enforcement to produce meaningful behavior change.

         Ordinance No. 25-15 – DAI Development Agreement, Amending Zoning from R-3
         to C-R Uses limited to Storage Units, and Preliminary Site Plan Approval for
         Approx. 5.63 acres Located at 12270 South 4000 West. Applicant-Krisel Travis
         and Joe Salisbury representing DAI Utah

Mr. Lethbridge explained that the proposed development, located off 4000 West and north of
12600 South, is a storage facility with outdoor vehicle storage, replacing an undeveloped,
platted residential subdivision from 2006. The property, which has remained dormant due to
legal challenges and the recession, is now being considered for development. The applicant's
proposal includes two existing homes that will remain and one lot to be retained for future
development. The zoning would be changed to commercial regional (CR) but limited to the
specified storage facility uses. Architectural standards and building height limitations are set
within the development agreement, and improvements such as curb, gutter, and sidewalk would
be made along the frontage.

Mr. Lethbridge said that a traffic study confirmed that the proposed development would not
generate more traffic than a residential development, and no changes to the road's right of way
are required. The applicant's proposal includes fencing and architectural elements that aim to
blend with the surrounding area, including residential properties. The Planning Commission has
reviewed the project in multiple meetings, ensuring it aligns with the community's needs while
providing commercial benefits. The development agreement includes several conditions to
ensure the project adheres to the outlined standards and fits appropriately within the
neighborhood.

Mr. Lethbridge noted that the proposed development includes several exceptions to City code,
including the removal of a required landscape buffer along the northeast property line, due to
the mature landscaping at the adjacent LDS church and the applicant’s commitment to an eight-
foot fence. The setback for two buildings on 4000 West is reduced from 40 feet to 36 feet, with
support from the property owner and the Planning Commission, as the buildings will remain
single-story. Additionally, a unique signage tower is proposed, replacing the standard pole sign,
as the current sign code doesn’t account for this design. These exceptions were reviewed and
approved by the Planning Commission, which recommended the development with a condition
to clarify building heights, now addressed in the agreement.

Councilmember McDougal expressed support for the development, appreciating the design and
acknowledging the circumstances that led to this solution rather than the originally planned
third-acre lots from 2008. He noted that the two-story buildings would not exceed the height of
two-story homes and praised the aesthetic value of the proposed signage. However, he raised
concerns about the need for a zoning change in the development agreement, fearing that, in 15
years, the zoning could revert and allow for other uses. He recommended removing the zoning
change and approving the project solely for self-storage to ensure it remains dedicated to that

         Resolution No. 25-28 – Establishing a Formal Perimeter Boundary for a Public
         Park Known as the Riverton Main Park

Ryan Carter, City Attorney, clarified that the boundary adjustment under discussion does not,
by itself, authorize or imply the sale of any City-owned parcels. Any sale would require Council
approval and likely involve a formal plat process. While a few subdivided parcels could
theoretically be transferred by deed, most would need additional steps, including filing and
creating an approved plat. The intent of the adjustment is simply to clarify proximity and
boundaries between City property and adjacent commercial land.
