WORK SESSION
2.     Community Development Department – 2021 Ordinance Amendment; Schedule and
       Drafts Overview.
Planning Manager, Jonathan Teerlink, provided an overview of the accomplishments staff has
brought to the Planning Commission before the City Council makes a final decision on draft and
ordinance amendments. The Commission members were provided with an R-1 worksheet that is
provided to residents to residents and home designers to use in interpreting the ordinance. He
thought it would be helpful to review it to allow the Commission to better understand what is
involved in a zoning review when a new home is constructed.

Mr. Teerlink stated that there have been several iterations with this being the most recent. The
premise was lot dimension, how wide a property is, how wide it is, and whether it is on a public

street or private road. Mr. Teerlink explained that most ordinances pertain to fitting new
development within a street context of an existing subdivision development. In the City of
Holladay, there are several instances where larger properties are built next to smaller lots. In 2007,
they standardized setbacks when lots are not standard. It contributed to the “McMansion” issue
where large homes are built next to smaller lots. He clarified that dimensions that are standardized
for a setback for front, side, and rear yards and pertain to lot size and are proportionate. The
property size must be entered along with the width and whether it is on an interior or private
driveway. That will provide a standard for how the front, side, and rear yards are determined.

Commissioner Banks considered “McMansions” to be homes that are disproportionate to the lot.
He asked how this addresses that phenomenon if lots of less than 1.5 acres still have a smaller
setback. Mr. Teerlink stated that the 2007 ordinance does not stifle the expansion of properties
and anticipates that most residential neighborhoods will turn over at some point but still allows for
some flexibility. Building areas are larger than what existed initially but it situates the homes in a
manner that should be somewhat in line with the existing homes on the street. There will also be
height and lot coverage restrictions.

Mr. Teerlink reported that on average there are 10 to 18 new homes rebuilt every year and each
uses this same averaging technique. There are several neighborhoods that have done the averaging
work, which makes it easy to apply set front yards to every street in the City. On private roads,
there is a standard. If it is less than 20 feet, you are 30 feet from the center of the right of way. If
it is 20 feet or greater, you are 20 feet away.

Side and rear yard setbacks were next addressed. The side yard pertains to the lot width. The
ordinance specifies that a percentage of the lot width can be represented in side yard setbacks. One
side must be 10% and the other has to be the remainder of 25%. Even though a large lot may be
next to a small lot, proportionality is being used. Rear yards are calculated by finding the lot size
on the chart, which corresponds with a number.

Mr. Teerlink next addressed the section of the ordinance that addresses vertical controls. In the
City of Holladay, they do not have only overall height limits. There are several controls pertaining
to how height is measured and regulated. The overall height is the maximum allowed based on
the lot size.

The concept of building corridors was next addressed. It is a measurement of the depth of the
homes on either side and is used in the front yard calculation to determine how deep the home can
be built on the lot up to the maximum height. It eliminates the issue of large homes being built
deep on the lot and obstructing views or creating privacy concerns in backyards.

Mr. Teerlink noted that protections were put in place to prevent properties from being combined,
which would be twice the minimum lot size in the zone. If two properties were combined, the side
yard setbacks would remain proportionate to the lot width. The wider the lot, the deeper the side
yard setbacks. Another control on height was graduated height, which controls how tall walls are
near property lines. The final height control was the idea of mass and scale. They go through an
exercise to design the building envelope, however, if the property looks more like a Colonial or

Lot coverage issues were next discussed. Mr. Teerlink reported that the Village and HCR Zones
do not have coverage maximums. Based on lot size, there is a structure limit as well as a total
impervious surface limit. Mr. Teerlink clarified that the ordinance allows a property owner to
build across the setback line up to a maximum of 15%, however, to cross that line you must show
that you are compliant with the average of those yards by 10 measurements across the front. Chris
Layton asked if what is considered impervious surface is further defined. He noted that there are
hardscape designs that are considered by many to be impervious due to the way they allow
moisture to continue to go into the ground. Mr. Teerlink explained that it was not further defined
and was left open to interpretation by the City Engineer. There is a 100-foot protection area around
waterways. Natural waterways have a 100-foot protection area and canals have a 20-foot
protection area.

Mr. Teerlink explained that when the City incorporated there were standards from Salt Lake
County that included protection standards regarding what can and cannot be done in terms of
disturbing the ground within the 100-foot area. His opinion was that they got to this point and
tried to write the standards out and either gave up or ran out of time before leaving the Planning
Commission to deal with it. That creates a specific set of regulations depending on the situation.
It also creates situations involving arbitrary considerations in applications.

Mr. Teerlink and City Engineer, Jared Bunch established a set of regulations and standards based
on distances from waterways. He felt it was better for those along the stream to understand exactly
where their neighbors are required to be, what they are expected to perform at, and what will be
required of the City regardless of the public meeting process. The first element addresses the zones
involving the 100-foot protection areas. Within the building envelope, if some of the area falls
within 100 feet, it automatically refers to the section regarding what can and cannot be done. It is
lengthy because they have determined that not every property is going to be a clean slate. Some
properties already have some development and many already have accessory buildings.

Spring Creek is a natural waterway that ends at the end of Murray Holladay Road where it is piped
to the Jordan River. The protections are the same, however, because there could conceivably be
riparian corridors up and down Spring Creek. By applying the 100-foot buffer on either side of
the creek one can see that there are structures within the area. When additions, teardowns, rebuilds,
or sheds are proposed in buffer areas, the ordinance specifies what can and cannot be there. Other
cities have similar ordinances and protections and have designed the notion of the high watermark.
Mr. Teerlink considered that to be one of the most critical elements of the ordinance and how it is
delineated. The definition is essentially an industry standard for environmental scientists.

Mr. Teerlink stated that when writing ordinances, they always defer to a immovable position in
the event water level changes. Commissioner Banks commented that there are volumes of case

Mr. Teerlink addressed defining elements of the waterway protection section and what the zones
entail. The other element determines whether there is an undeveloped piece of property.
Developed land will have more impacts and disturbances than undeveloped property. The draft
document was to be provided to the Commission members for further review. Mr. Teerlink
explained that Category I usually consists of Legislative or City Council required amendments.
He stated that the Code Enforcement section is in the process of being rewritten because the City
contracts with the Unified Police Department (“UPD”). Enforcement issues were discussed. It
was noted that unless a violation is considered a nuisance, there is no criminal penalty.

HB-388 allows the Planning Commission and/or staff to review subdivision plats if they consist
of only two lots. It was reported that the City Council enacted a moratorium on recreational
structures, specifically as they relate to skateboard ramps. A new amendment was being
considered to the Noise Violation Ordinance. It would be similar to the Swimming Pool Ordinance
enacted a few years ago that placed time restrictions on when pools could be used. Skateboard
ramps are specifically singled out as a prohibited use.

Mr. Teerlink reported that there were questions surrounding Accessory Dwelling Units (“ADU”)
as they await the outcome of the current Legislative Session. There were rumors that there will be
further restrictions on what municipalities can and cannot do. The intent was to address ADUs.
They need to adopt an ordinance but staff wants to make sure that they do not adopt something
that could be in violation of State legislation.

Chapter 13.71 clarifies the ordinance and changes instances of “should” to “shall”. Chapter 13.88
pertains to non-conforming uses and structures. It needs to be completely rewritten to be in step
with recent court cases nationwide. It was identified as an older section of the ordinance. The
Historic Preservation Section was described as a complete update of policy and clarifications.
Some of the benefits of having a property on a Historic Preservation List were described. For
example, it allows for flexibility regarding use of the land.

Mr. Teerlink encouraged the Commission members to review their own properties against the R-1
worksheet so that when they get to accessory buildings, gates, or allowable yard encroachments
they will know the background and can make a quicker, more educated decision. The majority of
the work on the document was expected to be completed before the end of the fiscal year.

ADJOURN
Commissioner Banks moved to adjourn. The motion passed with the unanimous consent of the
Commission.

The Planning Commission Meeting adjourned at 7:23 p.m.
