There are about 2000 species of fireflies and they are classified in the family Lampyridae. Of those, only
about 150 live in North America. How many are in Utah? We don’t know. Maybe three or six. We're still
trying to figure that out.

The light that these bugs produce is a “cold” light - it doesn’t create heat like a light bulb, meaning it’s
energy efficient. And it’s created through a chemical process with an enzyme called luciferase (and the
substrate luciferin). From Latin Lucifer the Light-bringer.

So how does this serve the beetle? Two ways: it’s a warning to predators that the beetle is distasteful to
eat (in both its larval and adult forms). As an adult though, the glow or flash of lighting serves for mate
attraction. The lighting signals species specific messages.

I'm going to end with this. Why is this important?

Are fireflies the most important keystone species in the environment? Perhaps not. Perhaps every biome
isn’t dependent on them and perhaps if they all went away it would make very little impact on the
ecological web. But here’s what I know, and I know you know too.

We're in trouble - it’s because not enough people love what needs to be saved. We're too disconnected.
But there are wonderful things are out there. And if something this charismatic, this amazing has been
under our noses all this time, calling to us to be in love again with our very own backyards, what else is
out there? And if we can introduce people to something this magical, what steps might they take to want
to protect our lands?
