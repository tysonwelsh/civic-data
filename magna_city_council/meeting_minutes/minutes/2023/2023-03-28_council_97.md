Magna Water District

               Clint Dilley, General Manager, Magna Water District, updated the Council on the
Magna Water District activities. The Magna Water District applied for and received a $350,000
grant through American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA) funding, for secondary water meters. It wants
to use this money as a one-time incentive for people who have the lateral installed on their
property, but are not using it, to convert to secondary water. Ultimately, the more people who use
secondary water, the better the community and district will do because that is lower cost water.

              Council Member Prokopis asked how many households would be able to convert
with that amount of money.

               Mr. Dilley stated Magna Water District is planning on purchasing 1,000 meters
with the money. The grant pays 70 percent of the costs, and the Water District pays 30 percent.
Magna Water District has two sources of secondary water. It uses ground water wells that pump
a small quantity of water out of the aquifer to the reservoirs and Utah Lake water, which is poor
quality water and subject to problems, such as algae blooms, so it is unreliable at times.

He continued the update stating construction will start on the Waste Water Treatment Plant reuse
project in April, and that water will be a much higher quality water than the secondary water
sources, as it will have nutrients in it that actually help lawns, and it will be more reliable. That
will be under construction for about a year.

The Magna Water District is also putting in for a grant through Congressman Chris Stewart’s office
for some discretionary spending for the Haynes’ well replacement. That grant is 80 percent, so it
would be almost $2 million in grant funds. That project is already under design. Other projects
include upgrades to the Zone 3 secondary pump station, which will start next week; the water line
replacement project on Florence Dive and Edith Drive; and a 30-inch sanitary sewer trunk line
from 2100 South across 201 to the other side of the Frontage Road, which will be bid out in April.

Due to historic snowpack this year, most areas in the state are out of extreme or severe drought
and are moving into the moderate drought category. However, most of the reservoirs statewide
will not fill with water, although some of the smaller ones will, and one year of record water will

              THIS BEING THE TIME heretofore set for a public hearing to consider the following
rezone request, and proposed ordinance:

File No. REZ2023-000816

Ordinance No. 2023-O-05

              Brian Tucker, Planning Manager, Greater Salt Lake Municipal Services District
(MSD), delivered a PowerPoint presentation on the rezone of a portion of Magna Main Street. He
reviewed a background of Magna Main Street, including the C-3 zone that had been applied to
the western portion of Main Street and the problems associated with it; the General Plan adopted
in March 2021; the work on creating a Magna Main Street District Plan, which should be
completed late 2023 or early 2024; the Council’s adoption of the Downtown Historic District (DH)
Mixed-Use Zone to encourage building forms that are more compatible with this area; and the
Council’s passage of a resolution asking the MSD staff to initiate the process to define the Magna
Main Street Historic District, and rezone it placing it under the newly created DH Zone.
                                                  4
DATE            TUESDAY                                   MARCH                                28, 2023


As a result of the Council’s resolution, the MSD identified a study area that included all of the
Downtown Historic District, the C-3 Zone and adjacent C-3 property, and the Magna Main Street
property from 8800 West to 9200 west; and it sent out notices to property owners who would be
affected by this rezone. He reviewed the DH Zone uses, stating many of the existing business
uses would be permitted or they would become non-conforming uses, which meant they would
be grandfathered in. Architectural standards would be applied to new construction, rebuilds,
exterior remodels, and in some circumstances to additions, especially additions that would affect
the façade. With two exceptions, all the single-family homes within that study area were legally
non-conforming under the C-3 zone, and they would still be okay under the new zone.

The MSD came up with three scenarios for the Council to consider. He explained the boundaries,
benefits, and drawbacks for each of the scenarios. Then, there was a no change scenario,
wherein nothing would be done until the historic neighborhood’s plan was complete. The MSD
staff and the Magna Planning Commission recommended Scenario 3, which only included the
portion of the rezone area included in the National Register of Historic Places.

As part of the notice, State Code says owners of real property can, within 10 days of the first
public hearing, file a written objection to the inclusion of their property in the potential rezone area,
and the MSD did receive two letters following the March 16th public hearing. One was from Cox
Land Investments, which has the impound yard at 9152 West Magna Main, and the other was
from the Gem Theater, across the street. The letter protesting inclusion does not mean the
Council is precluded from rezoning this property; it only means their protest is part of the official
record.

the long-range plan was identified.

district now. It protects the historic district from future development under the C-3 zone, as that
is completely disparate and incongruent with anything else on Main Street or what was wanted

Sports Salt Lake

                Clay Partain, Executive Director, Sports Salt Lake, delivered a presentation on
Sports Salt Lake, which is under the umbrella of Visit Salt Lake. It is an organization that promotes
sporting events in the County, which brings in tourism and positively impacts the community.
Sports tourism impacts the economy; it represents almost $40 billion of economic impact
throughout the country. Last year, Sports Salt Lake helped bring in 60 sporting events, equating
to 250,000 people who came into the County and spent money, and that had a $120 million direct
impact on the County. This impact can trickle down, helping municipalities by creating tax
revenues and producing jobs. Sports tourism is also great public relations for a municipality and
brings communities together, it can increase visitor tourism during slow periods, and it can help
promote a healthy community. The most important factor to increase sports tourism is having a
venue, such as a great trail for a trail-running event, or a great pool for a swimming event. Then,
there are other key ingredients when creating, targeting, or promoting something and they all
need to work together. If a municipality wanted to get more involved in sports tourism, it would
probably want to go through a feasibility study or it could attend a sports tourism summit, which
is great for networking and trading ideas. Sports Salt Lake hosted a summit last year and will
continue to host them in the future.

              Council Member Hull asked Mr. Partain to talk about the Salt Lake Regional
Athletic Complex in Rose Park.

                 Mr. Partain stated the Salt Lake Regional Athletic Complex was built in 2015. It
has 16 football-sized fields, and can host dozens of sports, i.e., football, soccer, rugby, lacrosse,
spikeball. It is the second most used facility in Salt Lake County in terms of hosting large sporting
events that brings in visitors, and it is capable of hosting events all summer.

Mahogany Ridge Development and Parking Issues

                Mr. Ben Hansen, Ivory Development, delivered a presentation on some proposed
changes to the Mahogany Ridge Development to address the parking issues. Ivory Homes has
a 50-foot right of way, which includes curb, gutter, sidewalk and park strip, and 25 feet of asphalt.
Ivory Homes has discovered a foot of space between the sidewalk and actual property line, which
it can eliminate on both sides of the road. Then, it can shorten the park strips from five feet to
four-and-a-half feet. That would give it a total of 28 feet of asphalt on the streets, which Ivory
Homes believed would solve some of the problems. Some of the private streets have also been
widened to 22 feet of pavement, so people can park along those streets. Another change would
be to put driveways together, rather than on the high side, to create more space on the street in
between driveways. Where there are two parking stalls in each garage, Ivory Homes could put
in another stall, similar to a recreational vehicle (RV) pad. Then, for the the tighter townhomes
that are single car garages, Ivory homes has adjusted its open space, spreading it throughout the
community rather than concentrated in one or two areas, and adding more off-street parking.
