OPENING CEREMONY:
Celina Wille, USU Extension Associate Professor and Associate Director of the Latinx
Cultural Center provided the opening ceremony and led the audience in the pledge of
allegiance.
Hispanic Heritage month is a time of celebration. Before the arrival of the Mayflower,
(November 11, 1620), there are Spanish explorers such as Juan Ponce de Léon who
arrived in Florida in 1513. The first European language spoken on the American
continent after the native indigenous languages is Spanish. There is a legacy and
contributions to the United States. It is important to recognize the contributions as this is
a population that is here to stay.
Currently, about 70% of the Latino population within the state are U.S. born. On average
at least in Utah, this is a very young population. The median age is about 25 years old. At
least for Northern Utah, some are recent immigrants' and others have been here for
generations. It is an invitation for the Council to ponder on the human capital at a local
level. The Logan School District alone has 30% Latino student population. In the next
decade, these students will be sustaining the economy.
As part of the celebration at Utah State University, there is an event titled ‘Discovering
our roots’. The entire community is invited to the event, (October 22). This will be a
celebration of culture, food, and music. Vice Chair Ernesto López will be participating in
the event.

Water School – Chair Simmonds

Chair Simmonds summarized several of the presentations discussed at a two-day
conference (composed roughly of 20 government water representatives) that she recently
attended. Utah Valley Basin is by far the largest geographically and Weber Basin
operates the most federally funded dams and reservoirs. One of the presentations was
regarding the Colorado River and its impact on Lake Mead and Lake Powell. The
tributary has been falling for years, but the water levels have recently become obvious.

The Colorado River is divided into an upper and lower basin with water flow that is
dedicated to Mexico. The upper basin is made up of Utah, Colorado, part of Wyoming,
and New Mexico. The lower basin is made up of Arizona, California, and Nevada. The
lower basin has a different allocation than the upper basin. The challenge going forward
is smaller and smaller (for Utah). Since the creation of the Colorado Compact, the
Imperial Valley has taken every single last drop of water allocation. Nevada only had an
allocation of 300,000 acre-feet per year.

April determines run-off efficiency for the Colorado River. If it is hot, cold, wet, or dry,
all these factors will impact how the run-off comes off the mountains. If there is enough
snowpack to fill rivers. Fall and Spring temperatures influence the dryness of the
mountain ground which in turn influences run-off efficiency.

The flow coming from the mountains into Lake Powell in 1983 & 1984 was four times
more than in 2021 & 2022. Regarding the Great Salt Lake, the water has receded and is
being watched closely to determine its continual impact. But agriculture has always had
the highest priority. The thought is that agriculture returns water to the system. Next was
water conservation, however, the use of water conservation was for development and
growth. The initiative 'let's make better snow' focuses on cloud seeding. The lake fuels
snow, which in turn fuels the snowfall in the mountains and fuels the ski season
(tourism). There is a concern about dust, the need to protect migratory birds, and the
ecosystem of the lake.

The railroad causeway was built, and it bisected the Great Salt into an upper and lower
half. Half of the lake is much saltier than the other half because the water cannot flow
freely. A decision will have to be made. Then there is the discussion of the mineral
industry, who has water rights. The question is if the mineral industry has those same
rights even when the lake is diminished.

There is a need for a better definition of agriculture optimization. Is it doing more or less,
etc. There is a need to change our view of the landscape. There is a need for higher
density. House Bill 410 is to improve and preserve the Great Salt Lake. There are even
some ideas to import water from the ocean or river or change the Bear River
Development Act. If the Bear River Development Act is changed this will directly affect
Cache County and Box Elder County. At present, we have a right to 60,000 acre-feet as
does Box Elder County. Weber Basin and Jordan Valley have a smaller allocation of
30,000 each. However, they have a growing population.

The Head of Southern Nevada Water Authority discussed the Colorado River water rights
and how Las Vegas is 90% reliant on that water allocation of 300,000. They only own
10% of water outside of the Colorado River flow. The drought response by Southern
Nevada is to create stringent water budgets and negotiate for more if possible. They will
address infrastructure, turf, etc. There are surcharges if golf courses overwater, etc. In ten
years, consumptive use has declined by 36 billion gallons while adding 400,000 new
residents. Their new conservation goal is 86 gallons per capita per day.

Not all water use is reported at a national standard, however, Utah has always counted all
water uses including portable, secondary, and reuse by all water uses. Some locations do
not count reuse water or secondary water at all. We try to count all of it. Our water use
looks more than twice of other locations per capita, because we count all water uses. We
need to talk about conservation differently.

Statewide we are considering grass removal including excess water use surcharges and
commercial use. Water rights are under consideration, yes, one may have the right to
water. However, if there is no water that question is moot.

Weber Basin owns all the water in the Weber Basin system. The secondary water and
culinary water are piped and metered for monitoring. It is one of the most efficient
systems in the state. It is a difficult challenge to work with a limited resource. Ultimately
water is the limiting factor in the State.

Councilmember Jensen asked how close the dust levels from the drying Great Salt Lake
are to becoming a health hazard to the community. (At present, the state is in the middle
of a 20-year drought).

Chair Simmonds responded that it depends on the time of year and how low the lake must
reach before it is determined that it is a crisis. The Great Salt Lake is presently at its
lowest point in our recorded history. Furthermore, there are still mineral industries that
are still taking water out.

Councilmember A. Anderson inquired if secondary meters have been looked at and what
might be the cost.

Chair Simmonds replied funds were allocated for some secondary metering. However, it
is not sufficient to cover everything. The metering approved was for secondary water to
homes, it does not affect agriculture at this point. There are pluses and minuses to every
alternative, but we may have to pick the alternatives that have slightly more pluses than
minuses. The state legislature will ultimately make metering decisions. The Water
Conservatory Districts will do most of that for themselves, (for example Weber Basin).
The Water Conservatory Districts are important as they have been given authority to
manage water in the state of Utah. However, the Water Conservatory District for Cache is
young and does not own any water. We wish to preserve and protect the water in Cache
Valley.

400 North Corridor Plan – Councilmember Amy Anderson

Councilmember A. Anderson read the introduction from the 400 North Corridor Plan.
(The 400 North Corridor Plan can be viewed on the city website).
“The form of 400 North has become overshadowed by its function. The 400 North
Corridor Plan provides a concept to create a meaningful streetscape design for the
corridor that seeks to shift the emphasis to a more multi-modal approach. It lays out a
design-oriented framework for future redevelopment and offers the opportunity to break
down physical barriers bisecting the north and south portions of the Adams
Neighborhood.

Redevelopment areas offer the opportunity for a transition in land use to a more
pedestrian-oriented, mixed-use, multi-modal cultural hub that serves the neighborhood,
broader community, and the campus. The corridor can reflect the character and culture of
the city, neighborhood, and campus for those traveling its length. While the streetscape
design and redevelopment approach alone will not automatically lead toward change, this
plan provides the framework and is a first major step toward making it happen.”

At the conference of ULCT, (Utah League of Cities and Towns), one of the discussions
was regarding having complete streets. Wider streets do not necessarily mean safer as
they lead to increased speeds. One of the concerns of 400 North is the speed of cars. The
400 North Corridor Plans modifies how the street is run. We may need to reevaluate the
implementation of the plan.

do so.

ACTION ITEM:

PUBLIC HEARING - Budget Adjustments FY 2022-2023 appropriating: $64,167
funds Community Development received from the State of Utah to be used to help
reduce homelessness in the community; $300,000 radio reserves toward replacement
radios in the Communication Center Fund; $5,460 funds received for
reimbursement for police overtime shifts; $11,400 a grant the Police Department
received for the bulletproof vest partnership program to be used to purchase vests
for police officers – Resolution 22-42 – Richard Anderson, Finance Director

At the October 4, 2022, Council meeting, Finance Director Richard Anderson addressed
the Council regarding the proposed budget adjustments.

Councilmember Jensen inquired how the $64,167 funds to reduce homelessness will be
applied.

Mayor Daines answered that a portion of the city’s sales tax automatically is remitted to
the State for homeless shelters across the State. The legislation was adjusted permitting
                                                                                   6 | Page

local funds to be used at a local level for homelessness. CAPSA qualifies as a homeless
shelter because many of its domestic violence victims are considered unsheltered. Part of
the funds will go to CAPSA for caseworkers and the other half to go to BRAG (Bear
River Association of Governments) to do outreach to those experiencing homelessness or
are on the verge of being homeless. The application works much like the CDBG funds,
the city receives the funds from the State and then proceeds to grant them to another
entity. In this case, CAPSA and BRAG for homeless efforts will provide reports of the
use of funding to the city, who in turn will provide to the State.

Councilmember Jensen asked if any of the funds would be for the new city warming
center.

Councilmember A. Anderson replied not as of yet as the warming center was just
approved last Thursday, October 13.

Richard Anderson, Finance Director added that the funds are coming from the State and
just passing through the City to be granted to the recipient organizations.

Chair Simmonds opened the meeting to a public hearing.

Bronwyn O’Hara, a resident of Logan said the grant is going to CAPSA and BRAG, but
the attachment for the grant says, “expense for departmental supplies”. She asked is this
for diapers or linen, just what is it for.

Mr. Anderson responded this is the description of the account in the general ledger. The
account is called supplies and the reason is that the funds/grant are not capital. We would
create a new specific account, but this is a general ledger, not a specific ledger. It is
merely the general account from where the funds are coming from.

Ms. O’Hara asked if the funds for CAPSA will be used for a case worker and what about
BRAG. Will the funds be exactly split in half between the two organizations and if these
funds are from the State not Federal or Covid funds.

Mayor Daines answered the funds for CAPSA is for a case worker and the funds for
BRAG are for outreach. This is how they are defined spending their funds per the grant.

Mr. Anderson replied that the grant application will state exactly how the funds will be
used and how much the entity will receive. As stated by the Mayor, the funds received
are from the State not Federal or Covid funds.

Ms. O’Hara inquired if any city or town can apply for these grants.

Mayor Daines responded that any city or town that has a qualifying homeless shelter can
apply for these funds. It is not based on population. CAPSA qualifies as a homeless
shelter as such the funds can be received and granted. BRAG is slightly different but still
met the criteria of the State.
