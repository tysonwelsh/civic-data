      Jason Walker, City Administrator, recognized the retired elected officials, and expressed

      Mayor Johnson explained that each applicant may introduce themselves for 2 minutes.

Introductions

    1. Jim Bratt
       Retired professional businessman and longtime radio/TV advertising salesperson from
       San Antonio. Moved to Lehi to be near family. Wants to help with infrastructure,
       economy, and safety. Grateful to outgoing officials and willing to serve even if not
       selected.

    2. Larry Banks
       Vietnam veteran with a PhD in Business Administration and an MBA in technology
       management. Former small-business owner, GM of JBL speaker manufacturing, and
       Provost at two universities. National speaker on institutional effectiveness. Has political
       and church leadership experience. Says he has no agenda and wants sustainable,
       growth-oriented Lehi aligned with residents’ values.

    3. Doug Clark
       Utah County native, retired tech executive (WordPerfect, Novell, Electronic Arts, NICE)
       with global leadership experience. Moved to Lehi in 2012. Priorities are for better
       communication and transparency, stronger economic development, transportation
       improvements, police funding, sidewalks, and green space.

    4. Robert Reader
       Works in school districts (Canyons), with 15 years’ experience and knowledge of
       forming a new district—relevant to Lehi’s upcoming changes. Values good city
       governance, and wants to protect solid systems from being dismantled. Initially hesitant
       to apply but felt a duty to offer his skills.

    5. Savannah Johnston
       Sixth-generation Utahn, mother of four young kids, wife of a Lehi small-business owner,
       and professor of constitutional government, civics, and law at UVU. Lived in Los
       Angeles and wants to prevent Lehi from following LA’s path (housing crisis, traffic,
       water issues, weak civic life). Advocates: less car dependence, multi-use zoning, better
       transit/bike infrastructure, more housing supply including density, and stronger
       civic/community spaces.

    6. Vern Keesler
       20-year Lehi resident, raising six children here. U.S. Marine Corps veteran with 15 years
       in local government; currently Provo City’s traffic manager, working with UDOT, MAG,
       UTA, and school districts. Wants Lehi to remain a great place for families with a strong
       mix of mostly single-family homes plus some other housing types, and to hold developers
       to their agreements.

    7. Katie Koivisto
       Was not present

    8. E LaRell Stephens
       20-year Lehi resident, business-owner electrician who manages schedules, budgets, and
       conflict on projects. Active in the community through Lehi High mountain bike team.
       Emphasizes using constitutional principles to protect rights and maintain Lehi as a good
       place to raise a family. Enjoys studying code (Utah law, electrical, etc.).

    9. Stephen “Polo” Su’a-Filo
       Civil engineer managing major water infrastructure projects (currently a $100M project,
       on or under budget). Focused on protecting water as a critical asset. Values clear zoning,
       responsible budgets, and strong investment in roads, utilities, and services. Brings a
       diverse background (Hawaii, South Carolina, mission in Idaho, Snow College football,
       Utah State grad) and chose Lehi for its faith, service, and family values.

    10. Josh Sorenson
        Was not present.

    11. Derek Shumway
        Born in China, adopted to Utah as an infant. Director of HR at a law firm; previously
        worked for U.S. Senators Mitt Romney and Mike Lee handling immigration casework,
        balancing differing political views. Volunteers with Americans for Prosperity and has
        state-legislature ties. At 29, emphasizes energy, time, and a desire to learn how city
        government works from the inside.

    12. Jess Green
        Longtime Utah County resident (43 years), lives in the historic Smith mansion near
        downtown. Real estate agent and also works for a power-generation firm. Concerned
        about safety, financial clarity, and city communication. Skeptical of high -density and
        some progress trends; wants to preserve small-town values, Main Street character, and
        ensure there’s a strong voice for those priorities.

    13. Emily Lockhart
        Single mom who moved to Lehi after a divorce and was able to afford housing in
        Holbrook Farms due to its varied product types. Has served 4 years on the Planning
        Commission, gaining deep land-use and process knowledge. Sees each application as
        opportunity for residents and pledges long-term commitment to serving Lehi in any
        capacity.

    14. Jared Peterson
        Well-known in Lehi with a history of community service and recent city -council
        campaign that gave him deep exposure to residents’ concerns (traffic, infrastructure,
        fiscal responsibility, community character). Offers a steady, practical approach;
        understands city processes and budgets. Emphasizes listening, hard questions,
        transparency, fiscal discipline, and continuity—argues that his experience lets him
        contribute immediately to finish the term.

    15. Derek Phillips
        Lifelong Lehi resident, married with five kids. Operates a global sales company based in
        Denver. Loves Lehi but is concerned about public safety, traffic, and infrastructure. Says
        he is heavily invested in Lehi’s long-term future and wants to help shape what happens

    16. Montane Hamilton
        Longtime Lehi resident, deeply involved in local governance starting from HOAs.

        code. Wants Lehi to be a regional leader in “smart development” that reduces water use,

    17. Tyson Hodges
        Construction background (plumbing/HVAC) with 18 years at Intermountain Health.
        Manages facilities for three smaller North Utah County hospitals, overseeing staff, large
        budgets, and long-term planning/forecasting. Married with four children from elementary
        through high school. Wants to apply his facilities and budgeting experience to Lehi,
        collaborate well, and help ensure the city is a good place for his kids to grow up.


    The Council then interviewed each applicant.

    1. Jim Bratt
       Asked how he’d continue the “good work” of prior councils, and work with differing
       council views and budgets. He cited the downtown master plan debate as a model of
       respectful disagreement, stressed common sense, listening to all voices, legal compliance,
       and his experience in PR, church stake councils, and interfaith boards. On budgets, he
       emphasized planning ahead, avoiding excessive debt, and his experience auditing stake
       finances.

    2. Larry Banks
       Questioned on how he’d collaborate with four others as a council member, and where
       he’d help if not appointed. He said councils are for collaboration, you support adopted
       decisions even if you argued another side, and he’d gladly help with infrastructure a nd
       problem‑solving by using data to isolate the “vital few” levers that matter.

    3. Doug Clark
       Pressed on explaining tough decisions to residents, his view of the Morning Vista tax
       subsidy, zoning/code familiarity, transparency limits under NDAs/closed sessions, and
       support for smaller landowners. He said residents need “the why,” not just votes; af ter
       studying the meeting and documents, he accepted the subsidy in principle but disliked the
       non‑specific agreement; admitted a learning curve on zoning but is used to digging into
       documents; said the council must agree on what can be shared within legal limits; and
       argued for city support packages (info, coordination, possible incentives) to help smaller
       commercial landowners too.

    4. Robert Reader
       Asked how the city should collaborate with a new school district and his budget
       philosophy. He stressed keeping “temperature” down as school district changes cause
       anxiety, supporting continuity and structure, and acting as a stabilizing advocate. On
       budgets, he focused on balancing finite resources, understanding community priorities
       now while planning for the future, and accepting that prioritization creates “winners and
       losers,” which is the hardest but most crucial part of governing.

    5. Savannah Johnston
       Questions probed partisanship in city politics, her pro‑density / anti‑car stance vs. the cost
       of making such projects viable (TIF, special taxing powers), how infrastructure shapes
       behavior, separation of powers at the city level, and changing mindsets in a car‑centric
       city. She flatly rejected partisanship in municipal government, said sh e’s uneasy about
       heavy government financing but pro‑market, argued infrastructure (walkability, transit,
       bike lanes) changes choices, clarified council is legislative not executive, and gave
       concrete examples (her husband’s long bike commute and lack of sa fe route to his new
       Lehi business) to support building transit and safe bike links.

    6. Vern Keesler
       Asked what Lehi should improve in long‑range planning, growth mandates from the
       state, the difference between advising and deciding, what Lehi’s done right, and
       budget/balancing public safety. He proposed syncing Lehi’s transportation master
       planning and modeling with MAG’s 4‑year cycle to better time capital projects and
       impact fee updates; defended local control of land use while acknowledging the need for
       a healthy housing mix; described his transition from quiet new planning‑commissioner to
       confident motion‑maker; praised Lehi’s transportation choices and family‑oriented land
       use; and said public safety is a top priority but must be balanced fairly against all
       departments, with no deferred maintenance.

    7. Katie Koivisto
       Joined late and was asked about council service on outside boards, what she’d do
       differently now, and her view of the current budget. She criticized the paid nature of the
       TSSD board and said service roles shouldn’t be fought over for money; emphasized
       keeping one’s oath and not quitting mid‑term; and strongly praised the city’s finance
       team. She characterized her time with the current council as contentious, and expressed
       excitement for the new council.

    8. E LaRell Stephens
       Asked what Lehi has done right over her 20 years here, how he’d prioritize as a new
       council member, park‑tax funding for the arts, and what h is top priority would be.
       He highlighted Traverse Mountain trails and other amenities as successes; admitted he
       needs to dig into current budget numbers; suggested re‑examining how much PARC tax
       money goes to Thanksgiving Point vs. local arts; and said h is top concern is that the
       police department is falling behind peers in staffing and funding, which he’d want to
       address.

    9. Stephen “Polo” Su’a-Filo
       Questioned on how he’d get up to speed, his water expertise and development approvals,
       statewide water‑rate/conservation debates, and future water projects. He said he’s used to
       quickly learning new technical areas and would study plans, budgets, and consult experts;
       argued water should be viewed at a statewide, big‑picture level beyond individual city
       rules; favored public education and transparent metering as powerful conservation tools;
       and gave a nuanced answer on rates, conservation, Central Utah Project history, and the
       current focus on moving water more efficiently while Colorado River allocations are
       sorted out.

    10. Josh Sorenson
        Was not present

    11. Derek Shumway
        Asked how he’d make “best decisions” despite upsetting some, the federal government as
        a partner, disagreeing publicly with colleagues, and handling state/federal overrides of
        city policy. He leaned on his Senate staff experience, saying you run the data, understand
        impacts, communicate clearly and kindly, then sleep at night knowing you did your best;
        said federal and state partners have a role when approached as friends; endorsed
        Governor Cox’s “disagree better” idea; and said the city must stand up respe ctfully when
        higher levels overreach, still communicating decisions clearly to residents.

    12. Jess Green
        Asked about the downtown plan (infill, mixed‑use, density), transparency, downtown
        challenges, and affordability vs. density. He was wary of high‑density and mixed‑use
        downtown, stressing preservation of Main Street, mom‑and‑pop character, and
        infrastructure/traffic concerns; questioned why council members sign NDAs when
        they’re supposed to be transparent to voters; said public safety and infrastructure are his
        top concerns; was blunt that he doesn’t have all the technical answers but feels compelled
        to stand up for small‑town values; and argued the affordability crisis is largely national
        (wages vs. prices, outside buyers, inflation), with high density being a band‑aid, not a full
        solution.

    13. Emily Lockhart
        Asked about her infrastructure‑first work with the legislature, what she learned from
        campaigning, handling state mandates, desired liaison roles, resident property rights vs.
        developer rights, and specific mitigations she’s pushed. She described working o n an
        infrastructure first bill to fund key regional projects, said campaigning deepened her love
        and understanding of residents’ concerns, and emphasized using existing relationships to
        show legislators the real impacts of their bills. She said she’d gladly serve with the
        chamber or historical society (or anywhere needed), framed rights through zoning/use
        tables plus health/safety protections, and cited Planning Commission cases where she
        imposed dust/noise and intensity limits to protect neighbors.

    14. Jared Peterson

       Asked how he judges optimistic developer projections, how to keep younger residents
       engaged in heritage, and how to rebuild Lehi’s tax base after losing businesses to
       neighboring cities. He said today’s data-rich environment lets you benchmark developer
       assumptions against real market prices and costs; argued that inviting younger people into
       existing events (parades, Heritage Days, rodeo, historical work) and giving them real
       assignments builds connection; and urged using the general plan as a living docum ent,
       looking all the way to buildout, and ensuring there are real, accessible sites and

    15. Derek Phillips
        Asked what he liked from current council members, how he’d get up to speed,
        property‑tax inflation adjustments, and police staffing vs. Orem and call‑volume metrics.
        He frankly admitted he’s avoided Lehi politics and would have a steep learning curve,
        but would dive in fast as he’s done in business; is instinctively anti‑tax‑increase but
        would evaluate adjustments case by case; pointed to Lehi’s officer count vs. Orem’s as a
        concern while acknowledging he’d need to reconcile that with leadership’s judgment and
        call‑load data; and stressed he’d want direct conversations with department heads before
        pushing changes.

    16. Montena Hamilton
        Asked about water conservation vs. property rights, financing dense/transit‑oriented
        projects like Thanksgiving Point, what he’s learned chairing the sustainability committee,
        and how he’d talk to residents about growth realities. He said you don’t surprise existing
        owners, but you must immediately fix policy for new development to meet future water
        and sustainability goals; dislikes socializing costs while privatizing gains but accepts
        there are cases where government must help catalyze transit‑linked density; described
        learning to reframe “green” issues in terms conservatives value (self‑reliance, safety, cost
        control); and argued Lehi must accept that growth itself is the core problem and use
        targeted higher density around identified centers and transit to mitigate impacts.

    17. Tyson Hodges
        Asked about his role in budget processes, balancing current vs. future needs, handling
        expectations from supporters, and staff‑stated needs vs. perceived shortages (esp. police).
        He explained Intermountain’s capital process (multiple facilities competing f or limited
        funds, heavy collaboration, a rolling 5‑year plan), said he’d use similar forecasting for
        city capital and staffing, and stressed that promises to specific supporters are
        inappropriate—council service should be about what’s best for Lehi, within legal
        constraints. On police staffing, he noted population comparisons (e.g., to Orem) but
        recognized he’d need to reconcile that with call‑load data and the chief’s professional
        assessment.
