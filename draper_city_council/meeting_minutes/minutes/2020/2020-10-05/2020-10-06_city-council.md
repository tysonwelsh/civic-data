1.0   Presentation: Ranked Choice Voting - Russ Fox

      1.1       Stan Lockhart, Utah Ranked Choice Voting, said that Ranked Choice Voting was
                an innovation that came to Utah five years ago by a conservative grassroots activist
                who believed it was a better way of voting. It was first advocated using it within
                the Republican Party who has used it over the past few years at conventions and
                caucuses. During the last election cycle, both Republicans and Democrats had used
                the system with great success. In a poll of delegates of Republicans, it was found
                that out of 3,700 delegates, 1,100 responded to the poll of which 72% wanted to
                use Ranked Choice Voting in elections. The issue had been before the state
                legislature for several years and was currently looking for a bill to use Ranked
                Choice Voting in partisan primaries to address the plurality issue when candidates
                have far less than 50% of people choosing party nominees when there are multiple
                candidates on the ballot. The legislature also wanted to see how Ranked Choice
                Voting would work in Utah and what voters thought about it. One of the arguments
                against the system, that Mr. Lockhart did not think held water, was that voters
                would be unable to figure out how to rank their votes. Studies have also shown that
                it just wasn’t true. Mr. Lockhart stated that Payson and Vineyard cities had used
                Ranked Choice Voting two years ago. He also said that Amelia Powers, the Utah
                County Clerk, coined the phrase that Ranked Choice Voting was better, faster,
                cheaper, and worked.

                Mr. Lockhart then explained what Ranked Choice Voting was. Voters rank the
                candidates in order of their preference. The first choice is counted just as in a
                traditional plurality election. If the candidate gets over 50% of the vote, they
                automatically become the winner. If no one gets 50% of the vote, then the last-
                place candidate is eliminated. The votes that went to the eliminated candidate are
                redistributed to that voter’s next choice and the votes are counted again. This
                outcome is evaluated. If a candidate has received over 50% of the votes then they
                are the winner. This process is continued until a winner is identified.

                Returning to Payson and Vineyard, Mr. Lockhart explained the results of a poll
                conducted by the Utah County Clerk of voters’ experience with Ranked Choice
                Voting. 86% of respondents said that they found Ranked Choice Voting very much
                or somewhat easy to use. Only 4.2% found it not at all easy to use. 82.5% of voters
                said that Ranked Choice Voting should be used in future elections. One of the
                discoveries was the people were skeptical of Ranked Choice Voting until they used
                it, but that after they had they liked it. There was also a positive candidate
                experience in both cities. 87% of candidates had a positive impression of Ranked
                Choice Voting. No candidate, including winners and losers, reported a negative
                impression. 75% of the candidates wanted to keep using Ranked Choice Voting in
                future elections.

During the past legislative cycle, Utah lawmakers voted to commend Payson and
Vineyard cities, as well as Utah County election officials for conducting the first-
ever Ranked Choice Voting elections in state history. Also, legislators and the
Governor officially encouraged all 249 cities and towns throughout Utah to adopt
Ranked Choice Voting for upcoming municipal elections. Governor Herbert made
the unsolicited comment that,

   Ranked Choice Voting is something I would support. I think it has advantages.
   If I can’t be your first choice, I want to be your second choice. So I think it
   brings a more civil campaign and I think that’s a good thing and we can
   concentrate really on the issues at hand.

Mr. Lockhart then explained why a municipal government should adopt Ranked
Choice Voting
   1. Taxpayer savings for cities. Taxpayer’s dollars are saved by allowing the
       city to hold one election in November rather than two elections. With
       voting, a city is charged based on the number of active registered voters, not
       on the number of people who vote. It costs exactly half the cost of the
       general election to run a primary. A city could save half of their election
       budget by conducting an election with Ranked Choice Voting.

   2. Shorter, less expensive city campaigns. Candidates can focus on a single
      election in November, rather than an August primary followed by a
      November election. With Ranked Choice elections, the candidate filing
      period falls in August, as opposed to in June for plurality elections. The
      time gets to be compacted to run a single election in November.

   3. Ready for cities to implement. ES&S awarded the State of Utah a contract
      for voting equipment that is capable of running Ranked Choice Voting
      elections. That was the same equipment that was used last year in Utah
      County to run the Ranked Choice Voting elections in Payson and Vineyard.

   4. Voters more fully express their will. Ranked Choice Voting allows voters
      to more freely express their vote for the person they most want to support
      even if that candidate isn’t favored to win. No votes are wasted. In the
      2016 presidential election, there was Donald Trump and Hillary Clinton.
      When Evan McMullin also ran, he was viewed as a possible alternative to
      the two primary candidates. His polling when up dramatically. When his
      polling reached about 20% the claim was made that a vote for McMullin
      was a vote for Clinton. This made voters who felt that they had the
      opportunity to vote for an alternative, now had to consider if a vote for
      McMullin meant a vote for Clinton. In the end, his popularity leveled out
      and declined by the time of the election.

   5. A winner by majority vote. The final tally is between the top two
      candidates, with the winner having more than 50% of the vote.

                            Page 3 of 19
   6. Eliminates the spoiler effect. It is not uncommon that the candidate seeks
      out another candidate to run because they think that it will take votes away
      from a more competitive candidate. Ranked Choice Voting eliminates that
      effect as well as vote splitting that can take place in elections when there
      are multiple candidates with similar values and issues which they support.
      Those three candidates can split votes against a single opposing candidate.
      This cannot happen with Ranked Choice Voting. Instead, the candidate
      with the broadest and deepest support is who is elected.

For Ranked Choice Voting to be implemented a notice to the Lieutenant Governor’s
office needs to be filed by March 15, 2021.

Councilmember Roberts said that he had been following Utah Ranked Choice
Voting closely. He said that he felt that our democracy needed lots of structural
changes to make things work better which made him curious about this. He said
that one of the common arguments against Ranked Choice Voting was ballot
exhaustion. A voter might only rank three candidates as opposed to five. He
wanted to know how Mr. Lockhart would respond to that concern.

Mr. Lockhart said that the power was put in the hands of voters. By state law, the
voter can rank every single candidate, but if they choose not to they are making a
proactive decision that they are not willing to rank candidates that they don’t want
to. There were many reasons why a voter might choose to do that. However, by
putting the power into the hands of the voter, they are enabled to have full discretion
on what they do. A person may only vote for three of six candidates because they
don’t know enough about the other candidates. Or it might be because they so
dislike a candidate that they don’t want their vote to go to them at all. As the rounds
go on, their vote may become exhausted, but that is because the voter has made that
choice not because that decision was made for them. Under plurality voting, there
were so many ways to gain the system that it restricts or creates unintended
consequences for a vote. Many times, a voter will have a candidate that they prefer,
but because they don’t have a chance to be the winner they don’t get a vote. Mr.
Lockhart said that he felt that this happened with the primary for the Utah Governor.
He felt that Greg Hughes and Thomas Wright didn’t get some votes because people
perceived it as a two-candidate race. That was something that you don’t have to
worry about with Ranked Choice Voting. You can vote with good conscience for
whoever you want to vote for, be that you rank all candidates or a subset.

Mr. Lockhart said that there were three ways that Draper could administer Ranked
Choice Voting. The first way was to go through Salt Lake County. Draper had
been outsourcing their elections for a little over 20 years. The pitch from the very
beginning in the late 90s when the county asked cities to do this was that cities
would be able to save money because the city would not have to duplicate efforts
and buy voting equipment when the county was already doing so. It made sense
for cities to allow the county to administer the elections. However, it has been
discovered that counties are resistant to any changes in elections. It doesn’t matter
if the change is Vote by Mail, ID at the polls, Same Day voter registration, or
Ranked Choice Voting voting. Because of this Sherrie Swenson, Salt Lake County
Clerk is unwilling to administer Ranked Choice Voting. Utah Ranked Choice
Voting has been in discussion and engaged with her, and had yet to see a valid
reason for her to not participate, but she remains unwilling. The county had just
approved the purchase of new equipment. The ability to execute Ranked Choice
Voting is embedded in the equipment that would be purchased, giving the County
the ability to use Ranked Choice Voting. Utah Ranked Choice Voting felt it
unlikely that she would be willing to. Mr. Lockhart explained that Ms. Swenson
charged cities about $2.50 per voter. In Utah County, they charge over $2.00 per
voter. That means that for every 10,000 voters Draper City is about to save $5,000
on an election using Utah County as opposed to Salt Lake County. Because Draper
is in both counties they have the easy option to use either county clerk to meet their
voting needs. Even in Draper went with plurality voting, they would save money
by using Utah County because it would save Draper many thousands of dollars. If
Draper used Ranked Choice Voting, they would also save themselves the cost of a
primary.

Voting tends to be more issue-oriented than typical plurality elections. He said that
candidates talk not just about the issues they care about, but also the issues their
opponents care about because they want to get the number two vote to their
competitor. He said that issues-based campaigns were good for democracy as
opposed to being based on the politics of distraction that so often happens in our
country and state. He said that City Council races have more negative campaigning
than all of the partisan races combined. For candidates to have an incentive to be
civil to one another is positive. He also said that the strategy was different in a
Ranked Choice Voting election than a plurality election. In a plurality election, a
candidate tries to find their base and plays to the base. In a Ranked Choice election,
however, a candidate has to reach out to all voters because even if you are not their
number one you might be their number two. As a result, voters get more attention
from candidates in a Ranked Choice election. The biggest positive in a Ranked
Choice election, however, is that the will of the voter is more fully expressed.

              from stovetops; whereas residents who have planned and participated in a home
