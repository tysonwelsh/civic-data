# Utah Civic Data — what it is

Decisions about how Utah grows get made in hundreds of separate rooms: city councils,
planning commissions, redevelopment boards, county commissions, regional transportation
bodies, and the Legislature. Each publishes its own records, in its own format, on its
own website. Nobody can see across them.

This project puts all of it in one place, in one shape, so you can ask a single question
and get an answer that spans every level of government.

---

## The five kinds of government it covers

**Cities and towns — 31 of them.** From Salt Lake City to Alta, population 380. For each:
what the council voted on, how every member voted, what the public said, who won the
election, and who funded the campaign.

**Planning commissions.** The appointed boards that review rezones and new development
before the elected council ever sees them. This is where most growth decisions actually
originate — and it's the layer that's hardest to follow from the outside. The project
connects each commission recommendation to the council vote that followed it, so you can
see where elected officials overrode their own professional planners.

**Redevelopment agencies.** The bodies that hold and spend tax-increment money for
downtown projects, housing, and infrastructure. Usually the same people as the city
council, meeting in a different legal capacity — which is exactly why their decisions are
easy to miss. Covered across 24 cities and Salt Lake County.

**Counties — 8 of them.** County commissions and councils, their planning commissions,
and their housing authorities. Counties run elections, govern unincorporated land, and
increasingly set housing policy.

**Regional and state.** The two metropolitan planning organizations that program
transportation dollars across the Wasatch Front and Utah Valley — including every funded
project and growth projections for each city out to 2050. Plus a decade of state
land-use and housing legislation, with every legislator's vote.

## What you can ask it

- How did my council member vote on housing, every time, since they took office?
- Which cities approve the most density — and which reject it?
- Where did a council overrule its own planning commission?
- Who funded this campaign, and how did that official later vote?
- What's actually funded to get built near me in the next five years?
- Every mention of accessory dwelling units, anywhere in Utah, at any level of government.

Questions like these are currently answerable only by someone willing to read thousands
of PDFs across dozens of websites. That's the gap this closes.

---

## Who it's for

Journalists chasing a story on deadline. Researchers studying housing supply. Advocates
who need to know what their city actually did. Officials who want to see what comparable
cities decided. And residents who simply want to find out what happened at the meeting
they missed.

---

## What makes it different

**It spans levels.** Existing tools cover one city, or one dataset. This connects a
neighborhood rezone to the county canvass, the regional transportation plan, and the
state law that constrains all three.

**The campaign finance is new.** No structured database of Utah municipal campaign
finance exists publicly. Much of this was recovered from handwritten and scanned filings
and transcribed by hand — including more than 22,000 individual donations from decades of
paper records that had never been machine-readable.

**It's built to be trusted.** Every record traces back to the government document it came
from. Original wording is preserved exactly as published. Where a city's records are
thin, the data says so rather than papering over it — so nothing here can quietly mislead
you.

**It's designed to be asked, not just downloaded.** A conversational interface is in
development, so you can get an answer without knowing a database exists underneath.
