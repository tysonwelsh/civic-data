       4.2    Informational Presentation on the Salt Lake County Public Safety Bond
              (Presenter,SLCO Chair,Laurie StrinshamX

Salt Lake County Council Chair, Laurie Stringham,spoke about the Public Safety Bond and indicated
that the County Council voted 8-1 in favor ofadding the bond to the 2024 ballot.

The County's needs include:

   • The consolidation and improvement ofthe county jail.

   • Expansion ofthe adult detention center to meet population growth and capacity needs.

   • Increased rehabilitative services and mental health resources.


   • During COVID-19, the County was forced to reopen the Oxbow facility, which had been
       closed for over 10 years, and move people into that jail. They have had to rehabilitate it, and
       it is barely fiinctioning.


   • Oxbow needs to be closed, and its inmates and services transferred to the expanded jail and
       adult detention center.


The County has spent 1 Vi years studying the problems and researching solutions. If they were to
expand the jail to the projected need within the next five years, the cost would be astronomical, so
                                  BLUFFDALE CITY COUNCIL

they needed a more cost-effective way to do it. After extensive research,they concluded that a Justice
and Accountability Center would best suit the County's needs.

Ms. Stringham noted that Utah does not do business like everyone else and many of its jails are
public/private/non-profit partnerships. The Justice and Accountability Center is part of that concept
and a small part of the County's Human Services, Homelessness, and Criminal Justice Action Plan.
It is the most cost-effective alternative to jail for low-level offenders, and it balances accountability
with resources and real solutions—^behavioral, mental health, substance use disorder, medical,job
training, housing, etc. It is connected to the criminal justice system, and not a "glorified homeless
shelter" as some have alleged. Regarding location, the County is considering a parcel near the jail,
as well as others in the area.


The goals ofthe Action Plan are to reduce crime,improve accountability, connect with resources and
real solutions, and break the cycle of repeat offenders. The County's population has grown, but no
jail beds have been added since 2001. The Justice Center will be cost-effective, fiscally conservative,
and responsible, and it is an integral part of the Salt Lake County Human Services, Homelessness,
and Criminal Justice Reform Action Plan. It has bipartisan support from the County Council, as well
as majority support from surveyed community members.

With regard to taxpayer savings from the Justice and Accountability Center, the jail currently costs
$136 per person per day, or $49,640 annually. The Justice and Accountability Center will only cost
$75 per person per day or $27,500 annually, a savings of$60 per person per day or $22,500 annually.
It is also expected to reduce recidivism.


The General Obligation Bond costs include:

   • $100 million for the Justice and Accountability Center.
   • $427 million for the jail expansion.
   • $90 million for maintenance and improvements to the adult detention center and sheriffs
       office.

   • $10 million for demolition ofthe Oxbow facility(costs to be recouped by sale ofthe property).

The County anticipates that the costs will be offset slightly by $20 million from the sale ofthe Oxbow
property, as well as the $100 million that was set aside for deferred maintenance and deliberate
savings for this project. The total bond cost is $507 million, and the impact on residents would be
$4.91 per residence and $8.93 per business per month. Ms. Stringham noted that the average private
property value in Salt Lake County is $602,000.


The County spent approximately 1 '/a years studying the Human Services, Homelessness, and
Criminal Justice Reform Action Plan, which was unveiled on July 24,2024. It was approved in an 8-
1 bipartisan vote in August of 2024. The first public hearing was held on September 19, 2024, and
the second will be held on October 8,2024. Ballots will be mailed on October 15,2024, and election
day is November 5, 2024. If approved, work will begin in January of2025.

Ms. Stringham stated that the Oxbow facility was not intended to be put back in service, and the only
investments that have been made since reopening have been to keep it functioning. They researched
different types of facilities and programs throughout the United States, including the Utah Juvenile
Justice Program. Utah has been redeveloping its juvenile justice centers under a new model. If a
child commits a crime, the system determines if there are serious issues that need to be addressed to
gauge where the child belongs in the system. Ifthey determine,for example,that the child is homeless
and in need ofservices,instead ofgoing into thejuvenilejustice system,they are moved into a services
area where they provide help and support to be successful moving forward. If the same child is
charged with a future crime, they no longer have the option ofservices. Instead, they are moved into
the system based on those choices. There are levels within the justice system. For example, in the
lower level, the child is told that if they go to school every day, get good grades, and follow the
required steps, they can work their way out ofthe system. If they do not follow the steps, they will
go up through the levels ofthe system.


That is the concept behind the Justice and Accountability Center. It gives people the opportunity to
either work themselves down and out of the system or up and further into the system. The Coxmty
hopes that people who arejust being rolled out will take advantage ofthat option and work themselves
down and out ofthe system in a way that saves money long-term.

In response to a question from Council Member Crockett, Ms. Stringham clarified that the repayment
period is 20 years. There are two tranches, one in 2029 and another in 2030 or 2031, so the bond
payments will come online in portions. Mayor Hall was provided with data on other justice centers,
rehabilitation rates, etc., and Mrs. Stringham can provide additional information via email as well.


Council Member Crockett liked the idea ofthe levels and the focus on rehabilitation and asked what

is being done to prevent children from entering the juvenile justice system in the first place.
Ms. Stringham stated that the County's plan is for the adult system, not the juvenile system. She used
the juvenile system as an example. Council Member Crockett stated that something should be done
to prevent them from entering the system and asked if the County is discussing that at all.
Ms. Stringham stated that a lot ofat-risk youth are homeless, which is under the County'sjurisdiction,
but the juvenile justice system is managed by the state. They are having a lot more success with this
new model.


The first model was established in northern Utah. They then used the cost savings of offering
wraparound services and the resulting decrease in recidivism to revamp a second juvenile justice
court. They have since used the funds saved on each site to complete all the juvenile justice sites in
Utah. There are signs that they are making headway, which is why the County believes something
similar should be done with adults. Many adults come into the system as young adults, and the process
should be different for someone who is 18 versus someone who is 35 or 50, or for people who made
one bad decision versus someone who has made multiple bad decisions. How to balance those
differences has been a major discussion.


They met with judges, sheriffs, district attorneys, public defenders, public mental health officials, the
office ofhomelessness,legislators, and elected officials from different parts ofthe county. Their goal
was to identify the gaps in the system and determine what was needed to fill them,and it became clear
that a large gap is in wraparound services as people are released from jail. A lot of people never go
to prison; they serve their time in the county jail. Some people lose their jobs or ability to support
themselves because they are in jail for extended periods waiting for a hearing. Ifthey are not charged
with a major crime, they can be stepped down. Some drug courts already do that. People are
monitored but they can still go to work every day and not lose their job in the process.

A lot ofnon-profits that help people being released from jail also work with the homeless. They serve
different demographics and have different goals, but they all work with people who would otherwise
fall through the gaps. That is where they came up with the concept ofthe Justice and Accountability
Center. It will have beds for high-level mental health patients that those services will not take, as well
as those who just need support to get out ofthe system and back on their feet.

In response to a question from Council Member Crockett, Ms. Stringham stated that the jail already
has enough personnel to cover jail expansion. They have the staff, but not the beds and space for it.
Regarding the Justice and Accountability Center, a lot ofother groups will come into that space. The
management cost is not as great as might be expected, and they are already researching and preparing
to apply for available grants. Their goal is to fully fund the facility through those other resources.

Council Member Lord asked about the projected construction timeline. Ms. Stringham stated that it
will take approximately 18 to 24 months for the jail, and they expect the Justice and Accountability
Center to be completed within that timeframe as well. If it does not pass, there is currently no Plan
B. A jail expansion would be more expensive, but that may be brought back for a vote to determine
if it is what citizens prefer. They have been trying to find a way to cut the cost ofthe jail. Services
cost more than a jail when you look specifically at personnel, but they cost significantly less when
you consider the decrease in recidivism.


Ms. Stringham indicated that Oregon's State Legislature passed a law requiring this type of step-
down, lower security center in every county, and positive statistical data is coming out of it. Their
cost per bed is significantly lower. People still serve their time and receive services, but ifthey follow
the rules, they are also able to leave the facility to work and spend time with their families.

Mayor Pro Tempore Austin thanked Ms. Stringham for the presentation.

         7.1      Resolution No. 2024-67 - Amending the Bluffdale Employee Policies and
                  Procedures Manual related to Salary for Full-Time Firefighters. (StaffPresenters
                  Bruce Kartchner and Todd SwinsleX

Human Resources and Payroll Manager,Todd Swingle, presented the Staff Report and indicated that
the amendment includes the following administrative changes:

     • Appendix to the Policies and Procedures: Removal ofthe organizational chart.

     • 1.10.020 Implementation: Removal ofthe line "and attached to these policies and procedures
        for reference."


     • 1.20.020 Employee Definitions: Expanded to include the full-time and part-time fire, EMS,
         and regular employees. Full-time firefighters and EMS employees work two 24-hour shifts
         every six days on a regular basis. Part-time employees work one 24-hour shift on a regular
         basis.



     • 1.20.040 Job Descriptions: Changed to include City Manager or designee.

     • 1.40.040 Exempt and Non-Exempt Employees: Assistant City Manager and Assistant City
         Engineer positions were added to the list ofexempt employees.
