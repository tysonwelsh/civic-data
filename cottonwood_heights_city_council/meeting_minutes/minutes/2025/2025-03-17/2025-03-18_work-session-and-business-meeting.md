3.0     STAFF REPORTS

        3.1     Lobbyist Report – Greg Curtis/Brian Allen.

Mayor Weichers invited lobbyists Brian Allen, Greg Curtis, Chantel Nate, and Kyle Jensen to
speak about the recent legislative session. Mr. Allen reported that the session started off quiet, but
got very busy toward the end.

SB 337, introduced by Senator Cullimore, did not pass. They had concerns about how this bill
could have impacted City projects, and Mr. Allen believes they would reintroduce it in 2026.
Mayor Weichers stated that the bill would have created a state agency with the authority over local
Land Use decision. Mr. Allen reported that veto approval for cities was negotiated toward the end
of the session.

HB 550, introduced by Representative Peterson, started out as exempting government entities from
inspection fees. It was negotiated down to only apply to water conservancy districts, but they still
oppose the bill.

SB 340, introduced by Senator Ipson, was the Protected Persons bill. It stated that if a person has
had a credible threat against their life or safety in the last five years, they could work with the
Department of Public Safety to obtain a certificate bypassing municipal codes for security
measures.

SB 328, introduced by Senator Stevenson, would allow local governments to waive proximity
requirements for alcohol licenses around playgrounds and parks. The bill initially failed, but was
reintroduced and passed. Mayor Weichers stated that the original ordinance also prohibits these
establishments within a certain distance of libraries and some other facilities and asked Mr. Allen
to verify where the bill applies.

HB 320, introduced by Representative Shepherd, would have allowed municipalities to impose a
fine of up to $5,000 if someone had three similar criminal violations within a 12-month period. It
did not pass.

HB 300, Amendments to Election Law, was highly publicized and controversial. The fourth
version passed. It still allows voting by mail, but after 2028 identification must be presented at
the time the ballot is requested and the last four digits of the identification must be included on the
ballot. Signature verification will still be required.

HB 256, Municipal and County Zoning Amendments, is in regard to enforcement of short-term
rental laws. It allows cities to look at listing sites and request that noncompliant listings be
removed. The final version was not as strong as they had hoped, but it did pass. Council Member
Hyland added that they can provide the listing to the State as evidence that Transient Room Taxes
(“TRT”) should be collected on the property. The governor had not signed the bill yet.

HB 40, School Safety Amendments, was amended significantly. ULCT worked with the sponsor
on modifications, and the seventh substitute passed. It takes some burden off of local law
enforcement. They are required to coordinate with the county on security plans, but it removes
other responsibilities they might have had for people inside the school.

SB 277, Government Records Management Amendment, transitions the seven-member volunteer
board to an administrative law judge. This will speed up the Government Records Access and
Management Act (GRAMA”) process. It does not change how records are accessed. Instead of a
monthly meeting by a volunteer board, records requests will be reviewed daily by a full-time
administrative law judge, and hearings will be held more frequently. Mr. Allen stated that he
believes it will make records more accessible and less expensive because they will have lower
legal fees. Mr. Topham agreed and noted that the City had encouraged and assisted the legislation.
He thanked Mr. Allen and Ms. Nate for their efforts in helping to get it passed.

HB 444, Data Privacy Amendments, added a member to the privacy committee representing cities,
changed annual reporting to December, and pushed contractor requirements to 2026. It also
removed some confusing language.

HB 355, Mining and Critical Infrastructures, was opposed by the ULCT. Representative Snider
made some concessions that improved the bill. It clarifies how a gravel pit can expand into land
they currently own. Cities will not be able to prohibit expansion if the company owns the land. In
response to a question, Mr. Allen reported that the land must be contiguous.

HB 295, Municipal Services Fee and Political Subdivision Lien Amendments would give
municipalities the ability to apply a lien if bills are not paid. It passed the House but failed before
being assigned to a committee in the Senate.

HB 44, Transient Room Tax Amendments, sponsored by Representative Bolinder, allows cities to
work with the county to obtain more of the tax revenues based on actual impacts. Mr. Allen
believes Cottonwood Heights could make a good case for an increase.

HB 511 would have required local governments to ask residents if they would approve of an
increase in property taxes. The bill was sent to an interim committee for study.

HB 454 and SB 310 would have allowed a religious exemption for utility and transportation taxes.
They worked hard to defeat both bills. Representative Peterson was not happy that her bill was
amended in the Senate, and it did not pass when it came back to the House. The issue will likely
come back in the next legislative session.

Mayor Weichers asked for more information on the ULCT’s opposition to the bill. Mr. Allen
stated that they were concerned about the precedent. The Transportation Utility Fee is a user fee;
everyone who uses the facilities pays the fee. People going to religious venues use those facilities.
If they exempt religious facilities, there is a concern that they will next exempt other nonprofits.
Mr. Allen indicated that they supported the ULCT’s position on the bill. However, at the Council’s
direction, they could support the bill in the next legislative session.

Mayor Weichers stated that he did not believe it was worthwhile to make such a large entity as the
Church angry over a bill that would not result in the loss of a lot of money. Mr. Allen stated that
it would impact other cities more than Cottonwood Heights. In response to a question, he clarified
that the exemption would apply to all church-owned buildings, including office buildings. In
response to a question from Mr. Shipp, Mr. Allen reported that it could also be used as a precedent
to exempt those properties from stormwater fees and other user fees. However, they work for the
City, not the ULCT, and would follow the Council’s direction.

HB 533, County Formation Amendments, would have created a petition process to split counties.
It failed. Mr. Jensen reported that he spoke to the bill’s sponsor, who plans to study the matter
further but expressed the belief that the population of Salt Lake County is too high. Mr. Allen
added that the bill followed many of the same arguments that were used to split the school district.
Mayor Weichers stated that he did not mind if it passes because it would force Salt Lake County
to pay attention.

HB 198 regarding billboards passed. It was amended so that if a sign is impacted by highway
expansion, it can move anywhere along the highway, but it must be within the same city. If it goes
outside the city, mutual agreement is required. If a location cannot be found due to city or state
issues, that party must pay for the sign.

HB 504, Conflict of Interest Disclosures by Municipal Candidates, passed.

SB 195, Transportation Amendments, targeted Salt Lake City and did pass. Council Member
Hyland stated that she attended a Utah Foundation meeting where the bill was discussed, and two
parts of the bill would affect the City. It requires connectivity planning and clarifies who has the
responsibility to maintain street lighting. The City would have to maintain any decorative lighting
installed on State highways, but the State would be responsible for lighting at intersections.
Council Member Hyland added that it would allow Tax Increment Financing (“TIF”) funds to be
used for corridor preservation.

In response to a question from Mayor Weichers, Mr. Allen stated that HB 465 was amended to
allow for coordinated efforts between the State and cities to address homelessness, but it does not
allow the State to completely take the efforts over.

In response to a question raised by Council Member Hyland, Mr. Jensen reported that funding was

the Fifth of Fifth sales tax. Funds will begin accruing on July 1, 2025. The Wasatch Front

Mr. Curtis stated that he believes SB 195 only provides TIF funds for state routes. Part of the

municipalities’ residents to use their roads, so they authorize cul-de-sacs instead of stubbing the

Regarding HB 502, Mr. Curtis reported that 2024 HB 488 changed the amount of the Cottonwood
Canyon fund from a fixed amount to a percentage to be used for canyon transportation. HB 502
changed it to allow that fund to grow from sales taxes. Several opponents saw it as an attempt to
fund the gondola. The intention was to fund Phase 1 of the environmental study for the
connectivity and transit hub at the mouth of Big Cottonwood Canyon. Years ago, $13 million was
appropriated to purchase land, but that purchase never took place. The Utah Department of
Transportation (“UDOT”) is still holding the money. HB 502 directs that revenue specifically to
ingress and egress in the transit hub. In response to a question from Mayor Weichers, Mr. Curtis
clarified that the bill does not affect Cottonwood Heights’ traction control program with UDOT.

Mr. Curtis reviewed the Affordable Housing Infrastructure Grants and their potential impacts and
noted that the definition of “affordable housing” was changed at the last minute and tied to median
income. A $70 million bond was authorized in Salt Lake County that will apply to both for-sale
and for-rent housing.

Mr. Curtis reported that significant state dollars have been put toward the revitalization project in
the downtown Salt Lake City tourist district. When they looked at local dollars, they were
surprised to realize that approximately $60 million per year is flowing to municipalities in Salt
Lake County from the Fourth of the Fourth and Fifth of the Fifth sales taxes. Salt Lake City had
to commit a significant portion of its Fifth of the Fifth taxes to the infrastructure associated with
the downtown revitalization.

        3.5     Public Works Grant Funding– Public Works Director/City Engineer, Matt
                Shipp.

Mr. Shipp presented the Staff Report with recommendations on Capital Project priorities.

The Public Works Capital Road Maintenance Budget had a balance of $4.25 million as of February
1, 2025. Of that, an estimated $3.5 million will be used to fund scheduled maintenance projects
in this fiscal year. Approximately $300,000 will be used to fund unplanned projects like sidewalk
trip hazard repair, unanticipated road repairs, and project change orders. Approximately $430,000
will be carried forward to Fiscal Year 2025-2026. Scheduled projects for the current year total
approximately $3.8 million.

The annual allotment to the capital Road Maintenance Budget is anticipated to be reduced from
$3.15 million to $2 million. Including the carryover, the total budget will be approximately $2.43
million. Anticipated projects for FY 2026 will cost $2.8 million, resulting in a shortfall of
approximately $400,000.

Pending grant projects would cost a maximum of $1.4 million. Those include:
   • Bengal Boulevard and Highland Drive intersection. City match: $219,193
   • Highland Drive Trail Phase 1. City contribution: $700,000
   • Highland Drive Trail Phase 2. City match: $241,784
   • Highland Drive Phase 3. City match: $35,000

   • Safe Streets and Roads for All (“SS4A”) Safety Study. City match: $100,000

no longer be available. If the funds come through, the City will have until 2027 to complete the

it was determined that the study likely will not cost $500,000. Mayor Weichers suggested
postponing the project. If it is determined that the City’s cost will be lower, they can do a Budget
Amendment or wait until the next budget year.
