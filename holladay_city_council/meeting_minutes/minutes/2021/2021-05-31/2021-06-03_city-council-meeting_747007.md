Mr. Francom explained that the State and Salt Lake County had been trying to address the urgent
need for affordable housing. He considered it to be a valiant cause. Between the housing market and
the uncertainty of an inflationary economy, the need was extremely urgent. In response to the
requirements under SB 34, the City of Holladay committed to allowing people with various incomes
to benefit from and participate in various aspects of neighborhood and community life. The City also
set a goal to accommodate new dwellings by focusing on appropriate additional dwellings within
existing and redeveloping neighborhoods. It was also stated that the City intends to study and adopt
a new ADU ordinance in 2021 to comply with the required third strategy per SB 34.

There had been several concerns raised during the last several Council Meetings. In discussions
related to the implementation of HB 82, there was concern about creating winners and losers by
excluding some neighborhoods. Mr. Francom understood that excluding small lot sizes, such as R-
1-4, but felt that the current Code could handle that through setbacks and the percentage of the
dwelling footprint. He did not believe it was fair to create a 25% exclusion area by any other means.
Additionally, there were concerns about there being too many adults in rental units. However, a study
conducted in Portland, Oregon found that 94% of ADUs were rented to two or fewer adults and 50%
were rented to a friend or family member. Mr. Francom also understood the concerns related to
parking. He was in support of requiring an off-street parking space. Additionally, he was in support
of charging a fee for adding an ADU but suggested that it be a one-time fee rather than an ongoing
fee.

Mr. Francom wanted to see the City of Holladay do more than less when in terms of ADUs. He did
not believe that allowing external ADUs would cause overcrowding in neighborhoods. It was
important to address the shortage of affordable housing as growth continued to take place in the City.
Multiple studies showed that allowing ADUs had a nominal effect on density but organically
increased affordable housing.

When speaking with neighbors and other City residents, a large majority believed that a garage
apartment or backyard casita should be allowed. Mr. Francom noted that ADUs would allow
homeowners to invest in their properties and increase property values. People could subsidize their
house payments or provide additional income for those in need. It would be possible to preserve the
character of a single-family neighborhood while still providing additional housing. He made note of
the high rent costs in the City and reported that one study showed that 50% of ADUs were below
market value rents. That same study found that 70% of ADU renters had only one car and 90% had
zero children. Mr. Francom stressed the importance of affordable housing.

ADU growth was slow and organic. Cities that had encouraged ADUs only had 1% to 5% of homes
with an ADU. Mr. Francom commented that he and others in his neighborhood would like the City
Council to consider doing more than just interior ADU requirements. There could be more lenient
height requirements for garage apartments and a code that allowed external detached rental units.

        a.      Discussion on Tax Relief Programs – Wayne Cushing, Salt Lake County Treasurer.
Ms. Chamness reported that Salt Lake County Treasurer, Wayne Cushing, was present to discuss the
tax relief programs offered through Salt Lake County. He noted that some might apply to those in
the City of Holladay. The two ways to obtain tax relief were described as follows:
    • Reduce the value of the home that taxes are being paid on; and
    • Obtain a tax credit (deduct the credit from the amount owed on the property tax).

The most popular tax relief program currently was for veterans. Mr. Cushing explained that any
veteran who has served and received a disability during that service is eligible to receive tax relief
through a reduction of the value of their home. That reduction was based on the percentage of the
disability received while in the service. He reported that in the City of Holladay, 104 veterans
participated in the program.

Mr. Cushing also discussed the Circuit Breaker program, which is a State program the County has
joined. The intention was to help senior citizens who are making very little money and provide them
with a break on expenses. Anyone over the age of 65 with an income of less than $34,600 can obtain
relief. Renters can apply to the State of Utah through the Utah State Tax Commission. Alternatively,
the County would administer relief to homeowners and reduce the amount of property tax owed.
Mr. Cushing explained that the credit received was on a gradual scale. The lower the income, the
higher the credit. He noted that for homeowners, the credit would come from the State but the County
administered it. At the end of the year, the State would reimburse the County for that expense.

In 2020, 2,400 residents in the County qualified for the Circuit Breaker program, and between $1.3
million and $1.4 million worth of taxes were deducted from that group. Mr. Cushing commented that
the Circuit Breaker program was able to assist a lot of elderly residents. However, there were not a
lot of people that qualified due to the $34,600 limit. In the City of Holladay, only 86 residents applied
and qualified. Mr. Cushing reported that the Circuit Breaker program included an income test that
was based on household income. Mr. Cushing noted that there had been a decrease over the last four
years of residents who qualified for the Circuit Breaker program.

Mr. Cushing explained that for the last 10 years, the County had done everything possible to find
those who qualified for the program. That included attending senior expos and annual visits to senior
centers to inform residents about the Circuit Breaker program. Despite those efforts, he felt there
were thousands of residents throughout the County that would qualify if they knew about the program.
He believed the $34,600 income level was not keeping pace with the needs of residents. They were
working with the Legislature to try to address that issue.

Mr. Cushing noted that the Legislature had a theory that they were looking into. Rather than a tax
relief program, a deferral program was being considered where residents would not need to pay the

        b.     Continued Discussion on Accessory Dwelling Units (“ADU”).
Mayor Dahle reported that the goal for this item was to determine a recommended direction for
IADUs. That recommendation would be forwarded to the Planning Commission. The Planning
Commission would then pursue their own independent review of the IADU materials presented. There
would be discussions and a public hearing at the Planning Commission level. The Planning
Commission would then pass their recommendation to the City Council. The Council would take the
Planning Commission recommendation into consideration and there would be another public hearing
at the City Council level. Mayor Dahle noted that the goal was to have something completed by the
end of August.

Previous discussions had taken place related to IADUs. Much of the debate centered around the
possible 25% exclusion area that was permitted in single-family residential zones. Council Member
Gibbons noted that his view of the 25% exclusion area had changed since the last City Council
Meeting. The main reason had to do with older residents on fixed incomes who may need alternative
sources of income. A lot of residents with that need lived in smaller lots. If an exclusion area was
applied to smaller lots, it would be difficult for certain residents to have an alternate source of income.
Council Member Gibbons noted that he did support an exclusion in areas where additional on-street
parking or congestion would create public safety issues.

Mayor Dahle reported that Council Member Peterson initially showed support for the 25% exclusion
area but after further discussions, came to the same conclusion as Council Member Gibbons. She did
not want to support the exclusion area because it would create winners and losers. There were also
enforceability issues to consider. Mayor Dahle felt it was important to consider the intention of the
State Legislation and the issues related to affordable housing. He was aligned with Council Member
Gibbons and suggested passing along a recommendation to the Planning Commission that stated:

   •   If there are specific areas within the City where allowing IADUs in the neighborhood would
       create physical safety issues, an exclusion would be appropriate. Otherwise, an exclusion
       would not be appropriate.

Council Member Durham wondered if the 25% exclusion area was inclusive of lots under 6,000
square feet or whether that was a separate exception. City Attorney, Todd Godfrey, commented that
the law was not specific on that. It simply mentioned “25% of the primarily single-family residential
zones.” However, there was a provision in the statute that allowed the City not to permit IADUs on
lots of 6,000 square feet or less.

Council Member Fotheringham commented that there had been discussions about ADUs prior to
HB 82. Those discussions had been put on hold until the City knew what would be required from the
Legislature. During the previous discussions, there had been conversations about external ADUs.
The City of Holladay currently has an ordinance on the books related to guest houses. There could
be a guest house or casita but there was a rule that it needed to be on property that was half an acre or
more. When looking at the ADU ordinance, he felt it was important to address external ADUs as
well.

                v.     Parks and Public Works – Gina Chamness, City Manager.
Ms. Chamness reported that the budget included a $242,000 increase for the Salt Lake County Public
Works contract. The largest portion of that was due to a change in how the City budgeted for slurry
seal. It had been budgeted separately in the Capital Budget last year but had been moved back into
the contract. City Staff had anticipated that they would be able to contract for the slurry seal
independently at a lesser cost, however, that did not happen. As a result, that cost was being assumed
into the Salt Lake County Public Works contract again.

Ms. Chamness explained that Salt Lake County had also reclassified many of its employees. That
resulted in an increase of $80,000 to the overall budget. That increase was reflected in the $242,000
number. She noted that the maps reviewed by Mr. Bunch earlier in the meeting did not include
maintenance costs that would be paid as part of the contract. There would likely be other road
improvements, which would increase the 16.5% number across the City. There were some other small
changes being made in Public Works. For instance, there was some double counting of waste
container expenses and some funding had been added for street sign replacement.

In parks, there had been some reductions in both equipment costs as well as building and ground
maintenance costs to better reflect the actuals. Ms. Chamness noted that there had been money in the
budget for the last several years that was set aside for a clean-up event that used to happen at City
Hall. That event had not happened for the last several years due to the constraints of the site. Since
that event would not take place on a regular basis, the funding had been removed from the budget.

Council Member Fotheringham further discussed the clean-up event. He noted that the event had
been discontinued but then there had been dumpsters delivered in neighborhoods by Wasatch Front
Waste & Recycling. The latter was recently changed and a reservation needed to be made in order to
obtain a dumpster. He wondered whether that could be looked at again. Council Member Gibbons
explained that the change from Wasatch Front Waste & Recycling had been due to COVID-19. It
had continued this year partly because decisions had to be made ahead of time and partly as an
experiment to determine whether or not that system should continue.

Council Member Durham asked about maintenance on the Lynne Lane Walkway. He wanted to try
to meet with the residents in that area over the summer but felt it was important to get a sense of what
could be done for them. Ms. Chamness commented that there was some flexibility in terms of
funding. Included in the Capital Projects Fund was an unrestricted Capital Projects line item. If the
maintenance was under the $10,000 range, it would be possible to accommodate that from a budget

were numbers per year, there was no information about whether those calls had come from within
Holladay. She added that the program was quite expensive when the numbers were broken down per
