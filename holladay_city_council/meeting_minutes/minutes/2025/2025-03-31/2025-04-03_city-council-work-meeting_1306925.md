II.    Granite School District Boundary Presentation – Steve Hogan.
Director of Planning and Boundaries for Granite School District, Steve Hogan, introduced himself
to the City Council and reviewed some of his professional background. He shared the Granite
School District Elementary Boundary and Closure Study (Area 5) presentation slides and
explained that this is the Area 5 Elementary Study. A map was shared highlighting the location
of the elementary schools. Mr. Hogan reviewed a table with the schools as well as relevant
information, such as the enrollment numbers and the building capacity. The Facility Condition
Index (“FCI”) is listed for each. No building on the list is unsafe, but certain numbers indicate that
there will be more maintenance needed and the building is closer to a remodel or a new build.

As for why the study has been conducted, it has to do with declining student enrollment.
Mr. Hogan shared some information related to enrollment, including a graph that tracks the 20-
Year Enrollment numbers. This past year, the number dropped to 55,546 enrolled students. He
next shared a graph that looks at the 20-Year Enrollment + 5-Year Forecast. This does not simply
include projections from the Granite School District, as a demographic company was hired to look
at and confirm the data. It is expected there will be a decrease of approximately 1,000 students
per year. Based on the projection, there will be 47,518 students enrolled in the Granite School
District in 2029. This is not a district-specific issue, but reflects a pattern that is being seen across
the State

Mr. Hogan pointed out that there is often a misconception that larger schools mean larger
classrooms with more students in each, but that is not the case. There is a student teacher ratio, so
if there are more students, there are more teachers. There is an economy of scale that needs to be
considered.

Mr. Hogan explained that schools are not closed to save money. Schools are closed to ensure the
sizing is correct and a better educational experience is provided. He shared the Study Process
information and explained that it is almost a full calendar year process. There are different
meetings held, and a lot of feedback is received. There is a final decision made in December, with
any changes implemented the next fall. There will be no changes made in this next school year.

Council Member Drew Quinn asked about the Dual Language Immersion programs. Mr. Hogan
explained that with a program like that, it is almost like running two schools. To have some of the
special programs, there needs to be more students. Council Member Emily Gray asked if the
students in these programs are included in the school population numbers, which was confirmed.
Mr. Hogan suggested that Council Members look at the website, as it shows how many students
live in each area, how many students are attending, and where those students are coming from.

Discussions were had about school closures. Mr. Hogan reiterated that there are many
considerations. A few years back, there was a study conducted with over 20 schools, and there
was one school closure. There have been other years where there were three of the nine schools
studied that were closed. There are a lot of factors that need to be considered in this process. Mr.
Hogan shared an example scenario with Redwood Elementary School and the different factors
considered. During the study, it is necessary to look at the surrounding schools and consider the
impact a school closure would have on all of the remaining schools. Additional example scenarios
were shared.

Mr. Hogan explained that data is considered, but there is also a desire to receive feedback from
others. There is a lot that can be learned through public input. He further reviewed the study
process. There have been some meetings held already, and there are some more meetings
scheduled. He asked that those interested submit feedback on the website, as he is interested in
comments and creative suggestions. December is when the final approval will take place.

Council Member Gray asked who looks at the data and determines how much each factor weighs
in. Mr. Hogan reported that the Population Analysis Committee (“PAC”) discusses those details.
At an average meeting, there are between 25 and 30 participants. There is not always consensus,
but there is normally enough feedback received to take recommendations back to the Board. In
the end, it is a Board decision that is made. He shared additional information about PAC members.

Mayor Dahle expressed appreciation for the presentation and the information shared. The City
Council has already received some inquiries, so any information provided to the Council is
appreciated. Mayor Dahle noted that the QR code can be included in the Holladay Journal.

III.     2025 Legislative Update – David and Ashley Spatafore.
Mayor Dahle reported that David and Ashley Spatafore are present to share a 2025 Legislative
update with the City Council. Ms. Spatafore discussed some of the housing-related bills and
informed the Council that the Housing Commission will continue. House Bill (“H.B.”) 37 – Utah
Housing Amendments deals with housing development and housing policy. The density overlay
can allow for a density bonus. If this is implemented, restrictions can be placed This bill gives
cities the tool to place certain restrictions with additional density.

H.B. 256 – Municipal Zoning Amendments is a bill that modifies short-term rental provisions
related to the authority of municipalities and counties. It clarifies that municipalities and counties

Senate Bill (“S.B.”) 181 – Housing Affordability Amendments was reviewed. Ms. Spatafore
explained that this bill enacts and amends provisions related to housing and affordable housing
funding. It enacts provisions of municipal land use regulations with regard to certain types of
parking spaces. Mayor Dahle asked how this will impact the City. It was noted that a slight

will hopefully assist with homeowner insurance. She shared additional information about the bill
and noted that the County will oversee this process. Council Member Paul Fotheringham is
curious about some of the unknowns, such as what the role of the Unified Fire Authority (“UFA”)
will be and whether they will receive a portion of the fee.

Member Ty Brewer asked about insurance companies and whether this bill will compel insurance
companies to provide some sort of coverage. Ms. Spatafore explained that it is possible to share
the risk assessment and mitigation efforts. That can be used to show the fire reduction efforts.

H.B. 198 – Highway Expansion Impacts on Signage Amendments was discussed. This bill amends
provisions related to the relocation of a billboard due to road construction or other highway
changes. It pushes relocation more clearly onto local government. If local government somehow
prohibits relocation, then there is a concern that the local government will need to cover the cost.
He is thankful this is not something that will have much of an impact in Holladay. The Council
reviewed the bill language

H.B. 502 – Transportation and Infrastructure Funding Amendments and the process that took
place. On the last night of the Session, there were certain changes made to the bill. It provided
funding to the County of the First Class Infrastructure Bank Fund, which was a new concept. He
reported that Salt Lake County can enact the “fifth of a fifth,” which is an extra 0.2% of the sales
tax and he has heard that will happen as soon as July. Part of that would go to the cities, part would
go to Salt Lake County, part would go to the new County of the First Class Infrastructure Bank
Fund, and part would be used to fund the express bus route on 5600 West. City Manager, Gina
Chamness, asked if the Legislature would control this fund. Mr. Spatafore believed that it was
something that needs to be discussed. The Legislature is going to appropriate it, but there should
be a discussion at the Conference of Mayors level about the development of a process that looks
at what the infrastructure needs are in the County. If the residents are paying into this, then there
should be a fair and open process that is established.

Mr. Spatafore further discussed H.B. 502 and the last-minute changes that were included. Part of
the infrastructure money authorized the issuance of up to $70 million in General Obligation
Transportation Bonds for affordable housing grant programs in Salt Lake County. This is
something that Holladay might be able to benefit from, depending on the rules that are developed.
However, he expressed concerns about the Legislature making determinations about local funding.

IV.     Review of City Survey – Kyrene Gibb with Y2 Analytics.
Mayor Dahle reported that Kyrene Gibb from Y2 Analytics is present to share information about
the City survey that was conducted. Ms. Gibb reviewed some of the key findings from the survey,
which are as follows:
    • Respondents believe that Holladay is heading in the right direction and give their quality
        of life an overall rating of 82/100. 90% respondents are at least somewhat likely to
        recommend Holladay to their friends and family;
    • In the open-ended responses, many people mentioned Holladay’s small-town charm,
        natural beauty, and community as reasons to why they love the City. Some raised concerns
        about population growth and traffic as things to prioritize in the next 3-5 years;
    • A total of 65% of respondents believe they are receiving an excellent or good service value
        for their tax dollars. Among individual City services, community events, garbage
        collection, and fire/emergency were highly rated. When asked where they would invest
        their budget for improvements, respondents allocated more money on average to surface
        maintenance of streets and roads than other services;
    • A large majority of respondents (82%) do not use public transport, and among these non-
        users, 41% remain unlikely to use public transit even if proposed changes are implemented;
        and
    • Majority of respondents (82%) agree that sustainability is an important goal for Holladay
        to pursue. They believe preserving and growing trees and water conservation should be
        the highest priority projects to meet this goal.

Ms. Gibb shared information about the survey methodology. For this survey, there was a new
random sample of residents used and there were 800 Holladay residents who completed the survey.
. Interviews were completed online between February 20 and March 17, 2025, and the outreach
methods included postcard invitations and text message reminders as well as some email
invitations. The interviews conducted result in a margin of error of plus or minus 3.4 percentage
points.

The City Health and Quality of Life information was reviewed. Ms. Gibb reported that on average,
Holladay residents rate the quality of life at 82, which is five points higher than in 2022. There is
a positive attitude about the quality of life in the City overall. Across the different Council
Districts, the average is 80 or higher, so there are not specific areas of the City that are expressing
disproportionate levels of discontent.

There were high approval ratings for City Officials, with 82% of respondents approving of the
City Council and Mayor, which is slightly higher than in 2022. District 3 had an especially strong
approval rating. The vast majority of residents are at least somewhat likely to recommend
Holladay to friends and family. This has increased slightly since 2022, from 88% to 90%. Some
of the things residents particularly like about the City include the location, sense of community,
the aesthetics of the community, and the quality of the neighbors and neighborhoods. As for what
sets Holladay apart from other communities along the Wasatch Front, residents mentioned the

sense of community, trees, and charm. The main issues that residents would like to see the City
focus on in the next three to five years are related to growth, traffic, and housing.

The City Services information was reviewed. Approximately two-thirds of residents believe they
receive an excellent or good value for their tax dollars, which has increased by eight percentage
points compared to 2022. Overall, a strong majority of residents feel it is safe to live in Holladay
and it is a great place to raise a family. There is an increase in those numbers compared to 2022.

The one area where there is a little bit more division is the share of residents who feel the City is
growing and developing in a positive way. 54% agree with that sentiment, which is still higher

Fotheringham pointed out that the word growth in an infill community can scare some residents.
As for business opportunities in the City, there were additional kinds of businesses that residents
would like to see, such as locally owned restaurants and cafes and locally owned retail stores.

Community events, City parks, and emergency preparedness are the highest rated services relative
to the other in-house City services. Overall, the services are moderately rated with the lowest
being 46%. The lowest ranked are planning, zoning, and building services and sidewalk
maintenance. Ms. Gibb believes the planning, zoning, and building services number reflects
resident attitudes towards growth and development more than actual interactions. The open-ended
responses do not tend to mention specific issues with Planning and Zoning. The number seems to
be more of an expression of growing pains than attitudes about the City department itself.

Residents were asked about Code Enforcement. Ms. Gibb explained that Code Enforcement is an
area where it is better for residents to elaborate so there is clarity about whether there is too much
or not enough enforcement. There were a lot of comments about sidewalks.

The Neighborhood Improvements data was shared. When it comes to resident attitudes towards
their own neighborhoods, there was a positive sentiment overall about the aesthetic of the
neighborhood and how connected neighborhoods are to the City. The perception of the
connectedness of neighborhoods has increased compared to 2022. The walkability of Holladay
neighborhoods is also highly rated.

Approximately one-third of residents have reported contacting a City office in the last year. When
residents were asked to speak about their experiences, there were positive attitudes overall about
the customer service experience. Most of the discontent residents expressed had to do with the
resolution of their issue not going the way they would have preferred or it not happening as quickly
as they would have preferred. 46% of residents were satisfied with the City's response and 41%
were at least somewhat dissatisfied. There were largely concerns about the resolution.

Ms. Gibb reviewed the City Initiatives and Focus Areas information. 82% of residents feel it is at
least somewhat important that the City prioritize sustainability. Residents would be most
supportive of preserving and planting trees, water conservation, waste management, and reducing
carbon emissions/improving air quality. Transitioning to renewable energy sources is appealing
to 47% and a third of residents are interested in seeing the City encourage more alternative forms
of transportation. As for housing and development in the City, there are consistent themes with
what has been seen in the past. However, the interest in having some form of higher density or

V.      Discussion on Financial Policy – Christian Larsen.
Mayor Dahle reported that Finance Director Christian Larsen, will discuss the City of Holladay
Financial Policies document. There has been a collaborative effort to create the document that is
included in the packet. Mr. Larsen stated that this is a draft document. He is interested in receiving
feedback on what can be improved to better align with the Council vision. The general purpose
of the document is to set parameters and guidelines for prudent fiscal operational practices and
management. The General Fund Reserve policies were reviewed. The State requires
municipalities to maintain an unrestricted General Fund balance between 5% and 35% of General
Fund revenues. The document states that Holladay will maintain a General Fund balance of no
less than 15% of budgeted General Fund revenues. Last year, the City was at 33.6%.

Mr. Larsen discussed the Storm Water Utility Fund. He reported that Holladay will strive to
maintain working capital of no less than 60 days’ worth of annual operating expenses in the Storm
Water Utility Fund. Discussions were had about the Storm Water Utility Fund. Mr. Larsen
reported that it is best to have some cushion between current assets and current liabilities so it is
possible to cover those liabilities in the short term.

The Revenue policy information was shared. Mr. Larsen read the following language: “Holladay
will not use one-time revenues towards ongoing expenditures.” He next read the following:
“Administrative fees will be charged by the General Fund to the Enterprise Funds to recapture
direct and indirect costs which benefit all funds.” The original amount set to be charged was
established when the fund was first created. Ms. Chamness believed the amount was based on a
percentage of the administrative time.

In the Expenditure policy section, it states: “If the midpoint of the City’s salary range is more than
5% below the market for any given position, the grade of that particular position will be adjusted
upward.” There were some Staff discussions about adding additional language to clarify that this
would be subject to budget constraints or subject to the discretion of the City Manager, so there
would be some flexibility. Under the Operating Budget policy, it states: “The City Manager has
authority to reallocate line items within a department budget, but any interdepartmental budget
adjustments must be approved by the City Council.” This is a fairly standard policy for cities.
Mayor Dahle wondered whether there should be a limit in place for what can be reallocated.
Ms. Chamness shared information about the UFA policy that is currently in place. Discussions
were had about the proposed policy. Mr. Larsen reported that auditors review the Financial

The Capital Asset Management section was reviewed. Mr. Larsen highlighted the following
language: “The City of Holladay will develop and maintain a Capital Improvement Plan, which

VI.     Overlay Update – Jared Bunch.
Mayor Dahle asked City Engineer, Jared Bunch, to discuss the overlay update. Mr. Bunch shared
the Summary Report for the proposed mill and overlay streets under County contract. He reported
that there is a map included in the Meeting Materials Packet. The streets on the map are proposed
to be repaved by Salt Lake County under the Fiscal Year 2026 contract. In the previous three
years, additional money has been spent outside of the County contract, which was from the bond
funds. A year ago, there was uncertainty whether there would be money left over for additional
paving projects. It turns out there is no additional funding for paving projects in the spring.

Council Member Gray noted that District 4 has three of the worst roads and asked why those are
not being addressed. Mr. Bunch explained that there has been communication with Salt Lake City
about the water mains in the roads and the leaks that occur. These roads are on the project list, but
the City has been informed that the work will not be funded this year. Repaving is needed, but
after that happens, the water main work might still move forward. It is possible to reach out again
about the necessary water main work so this matter can be further considered. Additional
discussions were had about the roadwork and what is proposed on the map for the Salt Lake County
work. Mayor Dahle noted that during the retreat, a commitment was made to prioritize the roads.
The survey results seem to indicate that the roads are important to the residents of Holladay.
