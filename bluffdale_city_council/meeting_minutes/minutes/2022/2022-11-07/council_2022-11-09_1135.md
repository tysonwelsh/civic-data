         2.1   Discussion Related to the Environmental Study of the 14600 South Railroad
               Crossing Project. (Staff Presenter, Michael Fazio).

City Engineer, Michael Fazio, updated the Council on the railroad crossing and stated that it is a
major project that is moving rapidly. A study was completed of all of the concepts and it was
determined that going above is not an option. It was decided that the only option was to go under.
Doing so would require parallel railroad tracks be built or the use of a jacking method. Staff has
been working closely with a company based in Italy and Spain. They met with the City a few
months ago with Union Pacific, which was significant. They have since completed a Type
Selection Study and informed Union Pacific Railroad regarding how to cross the railroad. Two
methods were compared. Staff was now waiting to meet again with Union Pacific.

Staff also met with Salt Lake City in an effort to redirect the Salt Lake Canal to the Upper Canal
during construction, which they did not support. An Environmental Study was also completed.
Mr. Fazio reported that the City is going beyond what is required. Because the City is the steward
of public money, environmental studies certify that they are following the proper process. An open
house was required as part of the process.

Mr. Fazio reported that the clearance is 17 feet, which is the highway standard plus four feet for
the structure on top that will support the tracks. This was compared to the current 12 feet. Four
lanes were proposed to go under with two trails. There will also be a wall to separate westbound
and eastbound traffic. City Manager, Mark Reid, reported that two boxes are built that are pushed
beneath the tracks. The boxes will run from the center to the edge of the trail.

Dave Kallas asked about the portion of the road that goes under the current trestle. Mr. Fazio
stated that it will be completely closed off. It was originally planned to be a trail but the decision
was ultimately made to close it off. It was noted that they must provide access for Spade. He
remarked that they will have to ensure that they stay within budget. Mayor Hall asked how the
project will be funded. Mr. Fazio stated that the City received $31 million. Dan McKay was
responsible for advocating for it and it was done over two separate years. $15 million was received
the first year and an additional $15 million was requested the next year. If the engineering cannot
be done, $13 million more will be needed for a flyover. Mr. Reid stated that Union Pacific doesn’t
care about the project cost and will support only what is best for them.

Mayor Hall reported that the State is funding the project because it is a regionally significant road.
Mr. Reid stated that the City has committed the $5 million they are getting back from the transfer
of Porter Rockwell Boulevard from the Utah Department of Transportation (“UDOT”). Potentially
the City’s Economic Development Area (“EDA”) money would be eligible as well since it is within
the boundary.

Mr. Fazio stated that currently they are on budget. The environmental document has been
published and is available for review on the City’s website. He reported that on December 6 there
will be a public meeting with formal adoption to take place on January 11. The next step will be
to contact property owners and begin working on possible acquisitions. Staff was also in the
process of preparing documents to hire a General Contractor and finalizing the design. Procedural
issues were discussed. Mr. Fazio stated that the process generally does not last more than one year.
The City has been working closely with a consulting company that works directly with Union
Pacific.

Mayor Hall was enthusiastic about the project and hoped they would be one of the first in the U.S.
to use old and well-used technology from Europe. She thanked Mr. Fazio for his vision.

               Grant Crowell).

Senior City Planner, Jennifer Robison, led the discussion on Detached Accessory Dwelling Units
(“DADU”). She stated that this last year they updated the General Plan and were asked to consider
adding another strategy to Accessory Dwelling Units (“ADU”) in a detached structure. For some
time, the City has allowed ADUs but they have been limited to being within a single-family
dwelling. Location standards are in place that specify that an ADU must be enclosed within a
single-family dwelling. The size has been regulated to be no more than 50% of the total square
footage of the home and only one is allowed per dwelling. One off-street parking stall must also
be provided. The design character must be within the residential character of the neighborhood
and all construction codes must be met. The single-family dwelling and the ADU must be under
single ownership and the owner must occupy one of the units. The use runs with the land as long
as it complies with all requirements. Currently, a permit can be obtained to make ADUs available.

It was determined that the City met all strategies in the last update but they now need to consider
others. In considering DADUs, staff looked at other communities that are now coming into
compliance and allowing them. The intent tonight was to discuss and give staff direction on how
to proceed. Some issues to consider included the following:

   •   Which single-family residential zones should allow DADUs and should they be limited to
       a minimum lot size. Currently, they are allowed in every residential zone that allows
       single-family residential.
   •   An ADU is for one additional family as defined. Is there a need to look at the definition of
       “family”.
   •   Determine whether a permit should be required.
   •   Determine whether a registration or license should be required.
   •   Should one ADU and DADU be allowed per parcel.
   •   Should ADUs and DADUs be owner occupied.
   •   Should utility metering be separated.
   •   Should short-term rentals be allowed. Currently, they are not regulated.
   •   Should require setbacks, heights, and lot coverages be maintained.
   •   Should DADUs be allowed in rear yards and if so, should there be design standards.
   •   Should tiny homes be considered.
   •   Should additional parking be required.
   •   Should building and fire codes be complied with.
   •   Should there be appearance and conditions imposed.
   •   Should an additional kitchen be required.

Mayor Hall stated that the Council Member who brought up the issue is not present and would like
to be part of the discussion. Possible options were discussed.

Council Member Kallas suggested that each of the Council Members give input and come back in
December with a draft ordinance for discussion.

Mrs. Robison acknowledged that this is a major shift in the City’s policy, which she felt was worthy
of coming back to the Council.

Mayor Hall asked what size of lot is required for a DADU and if it is required to be a garage.
Currently, storage facilities are being used as second homes. Mrs. Robison suggested that the
Council first determine if ADUs should be allowed in detached structures. Dave Kallas felt there
were differing opinions but he supported them. With a dwelling unit, he was concerned with
having one utility meter. He suggested there be a standard and that it be required to be owner-
occupied. A front property line setback should be required and DADUs should not be allowed in
setbacks.

Council Member Crockett stated that consideration should be given to the location of decks and
stairs, which could impact the neighbors. Council Member Kallas suggested that the Building
Code be complied with. He asked if only a dwelling unit could be constructed or if it would have
to be accessory to a garage.

Mayor Hall's recalled that Council Member Aston wanted it to be connected to a garage. Council
Member Crockett stressed the importance of design standards. She questioned the difference
between a tiny home and a garage that is converted into a home and the impact on the neighbors.
She recognized that a tiny home might give the appearance of subdividing. Council Member
Kallas clarified that he did not consider an ADU to be a tiny home. He commented that tiny homes
are often portable and built on an axel. An ADU would need to comply with Code with respect to
a dwelling unit, which is on footings and foundations.

Mayor Hall asked for feedback on the advantages and disadvantages. Council Member Crockett
stated that it gives the appearance of having two houses. She saw no difference in terms of impacts
on the neighborhood. Council Member Kallas stated that an ADU should have the same address
as the primary residence. Mrs. Robison stated that it would be on the same parcel and addresses
are assigned when a parcel is created. Dave Kallas commented that if someone were to have a
home on one acre, a mother-in-law apartment in the basement, and a DADU in the back there
would be three rentals on a property. He stated that the question is whether there is a limit on the
number. Mrs. Robison commented that currently only one ADU is allowed per single-family
dwelling or parcel.

Mayor Hall questioned State Code and if the City is required to allow an ADU in the original
dwelling. Mrs. Robison’s understanding was that State Code only allows for one but she offered
to research the matter further.

Council Member Crockett considered parking to be a significant issue. In a situation where there
are three families in one home, parking for several cars will be needed. City Attorney, James
Dunkelberger, reported that State Code allows the City to require a primary dwelling to include
one additional on-site parking space for an Internal Accessory Dwelling Unit (“IADU”). The
statute establishes the rule that a municipality may not establish restrictions or requirements for

small accessory buildings to be closer to the side and rear property lines. The Council had since
asked Staff to review the Ordinance and alleviate the portable and skid requirement, potentially
allow for zero setbacks, reduce the distance from the main dwelling requirement from 10 feet to
zero feet, allow detached accessory buildings in side yards near the front of the house, allow
additional building height, increase lot coverage maximums in Clustered Residential Parry Farms
and Spring View Farms, as well as other administrative changes. The initial draft text was
reviewed with the City Council on September 14, 2022. Two public hearings had been held on
the matter and two Ordinance drafts were reviewed by the Planning Commission.

The recommendation from the Planning Commission was included in the packet. Mrs. Robison
reported that there had been discussions with the Fire Department and some recommendations had
been made from a fire safety perspective. It was requested that the distance between a dwelling
and an accessory building remain at 10 feet. She read the new definition:

    •   Small Accessory Building or Structure:
          o A small accessory building or structure that is 400 square feet or less. Small
               accessory buildings shall have no utilities with the exception of electrical power.

The setback language still states that a small accessory building or structure can be no closer than
two feet from any side or rear property line. A typical detached accessory structure in the Code
needs to be 10 feet from the side and 10 feet from the rear, but one of the smaller structures could
be moved a bit closer to the side or rear setbacks. This was consistent with what was already in
place for portable buildings. The Planning Commission recommended that the maximum wall
height change from 10 feet to 12 feet and the roof line height change from 15 feet to 16 feet. This
would allow for a slightly larger structure. The structure may be located in the rear or side yard
and not less than 10 feet behind the front vertical plane of the dwelling.

Mrs. Robison noted that the proposed amendments also addressed permissible lot coverage to
ensure that everything is consistent in zones with the Clustered Residential Overlay. The language
was updated to 40% rather than 30%. She asked for Council feedback on the proposed text
amendments and Planning Commission recommendations.

language as it was written. There are no design standards for small accessory buildings or
structures, which was of concern. Additionally, the language could create conflicts between
neighbors. She did not believe the proposed language addressed the original intentions expressed
