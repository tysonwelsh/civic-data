       Elected Officials Compensation

Kevin Hicks, City Manager, explained that while the City code currently ties elected officials' pay
to the mid-grade of a Grade 20 position, this structure has resulted in a significant increase this
year—just under 10%—based on benchmarking comparisons with other cities. He
recommended that the pay adjustment structure be adjusted so that elected officials receive the
same annual percentage increase as City staff through the budgetary process.

Mayor Staggs provided background on the 2019 code change that tied the mayoral salary to a
percentage of the City Manager's salary and Councilmembers' salaries to 15% of that amount.
The intent at the time was to recognize the significant time commitment required for the Mayor’s
role and to provide some level of compensation for Councilmembers, particularly given that
participation on various boards often goes unpaid. He expressed appreciation for the previous
decision and the current recommendation to remove the salary tie to Grade 20, instead aligning

adjustments with the annual merit and cost-of-living increases (COLA) provided to City
employees.

Councilmember Haymond expressed support for aligning elected officials’ pay adjustments with
those of City employees, stating that doing so would prevent the issue from becoming a
recurring political debate. He emphasized that keeping compensation at a reasonable level
ensures that individuals pursue elected positions out of a desire to serve the community rather
than financial gain.

Councilmember McCay supported adjusting elected officials’ pay in line with City staff but
expressed concern about the disparity between the Mayor’s salary and the Council’s
compensation. She pointed out that the Mayor currently earns 95% of the average salary for
similar positions, while the Council earns 122%. Potential solutions included reducing Council
pay, freezing it for a year, or allowing only the Mayor’s salary to increase this year while shifting
all future adjustments to match staff increases.

Councilmember Buroker agreed with adjusting elected officials’ pay in line with City staff and
supported holding the Council’s salary for at least a year to allow other cities’ salaries to catch
up. She emphasized the importance of ensuring the policy is clearly defined to avoid repeating
the discussion in the future.

Councilmember McDougal expressed support for aligning elected officials’ pay adjustments with
City staff and emphasized the importance of consistency, particularly since some officials also
receive healthcare benefits. He supported holding the Council’s salary for a year to bring it more
in line while allowing the Mayor’s salary adjustment to proceed.

Mr. Hicks asked each Councilmember to again nod if they were in support of this proposal.
Councilmembers McCay, Buroker, Haymond and McDougal all agreed.

         Traffic Calming and Congestion

Cary Necaise, Public Works Director, discussed the effectiveness of recently installed speed
dips in two subdivisions. A study conducted before and after installation showed minimal impact
on reducing speeds, with only a one-mile-per-hour decrease. The cost of installing each dip was
around $9,000, and correcting them would involve chiseling out the concrete and repaving.

Mr. Necaise discussed traffic access issues on 12600 South, particularly concerns about
residents exiting neighborhoods like Quail Lane near the Holiday gas station. The City’s traffic
committee reviewed the issue, and the City Engineer noted that UDOT is conducting an access
management study for the area, spanning from Bangerter Highway to Herriman. The study aims
to evaluate alternative routes, potential safety improvements, and possible median installations.

One proposed solution involved painting "Keep Clear" zones at key intersections to prevent
vehicles from blocking exits. However, implementing these zones could shift congestion to other
areas, particularly near Bangerter and Mountain View Corridor, potentially disrupting traffic light
timing.

Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, noted that when the Parkside townhome
community was approved, it was recognized that its access onto 12600 South would be
temporary until Dutchman Lane was completed. The development agreement ties the road's
construction to the second phase of the warehouse condos in the area, but that phase has not

Councilmember McDougal acknowledged that the situation has left some residents isolated with

scheduled, though there are limited resources, so requests are added to a waitlist. The
committee responsible for reviewing these issues includes the city engineer, streets manager,
traffic assessor, fire and police employees, the development director, and sometimes a traffic
engineer consultant.

         Wasatch Choice Vision Update

Mr. Hicks said that staff, along with some elected officials, have been working with the Wasatch
Front Regional Council on their 50-year vision plan. One concern highlighted is the placement of
a proposed Trax line, shown by a blue dotted line near Mountain View Village, which staff
believes is in the wrong location. They are advocating for the track to follow a different path
along 13200 South instead of along 13400 South, as planned. Another issue concerns the
potential station near Real Stadium and the need to avoid private property, particularly the
Eagle’s Flight area. Additionally, two proposed parks, one 10-acre and one 16-acre, were not
included in the vision and need to be added.

Councilmember Pierucci noted that while the idea of having a connection to the red line and the
Point is a long-term vision, it's important to include ambitious plans in the vision and work
towards making them a reality, even if they currently seem like a "pipe dream." The goal is to
align funding and stakeholders over time to make these projects feasible.

         Resolution No. 25-20 – Unified Fire Authority Staffing Standards for Station 121 (3
         or 4 Man Service)

         Resolution No. 25-21 – Unified Fire Authority Staffing Standards for Station 124 (3
         or 4 Man Service)

Mayor Staggs provided context for the ongoing budgetary discussion regarding fire service
staffing levels, emphasizing that the conversation is not a critique of Unified Fire Authority (UFA)
but rather an exploration of cost-saving measures. Initially, the City considered reducing staffing
at Stations 121 and 124 from four-person to three-person crews, potentially saving up to $1
million. However, after receiving input from fire officials and community members, he
acknowledged the importance of maintaining at least one four-person crew, given the
specialized needs of hazmat and heavy rescue operations. The potential compromise of
reducing staffing at one station instead of both could still yield approximately $500,000 in
savings while preserving key emergency response capabilities. He also highlighted concerns
about how staffing reductions could impact ambulance availability, noting that dropping to three-
person crews at even one station could lead to the loss of an ambulance.

Mr. Hicks explained that the agenda included two resolutions regarding fire station staffing
levels, with the expectation that a decision would be made on whether to maintain four-person
crews or reduce staffing to three-person crews at one or both stations. The revised budget
scenarios presented showed that if both stations transitioned to three-person crews, the City
would see an estimated $800,000 savings. However, the discussion had shifted toward keeping
Station 121 at four-person staffing while considering reducing Station 124 to three-person
staffing, which would result in savings closer to $400,000. This adjustment could delay the need
for a property tax increase by one to two years.

Councilmember Buroker expressed strong support for maintaining four-person staffing at both

Councilmember McDougal reassured the public that the discussion on fire department staffing

acknowledging the need to address rising costs and find ways to sustain services.

Councilmember Haymond expressed gratitude for the fire and police departments and their

Councilmember Pierucci reaffirmed the importance of these discussions, emphasizing that

         Property Tax Education

Nick Geer, Administrative Services Director, provided an educational overview of property
taxes, explaining that they serve as a stable revenue source for public services such as
education and public safety. He outlined the three key components of property tax: the entity’s
budget, the taxable value of properties, and the tax rate, which is determined by dividing the
budget by the taxable value. Riverton does not have a general property tax but has two taxing
entities—the Riverton Law Enforcement Service Area (RLESA) and the Riverton Fire Service
Area (RFSA)—each receiving about 11% of property tax revenue.

Mr. Geer addressed common misconceptions about property tax increases, explaining that the
certified tax rate is determined annually by the county auditor based on prior-year tax revenue,
adjusted for new growth. He demonstrated how property tax amounts remain stable despite
property value fluctuations and that revenue only increases through new growth or a Truth in
Taxation process. A five-year trend showed Riverton’s property tax budget growing from $5.8
million to $6.5 million due to new growth, while the tax rate declined as property values rose.
