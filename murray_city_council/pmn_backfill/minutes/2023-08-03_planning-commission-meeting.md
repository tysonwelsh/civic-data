TRAINING


Traffic Engineering Training was provided by Ryan Hales of Hales Engineering. He stated that
traffic engineers try to bridge the gap between a city’s master plan and the traffic on the ground.
They look at planning projects and try to determine how many trips a project will generate. They
attempt to project into the future. They review UDOT traffic studies as well. All signal warrants,
crosswalk speed studies, and passing zones anywhere in the state are rewiewed by Hales
Engineering. They look at land use, but also at roads – they try to bring the two of those together
Traffic impact studies are how they go from the general plan to the transportation master plan,
Planning Commission
August 3, 2023
Page 6

and then down to a more detailed level. When they look at different types of traffic impact
studies, they talk about trip generation. They refer to a compendium of documents called the ITE
Trip Generation Manual, and they can look up the land use office space. They can look at some
of those and identify what the trips are going to be from one of those types of places.

Vice Chair Milkavich asked how often the ITE Trip Generation Manual is updated. Mr. Hales
said that it’s updated every five to ten years. When a new business goes in, that isn’t part of the
most recent ITE manual, traffic engineers must do manual counts of trips as part of a traffic
study, sometimes evaluating before and after a new project.

Vice Chair Milkavich asked how is impact measured. Mr. Hales said the manual on Uniform
Traffic Control Devices outlines warrants that traffic has to meet in order to determine level of
service. Computer simulation is used, with light timing and volume, to assist in determining level
of service. The levels of service are graded A through F. Each light can range in levels of
service depending on the time of day. This information helps determine speed limits and how
the timing of lights is set, along with the direction of traffic flow (north-south, east-west).

Vice Chair Milkavich asked if they count the trips differently at intersections with drive-thru
businesses, like Swig or Starbucks, because they are different than McDonalds. Mr. Hales said
they do go to those intersections and physically count the trips.

Vice Chair Milkavich asked how they address traffic at ski resorts. Mr. Hales said in this case the
have cameras set up at drop-off points to count trips. The cameras monitor the parking patterns.

Commissioner Patterson commented that residents are often upset by new mixed-use
development because of the amount of traffic they feel it will generate. She wanted to know how
they can best explain that mixed-use development is going to improve the situation, not make it
worse.

Vice Chair Milkavich commented that there’s a gap in understanding with the public about what
is considered good in terms of traffic.

Commissioner Patterson said that she feels residents don’t care that the new projects will benefit
them, or that the overall level of service will improve. She feels they are just against mixed-use
development.

Vice Chair Milkavich said that residents are often concerned about the number of cars and the
speed of the vehicles.

Commissioner Nay stated that residents say they don’t want a new project because of the speed
of the vehicles, but the reality is that these residents already live there and are the ones
speeding. The new project isn’t going to impact that.

Commissioner Hacker said that part of the problem is that residents don’t believe professionals,
such as Mr. Hales. They disagree with the data presented by experts in the field.

Commissioner Patterson asked how they can help residents understand that when a mixed-use
project is presented to them, their primary concern is the traffic.

Vice Chair Milkavich still feels it’s frustrating to explain to residents that the commission has been
given good data, and a traffic study has been performed, but residents are still quite resistant to
a new project in their area. Mr. Hales suggested showing residents the data from the trip
generator before and after the mixed-use project is developed, to show them that it is probably a
ten percent to thirty percent reduction in traffic.

Commissioner Nay asked Mr. Hales to talk about the number of trips generated by a three-
bedroom apartment versus a three-bedroom home. Mr. Hales stated that single-family home
generates about ten trips a day. A multifamily unit will generate about six to seven trips a day.
This is possibly due to differences in affluence. Those who own homes tend to have more
children (in Utah) and own more vehicles. Those who own homes tend to be more affluent and
make more trips to purchase more items or eat out. In Utah, the average number of daily trips is
more like fourteen trips, instead of ten, which is the national average.

Mr. Hales and the commissioners discussed how townhomes may affect the trip count, but that
will remain to be seen in future. They also discussed how COVID affected trip counts uniquely
during 2020, as well as the increase in the use of Doordash, Grubhub, Uber, Lyft. Mr. Hales
stated that the goal of mixed-use development is to keep residents in a concentrated area,
minimizing the number of trips.

The commissioners discussed how residents still desire to have development of single-family
neighborhoods, with nothing over two stories.

Mr. Hales cited examples of Thanksgiving Point and New York City and the impact that mixed-
used development had on distributing the traffic more consistently throughout the day, instead of
in concentrated pockets, and the benefit this had on the community. He discussed how having
residents around when businesses are closed provides security.

Commissioner Hacker said that, in mixed-use developments, many residents don’t want
vehicles, because they are using public transportation, walking, or biking to work. He did say
that people coming from other parts of the country like California, Washington or Seattle don’t
see the traffic in Utah as a problem. The problem is the perception, because Utah used to have
no traffic, so any traffic seems awful to current residents.

Chair Pehrson acknowledged those comments that address the reason why Utahns perceive
that there is so much traffic, and that it’s something new. He feels that residents target traffic
because they feel it’s something they have control over. He feels the planning commission
needs to do a better job of educating residents.

Chair Pehrson asked Mr. Hales how often his company does a traffic study where they find there
is no bad traffic. Mr. Hales said they do not find A’s in the valley any longer. They now find
intersections that are F’s and they can’t fix. He emphasized to Chair Pehrson that all the
commission can do is build in the areas where they want future growth, even if they hear
complaints from residents. The reality is that Utah is growing and the people have to live
somewhere. The commission has to control the density in certain areas by directing the growth
in more thoughtful ways.

Commissioner Nay said that he wants residents to think about how many vehicles they
themselves have when they raise complaints about the increased traffic, because they are part
of the problem. He pointed out that, even if Murray City didn’t continue to build anything, the
problem would still get worse, because Murray is located in the middle of the valley and all the
municipalities around Murray are growing. All the cities comprise a regional network.

Mr. Smallwood asked if anyone has ever considered changing what’s considered and acceptable
level of service. He asked if the scale should be changed, because a C or a D would be
considered a bad grade in a school setting.

Mr. Hales said it’s best not to equate the grades with school grades. In the traffic setting, C’s
and D’s are acceptable and functional. He also stated if cities tried to build for a level of service,
intersections might be seven lanes wide, instead of a two or three lane road. A seven-lane road
is not practical.

Mr. Hales talked about the use of computer-generated scenarios, in addition to traffic studies.
Simulations will play out different models to manipulate traffic to see what works best. Some
options include modified intersection striping, adding turn pockets, changing the type of signals,
and adding protected green arrows.

Mr. Hales talked about parking issues. Most businesses use a demand-based equation to
determine what they think their needs will be instead of a supply-based equation. Businesses
usually have more parking than is needed. For apartments, the problem is the opposite. They
tend not to have enough parking. Some complexes charge renters to purchase parking spots.
Renters will opt to pay for one spot and then renters park on the street, creating new problems
for the city.

Vice Chair Milkavich commented that it’s difficult to push renters to use less parking when most
families still own two cars.

Mr. Hales said the answer isn’t just taking parking away from residents. He said that one thing
that can be done is to offer rideshare onsite at complexes, as well as access to light rail.
Developing mixed-used neighborhoods around transit, and creating walkable communities, is
what needs to be done. Adding bicycle and pedestrian paths to existing roads will facilitate the
use of transit and reduce the need for more parking.

transportation master plan. If someone moved into an area because they looked at the

with the approval of a new project, would there have any recourse to appeal that decision. If the
city wanted to reduce traffic on State Street between 4500 south and 5300 South, how would

Mr. Hales thanked Mr. Bullen for his questions. He said that traffic studies really do bridge the
gap between transportation master plan and general plan. A general plan is a guiding document
what a city wants to do long term. It's general land use plan that looks at things broadly. It
doesn't drill down to the level of zoning. A traffic study doesn’t drill down in the planning and
zoning side of things. It's too hard to go that deep and look at that micro scale. A city prepares
its general plan, and then creates a transportation master plan. From there, a city can identify
improvements, then those improvements go into the capital facilities plan. Then the capital
facilities plan is taken into typically an IFFP and an IFA impact fee facilities plan and impact fee
analysis. The next step is to develop the cost that is generated from impact fees to make those
improvements that are identified in the transportation master plan. The general plan document is
a guiding document. That's usually the one that he would look at if he were buying a home. He
would also look at the transportation master plan and looks to see what's generally planned in
the areas.

Commissioner Nay said that market forces are constantly evolving and changing and what's in
favor now may fall out of favor later. Market forces are too dynamic to plan for.

Ms. Nixon answered the question about doing a traffic study before changing zones. She says
that planners often suggest to the developers that they do traffic study in conjunction with
general plan and zone change requests, which they are oftentimes very willing to do it, so that
they know for themselves.

Commissioner Patterson commented on Mr. Bullen’s question about paying for traffic studies,
stating that developers are not colluding with traffic engineering companies. She asked how
much traffic studies cost.

Mr. Hales said that because of the development process, it is expensive. Developers take on the
risk and pay for those studies, because sometimes they might not build the project. Rather than
the municipality paying for it, and then having the project go away, it would be a sunk cost, that
would be very expensive for a city to do. As long as all the key elements are there, then it's been
done correctly, you can trust that it's accurate. Traffic and civil engineers stand by a code of
ethics. By way of having registration, that practicing engineer had to pass a certification test,
which includes a section on ethics. Engineers are bond to their ethics. There is no bias towards
developers. Because a developer hired someone doesn't mean that they bias the study. An
engineer’s stamp is their livelihood.

Chair Pehrson commented that a developer is not going to go to a traffic engineer and say they
need them to show that there's no traffic coming out of a proposed development.

Mr. Hales said that what they find when they have citizens hire them for studies, it’s because
they've got a different opinion, they want to make sure that it was done correctly. Mostly it's an
engineering firm wanting a different engineer just to make sure that everything was done
correctly.

Commissioner Patterson said that to answer Mr. Bullen’s question if residents want to pay for
their own traffic study, they certainly can.

Commissioner Nay asked what standing does Mr. Bullen have if he does pay for his own traffic
study to come before the planning commission to make any demands.

Mr. Smallwood said that it depends on the level of review. There are legislative actions and
administrative actions. If it were reviewing a specific project for a design review that already is
zoned for, in the MCCD, you can only impose conditions to mitigate impacts. It wouldn't have a
lot of standing. If it's a legislative action, where you're making a recommendation to the city
council, you can consider that more like a zone change.

Vice Chair Mlikavich asked how much a traffic study would cost. Mr. Hales said it really depends
on the project, but can range from $2,000 to $20,000. A trip generation study can range from
$1,000 to $4,000.

Commissioner Nay asked what the typical lead time is for a study. Mr. Hales said a trip
generation study can be done within about a week to two weeks. A traffic impact study can take
anywhere from two to six weeks. They are thorough reports, ranging from sixty to eighty pages.

Chair Pehrson asked, with all Mr. Hales knowledge and experience, what is the number one
thing decision makers can do to help with traffic. Mr. Hales said the answer is implementation.
Once issues are identified, it’s about moving forward with getting them constructed, whether that
means an impact fee or working with UDOT. It’s about getting the right parties to the table.
Oftentimes, the engineer gives the developer a list of things they need to do to make their project
work. It can include installing a traffic signal, which can cost $400,000 to $450,000. Sometimes
a project warrants something that big. Sometimes it's putting in a right-turn pocket. It's between
the traffic study and getting it onto the pavement is the biggest concern.

Chair Pehrson stated that he thinks there is a problem as a city. The planning commission sees
a traffic study, the engineer makes the recommendation, then the development project is
approved. Next, the planning commission agrees to the engineer’s recommendations, but the
project goes nowhere, or it takes three years to be completed.

Chair Pehrson asked about the technology related to lights with cameras and vehicle counting.
He asked if Mr. Hales envisions a future where traffic lights are operated by AI, instead of with
timers, routing traffic in real-time.

Mr. Hales said it's essentially already doing that. They currently use programs that are ahead of
their time. He said that UDOT is very progressive and leads the country in signal timing
technology. They use large algorithms that look at traffic flows and then make adjustments to
the signal timing. UDOT is doing a really good job.
