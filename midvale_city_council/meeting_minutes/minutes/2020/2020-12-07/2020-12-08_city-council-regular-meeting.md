D. CONSIDER TEMPORARY ZONING ORDINANCE FOR MONUMENT

SIGN STANDARDS IN THE MAIN STREET FORM-BASED CODE ZONE
ORDINANCE NO. 2020-0-20

Matt Dahl said earlier this year, City Council members expressed interest in having
electronic signage on the Midvale City Hall site for the purposes of providing information
to the public on a variety of issues. However, the primary and pressing need was to
provide information regarding the COVID-19 pandemic. Staff has pursued the design and
procurement of an LED sign to achieve the City’s communication goals. Through that
process, staff determined that in order for the sign to have the proper pedestal, display
area, and branding that it would narrowly exceed the limitations on height and surface
area established in the Main Street Form-Based Code (FBC).

Staff has also pursued a plan to fund the sign and its related infrastructure with the

Federal CARES Act resources received by the City. Projects funded through the CARES

Act must be completed by December 31, 2020. Given the immediate need to keep our

community informed about the current health crisis, the limited amount of time that

funding is available, and the duration of the normal process to amend land use
regulations, City staff seeking a temporary zoning ordinance that would allow for the
installation of the proposed sign.
In cases where there is a compelling, countervailing public interest, both the Midvale City

Municipal Code and Utah Code allow the City to adopt a temporary zoning ordinance,

effective for no longer than 6 months, without going through the typical ordinance

amendment process.

Staff has prepared a temporary ordinance that would establish new conditions for

monument signs and that would allow for the construction of the proposed LED sign. The

temporary ordinance would include the following:

Buildings on a lot with a width of greater than six hundred feet, measured across a primary

frontage, may have signage with the following parameters along such frontage:

e Up to 9’ tall and a maximum area of 75 SF per sign face.

e Up to two monument signs on one frontage.

e Signs shall be at least three hundred feet apart.

e Monument signs may not be pole mounted.

e Manually changeable text is not permitted for monument signs.

e May serve multiple purposes, such as seating.

e If placed closer than five feet from the front and corner side property lines, signs
must meet clear view requirements.

e Double sided signs are permitted.

Placing this temporary ordinance into effect would allow staff to install the proposed LED
sign using CARES Act funding and meet the need to provide another means of :
communicating with the public during the current public health emergency. The temporary
timeframe proposed in the ordinance could also allow for the monument sign
requirements to be reviewed and changed, if necessary, through the typical ordinance
amendment process.

Lisa Garner said the banner program with a company named CGI was brought to the City

Council. The Council made a favorable recommendation with moving forward with this

program. As the Legal Department reviewed CGl’s contract to install banners on city-

owned light poles, several legal considerations of this program were raised. Because

these legal considerations may have significant impacts on the use of City-owned

property and may expose the City to greater liability, they are being brought before the

Council to be considered before moving forward with the banner program.

The City has historically not allowed other entities to post banners on its light poles. As a

result, the City has maintained complete discretion and control of the speech on its light

poles. However, by allowing a commercial entity like CGI to place banners on city-owned

light poles, it will create a public speech forum that didn’t previously exist. The City will

lose its complete discretion and control of the speech on its light poles.

Before creating this forum, the City needs to contemplate the consequences that may

arise due to the placement of the banners on city-owned light poles. These consequences

could include First Amendment challenges, political backlash, and overall city liability.

This discussion is designed to highlight the challenges, present certain mitigating factors,

and suggest potential solutions to limit City liability so that the Council can make an

informed decision.
