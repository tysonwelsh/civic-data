34   Rob Jolley, RRJ Consulting, provided an overview of the recent legislative session, noting that a
35   record-setting 959 bills were introduced, with 582 passing—second most ever. Specific to
36   Riverton, he highlighted House Bill 502, sponsored by Representative Jordan Teuscher, which
37   included two major transportation appropriations:
38
39   $2.5 million for improvements to 2700 West between Bangerter Highway and 13400 South, and
40
41   Approximately $2.75 million for UDOT to assume jurisdiction over 12600 South, potentially
42   relieving the City of the need to contribute additional funding.
43
44   He also cautioned the City about increasing scrutiny and resistance from Salt Lake County and
45   school districts regarding Community Reinvestment Areas (CRAs) and tax increment financing.
46   Regarding housing, he explained that despite many proposals being discussed, few passed
47   during this session due to legislative leadership opting to observe the impact of previous
48   housing reforms.

43             Ordinance No. 25-12 – Amending the Commercial Architectural Standards in
44             Sections 18.65.060, 18.70.060, 18.75.050,, 18.80.060, 18.85.040, 18.100.060, and
45             18.215.030 of the Riverton City Code
46
47   Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, presented proposed updates to the City’s
48   commercial architectural standards, focusing specifically on the exterior appearance of
49   buildings. These changes have been discussed in prior Council work sessions and reviewed in
50   detail by the Planning Commission. The current standards, which rely on a rigid checklist of
51   required design elements, have become too inflexible as Riverton has grown and developed
     Page | 5 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     April 15, 2025



 1   more diverse commercial projects. Some proposed buildings that fit well within their
 2   surroundings couldn’t meet the strict checklist, while others met only the minimum requirements
 3   without delivering quality or cohesive architecture. Outdated references, such as “turn-of-the-
 4   century” design themes, and a lack of provisions for redevelopment further highlighted the need
 5   for revision.
 6
 7   The proposed amendments aim to modernize and standardize design expectations across all
 8   commercial zones while allowing more flexibility for thoughtful, context-sensitive architecture.
 9   The updates also address outdated language and improve alignment with current city and state
10   codes. Although the redlined code in the packet appeared extensive, Mr. Lethbridge explained
11   that the changes are largely consistent across different sections of the code, making the overall
12   revisions less dramatic than they may seem at first glance.
13
14   Councilmember Haymond raised a concern regarding whether the updated commercial design
15   standards would require costly architectural treatments on the rear of buildings—even when
16   those sides aren’t visible from public roads. In response, Mr. Lethbridge clarified that the
17   updated code includes a requirement for “four-sided architecture,” but that does not mean all
18   sides must receive equal treatment. Instead, it ensures that no side is completely neglected,
19   especially in situations where rear façades face residential areas. There is built-in flexibility,
20   allowing the Planning Commission to use discretion based on context. He emphasized that in
21   past applications, they have not required rear sides to match front elevations in quality,
22   especially when visibility is minimal, but they have expected a basic level of architectural
23   consideration.
24
25   Councilmember Buroker shared that the volume and complexity of the proposed architectural
26   code updates caused confusion and anxiety, making it difficult to understand their impact. After
27   reviewing a Planning Commission meeting, she felt that discussions—such as the percentage of
28   glass required—lacked clear justification, while conversations about newer metal materials were
29   more helpful. She appreciated that the ordinance requires commercial buildings to include at
30   least two design elements that tie into surrounding properties, offering flexibility without losing
31   visual cohesion.
32
33   Councilmember McCay raised concerns about the strength of language requiring “equal
34   architectural design consideration” for all sides of a building, suggesting it be softened to allow
35   flexibility, particularly for less visible areas like building rears. Emphasizing the need for Riverton
36   to remain business-friendly, she voiced concerns that requiring rear façades to match front
37   façades could impose unnecessary costs. She also questioned restrictions like the 50% glass
38   cap and the prohibition of visible mechanical equipment, arguing that features like glass can
39   enhance aesthetics and natural light, and equipment at the back of a building may not always
40   need screening.
41
42   Councilmember Pierucci expressed support for simplifying and modernizing the City’s
43   architectural standards, emphasizing flexibility and clarity in the process. While he didn’t have
44   strong objections to elements like the percentage of glass allowed, he acknowledged that others
45   might have more invested opinions. Ultimately, he supported the ordinance as a step in the right
46   direction and was open to either approving it or having staff refine it further and return with
47   revisions in a few weeks.

 9             Ordinance No. 25-13 – Text Amendment amending Sections 18.45.050; 18.50.050;
10             18.53.050; 18.55.050; 18.57.050 and 18.60.050 of the Riverton City Code relating
11             minimum parcel size for multi-family developments. Amendments proposed by
12             Riverton City
13
14   Mr. Lethbridge clarified that the proposed amendment does not automatically rezone property
15   once a minimum acreage threshold is met. Instead, it sets a baseline requirement for when a
16   rezoning application can be submitted. Any rezone would still be subject to the General Plan
17   and site-specific context.
18
19   Councilmember Pierucci asked for clarification regarding how much undeveloped land in the
20   City is currently zoned for multi-family use, specifically RM-8. Mr. Lethbridge confirmed the
21   General Plan includes areas designated for future multi-family development, all existing multi-
22   family zoning has occurred in response to specific proposed projects, and no such zoned land
23   remains undeveloped at this time.
24
25   Councilmember McDougal asked how the proposed code changes would affect projects that
26   have already received zoning approval but have not yet been developed. Mr. Lethbridge
27   confirmed that if a project has been approved but remains undeveloped, the property owner
28   could return and submit a new application under the updated code standards.
29
30   Councilmember McDougal also raised concerns about how Riverton’s proposed reduction in
31   minimum acreage requirements for multi-family development compares with similar cities and
32   what message it sends. Mr. Lethbridge responded that approaches vary widely: some cities, like
33   Herriman, have eliminated minimums to encourage growth, while others prefer larger projects to
34   ensure sustainability with infrastructure and open space. The proposed changes—such as
35   allowing 0.8-acre projects along major roads—signal a philosophical shift toward
36   accommodating smaller developments, which could result in more fragmented projects lining
37   those corridors.
38
39   Councilmember Buroker expressed concern about the perceived urgency behind proposed
40   reductions in minimum acreage requirements for multi-family developments. She questioned the
41   need to significantly lower thresholds—such as from five acres to two, or two acres to 0.8—
42   especially given constraints like UDOT access and the risk of creating a piecemeal appearance
43   along major roads. She indicated support for a more measured approach, suggesting a
44   reduction from five to three acres for RM-8 zoning as a reasonable compromise and proposed
45   holding off on any further changes until additional discussion could take place.
46
47   Councilmember Pierucci emphasized that the proposed changes to minimum acreage
48   requirements should be viewed as a tool to provide flexibility rather than a mandate for
49   increased small-scale development. He argued that the changes would not automatically lead to
50   piecemeal development but would simply allow the Council discretion to evaluate whether
51   smaller projects make sense on a case-by-case basis.

16             Ordinance No. 25-14 – Amending Riverton City Code Section 18.135.200,
17             Temporary Commercial Uses, Addressing Temporary Commercial Uses on
18             Residential Property, Amendments Proposed by Riverton City
19
20   Mr. Lethbridge presented a proposed amendment to allow temporary commercial uses—such
21   as fireworks stands and Christmas tree lots—on vacant, undeveloped residential parcels with
22   direct access to major roads, while continuing to prohibit them on residential lots with homes or
23   on interior streets. These uses would be limited to seasonal, holiday-related sales and still
24   require a conditional use permit (CUP), which opens the process to public input but obligates
25   approval if conditions are met. The Planning Commission supported the draft with the CUP
26   requirement.
27
28   Councilmember Haymond expressed concern about overregulating private property, particularly
29   in cases where individuals want to use their land for small-scale, temporary sales like fruit
30   stands. Mr. Lethbridge clarified that the current draft only applies to undeveloped residential
31   properties along major roads and is limited to holiday-related sales like Christmas trees and
32   fireworks. However, he noted the language is flexible and could be amended to allow broader
33   uses—such as fruit or shaved ice stands—if the Council desires, though those types of uses
34   have been rare and are typically set up in commercial parking lots.
35
36   Mayor Staggs discussed whether it's worth requiring a $275 fee and a 14-day notice period for a
37   Conditional Use Permit (CUP) when such uses are often automatically approved if they meet
38   the criteria. He suggested removing the CUP requirement entirely, instead allowing the use by
39   right if parameters are met.
40
41   Ryan Carter, City Attorney, pointed out that a Conditional Use Permit (CUP) provides a quick
42   way to address issues, as it allows the City to remove the use if the applicant violates terms or
43   conditions. While other remedies exist, he noted that CUP revocation is faster and more direct.
44   He recommended that instead of approving the ordinance as it stands, staff should bring it back
45   for further review and adjustments, ensuring consistency and addressing potential unintended
46   consequences before moving forward.
47
