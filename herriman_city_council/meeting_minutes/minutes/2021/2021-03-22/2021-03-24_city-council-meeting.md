    2.1. Discussion of the proposed amendment to the Engineering Standards - Jonathan Bowers,
         City Engineer
City Engineer Jonathan Bowers recalled he had distributed the updated Engineering Standards to the Council
previously, and they had requested additional time to review the document as it was fairly lengthy. He explained
he would discuss the way the standards were structured in the code, as well as the policies. He added there was
an additional item of discussion about roadway cross section requirements, and apologized the Council had not
had a chance yet to review the item, and encouraged them to ask questions throughout the presentation.

City Engineer Bowers said in section 9-2-2, the engineering standards were adopted by code. He said those
adoptions included a guide of what happened in a public right-of-way, and also explained how to adopt
APWA. He distributed a document which listed the items specifically discussed by the Mayor and Council
Members in the last meeting, showed which items he had made an adjustment to, and which items he proposed
to maintain.

City Engineer Bowers began with item two, which was in regard to contaminated soil. He recalled the topic of
discussion in the last meeting had been if a plat of land should show where contaminated soil was on the land
or not, and said new subdivisions were required to mitigate contaminated soil, and so plats for a subdivision
should not have any contaminated soil on it in the first place. He recommended the plat should show any
contaminated soil in areas where no action was required. Mayor David Watts asked if mitigation meant the
removal of the contaminated soil or if removal just meant they would put a cap on it, and City Engineer Bowers
explained the answer depended on the land use. Mayor Watts expressed concern if contaminated soil was not
fully removed, the root systems of plants and trees could extend into contaminated soil, and he was in support
of the recommendation.

City Engineer Bowers spoke about item number four, which stated this may be required to add potential traffic
increases due to other anticipated developments. He proposed they include the words “may” and “shall” as it
depended on if there was a development needing to be included or not.

City Engineer Bowers discussed item number seven, which was about payment design criteria. He explained it
was 20 years as a standard practice, but he thought it would be prudent to state this was the minimum
requirement, as he had seen some consultants propose something above 20 years.

City Engineer Bowers moved on to item number nine, and stated certain areas identified in EPA records showed
high arsenic and lead levels, and those areas were required to provide an environmental assessment area. He
said the City Code and procurement for contaminated soils had a more definitive language in the contaminated
area, and the section could be modified to mirror the language. He noted the area of anticipated contamination
had been approved by the EPA, and was held and maintained by their GIS mapping system. He wanted to make
sure they matched the procedures and policies exactly with what was in the manual about contaminated soil
procedures.

City Engineer Bowers said they would add soil remediation plans should include impacts to the surrounding
areas to item number ten.

City Engineer Bowers said his recommendation for item number 11 was to make no changes. He explained the
scope of the traffic committee was generally limited to existing traffic conditions and deficiencies, and did not
focus on proposed or new construction. While the traffic committee may consult with the City Engineer, the
committee would not be closely involved with new infrastructure.

City Engineer Bowers proposed they remove item number 13, which banned the use of protection strips. He
said there were some cases in which protection strips were appropriate, and felt it would be included in the City
Code. He said the intent of the ban was to mitigate trespassing issues if a plot of land was broken into
subdivisions. Mayor Watts commented it seemed similar to corridor preservation, and City Engineer Bowers
agreed, and said this was why he proposed it be removed from the standards. Councilmember Clint Smith
agreed it should be removed, as it could cause issues with emergency access.

City Engineer Bowers said item number 14 should have a change in wording, which stated residential driveways
should not access arterial and major collectors. He explained while minor collectors carried lower volume, and
it could significantly harm property owners if it was strictly prohibited. For minor collectors, there was less
inherent risk, and so he thought it would be better to take those on a case by case basis. He recommended a
change to the wording to say minor collectors were not generally allowed, unless approved by the City
Engineer. Mayor Watts disagreed, and pointed out there was an area in Herriman in which there was
disagreement as to if a road should be residential or for minor collectors. City Engineer Bowers listed some
other areas in Herriman which had minor collectors, and said there were other areas in the town in which people
could not otherwise develop, and would only have access off that particular road. He suggested they make it
the prerogative of City staff as to if a minor collector should be allowed in a given area, and Mayor Watts said
that he would prefer for the Planning Commission or the City Council to be the body which would make the
decision to allow an exception, rather than staff.

It was also noted the Planning Commission was generally good at pointing out when lots could not face a certain
road or direction. Specifically, it was pointed out that there was one lot in the Valhalla subdivision which would
March 24, 2021 – City Council Minutes                                                Page 4 of 24

it brought before the Planning Commission. It was agreed it should be under the jurisdiction of the Planning
Commission to determine if an exception was warranted or not. City Engineer Bowers commented he would
not push back if the consensus was it should be added to the code.

Councilmember Ohrn thought this was similar to the flag ordinance, and said there were some property owners

also said they had discussed deviation to the standard application, which he said was a new procedure, which

City Engineer Bowers spoke about a conversation he had held with the Fire Marshall in regard to the new
standards, and reported the Fire Marshall was concerned about roadway widths. He explained the UFA
requirements stated anything with less than 20 feet of drivable width was not allowed, and anything between 20
and 26 drivable feet did not allow fire hydrants, and had restricted parking. Anything between 26 and 32 drivable
feet allowed for fire hydrants, but still had restricted parking. Beyond 32 feet, both fire hydrants and unrestricted
parking were both allowed. City Engineer Bowers said the Fire Marshall had expressed concern with section
4.05.05 of the Engineering Standards, which stated private roadways should be designed to the same standard
as public roadways, and another section which stated for emergency vehicle purposes, all residential units should
be accessible and addressed of a roadway in a way which conformed with the City road cross-section. Therefore,
all residential units would have to be addressed off of a road that was at least 32 feet. The Fire Marshall thought
Herriman did not have to conform to the rule, and felt the requirement that all units be addressed off of a road
that was 32 feet or more would be onerous to developers. City Engineer Bowers proposed private roads have
a minimum drivable width of 26 feet rather than 32, and would have to conform to all other UFA
requirements. As well, all residential units would have to be addressed off of a road which conformed to the
requirement and all other UFA standards. Councilmember Ohrn clarified this would not impact the rules about
parking, and City Engineer Bowers confirmed.

Mayor Watts asked the width of a standard fire truck, and Councilmember Smith estimated them to be about
eight or nine feet, not counting the rig. Councilmember Smith asked City Engineer Bowers if there was any

Mayor Watts said his concern was about the ease of access for emergency vehicles, which is why he thought it

     2.2. Discussion of Fire Station #103 facility- Monte Johnson, Operations Director, Anthony
          Widdison, UFA Captain & Dominic Burchett, UFA Assistant Fire Chief
Operations Director Monte Johnson explained Herriman City had recently left the UFSA, and they now owned
Herriman Fire Station 103. He stated the building had been constructed in 1978, and two additions had been
made since then to the structure. He showed some photographs of the common living area and the kitchen
from several angles. He pointed out there were cabinets for storage, as the building was very small. He also
showed the sleeping quarters for the fire fighters. He recalled in the early 1990’s, there had only been volunteer
firefighters stationed in the building, and after a fire in Rose Creek in which two children had perished, the
County had been prompted to re-evaluate the services and add a full-time crew to the fire station.

Director Johnson explained they had recently done an inspection of the building, and noted they had had some
issues with the building as it was not built for today’s standard. UFA Division Chief Anthony Widdison spoke
about the living situation in the building, and said both himself and UFA Assistant Fire Chief Dominic Burchett
had spent many years there. He reiterated the station had not been built to meet the needs of a full-time crew,
and several remodels had been done either by the crews or by Capital repairs over time. He said bedrooms had
needed to be added, and the amount of personnel had been increased. He noted some of the bedrooms had
not even had windows in the original design, which had since been changed. He said the crews had made the
station functional and comfortable for 48 hour shifts, but many of these things were “band-aid” solutions, and
he spoke about how small and cramped some of the bedrooms and hallways were in the building. He explained
they had focused on the improvement of the look of the building, but there were some deeper issues they were
not able to resolve.

UFA Assistant Fire Chief Dominic Burchett said the station had been designated to be replaced about five years
ago, and so with this in mind they had not put a lot of money into updates to it as they knew it would ultimately
be replaced. He reported their logistics team had found a number of things that would have to be updated in
order to make it a functional and comfortable living space.

Director Johnson showed some photographs of the building, and went over the list of items that needed to be
repaired. He said the air ducts and HVAC system had issues that needed to be repaired, since at the time it was
built, construction voids had been used for ventilation and so there was dust, dirt, and other contaminants in
the vents, that the fire fighters then breathed in. He reported there was also a lack of insulation, which meant
the furnace had to be on all the time. As well, the windows leaked and did not have screens, and the kitchen
was in a state of disrepair, with many appliances needing to be replaced. He noted building codes were very
different now than what they had been when the building was constructed. He stated the carpet was worn down,

    2.3. Discussion pertaining to the Emergency Declaration relating to signage, delinquent utility
         accounts, and business licensing - Chase Andrizzi, City Attorney
City Attorney Chase Andrizzi stated on February 25, the Council had requested legislative items related to the
emergency declaration be brought back before them for review. For background, he said in March of 2020, the
City Manager had declared a local state of emergency, which by statute was good for 30 days, although it had
been ratified by the City Council in April, which made it viable for an indefinite amount of time. The City still
operated under a state of emergency, and would continue to do so until the City Council revoked it. During the
state of emergency, the Council had passed two resolutions on May 13th, 2020. The first had suspended all
business license renewal fees until April 30, 2021, and the second one pertained to the water fees, in which the
reconnection fee had been lowered and the delinquent fee had been indefinitely suspended. City Attorney Chase
Andrizzi asked for direction from the Council in regard to those resolutions.

Mayor Watts inquired as to how many people had not paid their water fees, and it was replied that it was typically
about 30 to 70 people, and at the beginning of that month, there were about 36 people who had not yet paid. City
Attorney Andrizzi said they had been concerned people would not pay, but the suspension of delinquent fees
had not had a large impact.

Councilmember Smith clarified there was no deadline for the water fee waiver. He also asked if that could
prohibit them from any Care Act Funding if they chose to remove the suspension. City Attorney Andrizzi
confirmed, and thought it would be best practice to mirror what the state did. Councilmember Smith was glad
to see most people still paid their water fees on time, and agreed they should follow what the state did. He did
think they should set a termination date for the water delinquency fees, but felt there were many businesses in
Herriman that had not weathered the impacts of COVID-19 very well, and so was in favor of an extension of
the business license fee waiver until the end of the fiscal year. He thought it was not a large financial impact to
the City of fees that were not collected, and felt that they should support their businesses. Assistant City Manager
Tami Moody commented if they wanted to continue the suspension of fee collection, they should extend it to
the end of the entire year, not the fiscal year, and businesses renewed at different times. City Attorney Andrizzi
said the resolution could be drafted in a way that allowed the waiver of up to two years, and Mayor Watts said
he would support. Mayor Watts also agreed that they should mirror the State, and asked City staff to bring a
resolution before the Council once the State lifted the suspensions. He also felt it was time to reinstate the
collection of water delinquency fees beginning on April 30th. Councilmember Ohrn commented the mask
mandate would end on April 10th, although the County might implement their own mask mandate. She also
requested they look into what the financial impact to the City would be if they continued to waive business
license renewal fees.

City Attorney Andrizzi summarized business could receive a total of two waivers, to be fair to businesses that
had renewed last year before the waiver went into effect. He said he would draft a resolution and bring it before
the Council for approval. Assistant City Manager Tami Moody reported as of February, the business license fee
suspension had been $32,400 for regular businesses, and home occupations were $6,000.

    2.4. Discussion pertaining to the 2021 municipal election options - Jackie Nostrom, City Recorder
City Recorder Jackie Nostrom offered different options for the City to consider related to the 2021 municipal
election. She said Utah was now a vote-by-mail state, so it would be an option to have a declaration of candidacy
would be in June, a primary election in August, and then a general election would be held in November and
ballots would be mailed to each voter. She said that currently, there were 26,922 active voters in Herriman
City. She said the fees assessed from Salt Lake County were set based on this number, and the cost would be
$51,414. Herriman had the option to participate in the pilot program for ranked-choice-voting, and City
Recorder Nostrom reported she had spoken with the Vineyard Recorder who had adopted ranked-choice-
voting, and said they were in support of ranked-choice, although they did not have districts like Herriman, and
so voting would be slightly different. She shared an informational video which had been produced and
distributed in Vineyard, to explain the process and how ranked choice voting worked. The video overviewed
the winning candidate would have to obtain more than 50-percent of the votes to win, and also explained how
the process worked if the election was for more than one seat. She explained utilizing ranked choice voting
would eliminate the necessity to hold a primary, and only the ten candidates were allowed to be ranked according
to state law. Councilmember Smith clarified that meant no more than ten candidates for a certain seat, which
Recorder Nostrom confirmed. She said in ranked choice voting, the declaration of candidacy would not
March 24, 2021 – City Council Minutes                                                   Page 11 of 24
commence until August, which would shift their planned time frame. She asked the Council if they would be
interested in the pilot program for ranked choice voting, or if they wished to do traditional vote-by-mail for the
election, and asked if there were any questions.

Councilmember Shields said he was strongly opposed to ranked choice voting in Herriman City. He felt there
was too much opportunity for candidates to be disenfranchised, and it might force voters to pick a candidate
they opposed. Mayor Watts asked if he had to vote for all the candidates, and City Recorder Nostrom replied
in the negative. She did not see there would be a major difference between the results of a traditional vote and
the results of a ranked choice vote. She said the reports had indicated there would be an influx of people who
felt they were voting for somebody, versus voting against somebody, and so they felt the experience was
positive. She added there would be an educational component for residents, and there was funding

annexed into the City. City Attorney Chase Andrizzi said that was correct, and elaborated that any annexation
applications that were completed before October 31st could be annexed into the City on January 1st, and
applications were filed after October 31st could be annexed in July of the next year.

City Attorney Andrizzi said the first step in the process was for the owner of the unincorporated area to send
the letter of intent to petition to be annexed to the City Council, as well as the County and any other affected
entities. Once had been received, the landowner had 20 days to send out a notice of his intent to all property
owners near the unincorporated land, as well as file the official petition of the intent annex. After this process,
the City could choose to accept or deny the petition, and he clarified that acceptance of the petition did mean
that they accepted the land into the City. He said the City had 14 days to accept or deny the petition, and if no
action was taken then it would be accepted by default. He said after the time of the acceptance, the City then
had 30 days to certify the petition, and he explained this process would determine the property owners, the value
of the land, and if any nearby landowners would be impacted. Once the City was done with this process, they
were obligated to notify the County as well as the public, and there then would be a 30 day protest period. He
elaborated statute limited the people that were qualified to file a protest, and only people who lived within a half
mile of the proposed annexation area and institutions that were directly impacted who could file a protest. He
added protests were filed with the County Boundary Commission, and so it was not something the City dealt
March 24, 2021 – City Council Minutes                                                  Page 22 of 24
with directly, although they were notified. If a protest was filed, the City could choose to deny the petition, or
they could wait for the County Boundary Commission to conclude, which he said could take over 200 days and
was very involved.

City Attorney Andrizzi then explained that if no protests were filed, the City then had a notice requirement to
inform residents of a public hearing. He said at the hearing, they would gather feedback from residents, and at
the end of the meeting it would be the Council’s prerogative to approve or deny the petition. They could also
take no action, which essentially killed the petition. If the petition was accepted, the City had 60 days to notify
the lieutenant governor, who would review the documents and ensure that everything had been done
correctly. The lieutenant governor would then certify the annexation, and would either go into effect in January
or July 1st. He summarized in all, it took about 209 days to complete the legislative approvals, if all the days
were used for each step of the process and no protests were filed. He said most developers who wanted to come
into the City usually had their documents prepared so that things could get filed in a timelier manner, which
made the overall process much quicker.

Councilmember Smith asked what the shortest amount of time an annexation could be completed in. City
Attorney Andrizzi calculated if everything was filed within two days after the petition was received, everything
was accepted by the Council within a reasonable time frame, and they abided by their noticing requirements, an
annexation application could probably be completed within 68 days. He commented was the shortest time frame
possible, but it was more realistic for the process to take closer to three to four months, and said if there were
protests filed it made the overall process much longer. City Attorney Andrizzi also observed the times in which
the City Council could deny the petition, and explained they could deny the letter of intent to file a petition, deny
it if there were any protests filed, or after the public hearing.

Councilmember Shields thought it was important to note that while this conversation applied most relevantly to
Olympia Hills, it applied to any unincorporated areas that wanted to move into Herriman City. He spoke about
the updates to the Bonneville study, as well as Finance Director Alan Rae’s previous report on the financial
impact of the annexation of Olympia Hills, and said things such as those were important to consider when they
evaluated the feasibility of an annexation.

Councilmember Ohrn felt there were more discussions to be had, and said she was always willing to talk about
options, but did not want this conversation to derail them from other infrastructure projects that needed to take
place within their own City boundaries.

Councilmember Smith noted now as they started to see progress on Olympia Hills, it was important to foster a
good dialogue with the land developer there, and important for them to understand what the potential timeline
for annexation would look like. He said the Council’s job was to find ways that will best help Herriman to deal
with the impacts that Olympia Hills will cause for them in the future, and expressed appreciation to City Attorney
Andrizzi.

Councilmember Shields agreed that it was paramount for them to maintain a relationship with the developer of
Olympia Hills, and said it was always better to work as a team rather than as rivals. He clarified at that time,
