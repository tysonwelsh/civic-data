   2.5. Discussion regarding parks fertilization services – Anthony Teuscher, Deputy
        Director of Parks, Recreation and Events

                Councilmember Shields recused himself from the discussion.

Deputy Director of Parks, Recreation and Events Anthony Teuscher proposed transitioning
the fertilization services for city parks from a contracted arrangement to an internal
operation. He explained the City was responsible for the upkeep of approximately 180 acres
of well-maintained grass areas. Since 2018, the expenses associated with external contractors
have escalated by 67%, while the maintained acreage only increased by 15%. Deputy Director
Teuscher highlighted several advantages of managing the service internally, including
enhanced control over the products utilized and the rates of application, improved quality,
and flexibility in scheduling applications, potential cost reductions, and the addition of a full-
time employee who could support other park duties outside the fertilization schedule.

Deputy Director Teuscher explained that the service required four applications throughout
the year: early spring, late spring, mid-summer, and late fall. Staff oversite and quality
assurance efforts has increased substantially, which Deputy Director Teuscher found
frustrating. This internal transition would increase control over the product quality and
ensure proper application rates, potentially leading to cost savings. With a full-time employee,
estimated costs would remain at about $144,000 annually, including $88,000 salary/burden
January 22, 2025 City Council Meeting Minutes                                        Page 3 of 16


and $55,000 for materials. This change would effectively provide extra labor for various park
duties, owing to the flexibility of the new position being partially seasonal.

Deputy Director Teuscher noted the City already owned the necessary equipment for
operation, minimizing upfront investments. There was discussion about whether one
employee could manage the task and if there were potential unforeseen maintenance costs.
Staff felt this proposal would lead to better quality assurance and potential long-term
financial savings due to more efficient resource utilization. The Council queried about
maintenance expenses for equipment, personnel requirements, and comparisons with other
municipalities' models. Deputy Director Teuscher noted that with existing equipment,
maintenance costs would likely be minimal, primarily involving fuel, oil, and occasional small
repairs like pump diaphragms. He assured the Council that other cities facing similar
challenges also observed increased costs with contracted services, further supporting the
proposal.

Councilmember Jared Henderson expressed concern over increasing staff which might
compound future costs yet recognized the need to explore this internal transition due to
rising contractor costs. Councilmember Sherrie Ohrn pointed out additional savings could be
realized from reduced oversight necessities, potentially offsetting some costs associated with
hiring a new full-time employee.

Mayor Palmer emphasized that this change could effectively provide an extra six months of
labor for miscellaneous park duties at a net-zero cost shift, highlighting the multifaceted
benefits of bringing the fertilization services in house. Mr. Teuscher detailed that if this
initiative were approved, he intended to bring an amendment to a future meeting. This
amendment would formalize the creation of a new position, enabling the Parks Department
to utilize the new full-time employee across different tasks outside of the primary fertilization
schedule. By employing someone for about six months focused solely on fertilization and
weed spraying, the City could utilize this employee for other necessary park maintenance
activities during the rest of the year.

Assistant City Manager Thomas also remarked on potential cost savings from reduced
oversight, noting how current contracted services often require excessive monitoring to
ensure proper application and coverage, which has been a cause of frustration for city staff.
Ensuring effective and accurate fertilization would not only improve park aesthetics and
quality but also streamline operations across the Parks Department. Councilmember Teddy
Hodges asked if there was a specific candidate for the position. Deputy Director Teuscher
responded in the negative; however, would ideally like to have the candidate licensed to apply
pesticides. The Council expressed support moving forward with the proposal.
Councilmember Ohrn asked for a report back from staff in the future.

    2.1. Herriman Main Street & Herriman Rose Blvd Community Update – Bryce Terry,
         City Engineer
City Engineer Bryce Terry provided an update on community feedback and potential
solutions for addressing pedestrian safety concerns at the intersection of Herriman Main
Street and Herriman Rose Boulevard. He noted a community meeting was held in December
to gather input from residents, focusing on major concerns such as traffic speeds, volumes,
road width, and overall walkability. He offered short-term recommendations including adding
speed feedback signs and enhancing crosswalk striping and lighting.

City Engineer Terry explained that during the community meeting, it became evident that
residents were also worried about the general conditions along Herriman Main Street. They
noted that existing speeds, due to the arterial nature of Main Street, contributed to the
perceived lack of safety. City Engineer Terry explained that many of the suggestions gathered
during the meeting involved exploring comprehensive traffic calming measures. These
included ideas like lowering the speed limit, introducing speed humps, and possibly installing
raised intersections to help control vehicle speeds more effectively.

The Council debated various potential solutions, considering both the safety and traffic flow
implications. Mayor Palmer highlighted the importance of balancing these improvements with
existing infrastructure constraints and budget limitations. He pointed out the complexities of
trying to enhance walkability while still accommodating the primary function of the road as
a major thoroughfare.

HPD Chief Troy Carr explained that the primary issue was the safety concern, which was
considered "super unsafe" before. The solution involved rerouting the path to make it safer,
which included adding another crossing guard. While the new route is more expensive than
the previous one, Chief Carr emphasized that safety was the priority, particularly when it
comes to the well-being of children. He acknowledged that the new setup might not be the
most convenient for everyone, but ultimately, the goal was to provide the safest option, even
if no solution is entirely "safe."

Councilmember Henderson explained that there was no perfect solution for everyone and
that it's impossible to make everyone happy with a decision. He acknowledged the concerns
raised in emails, emphasizing that while he listens and tries to understand others'
perspectives, disagreeing with someone doesn't mean he hasn't listened. He noted that while
money was always a factor, the key focus should be on finding the safest, most realistic
option. He also discussed the potential liability for the Council if they choose a less safe
option, despite knowing the safest course of action. Councilmember Ohrn added that the
safety of the children was not in question, and while the extra resources, like the crossing
January 22, 2025 City Council Meeting Minutes                                      Page 5 of 16


guard, were necessary, she didn't see a reason to spend additional money on measures that
wouldn't improve safety but might only make people feel better.

City Engineer Terry noted that implementing a median with pedestrian refuge islands could
offer a viable way to break up the crossing and provide more protection for those on foot,
making the road feel more pedestrian-friendly. However, he cautioned that such
modifications would require significant investment and careful planning to ensure it did not
inadvertently lead to increased traffic congestion or divert traffic through residential areas.

In the interest of addressing safety concerns, the Council reached a consensus on moving
forward with some of the lower-cost, short-term recommendations. This included deploying
speed feedback signs to alert drivers of their speeds as they approached crossing areas. The
Council also agreed that long-term options deserved further consideration, and members
encouraged continued data gathering and analysis to inform any future decisions. City
Engineer Terry assured them that he would bring back refined plans for additional discussion
in an upcoming meeting, with the goal of ensuring that solutions were not only needed but
also sustainable and effective over the long term.

    2.4. High Speed Internet Task Force Updates – Blake Thomas, Community Development
         Director
Community Development Director Blake Thomas presented two significant updates from the
High Speed Internet Task Force. The first update pertained to a fiber conduit lease agreement
currently being worked on with All West Communications. All West was in the process of
developing a fiber network throughout Herriman City and has paused their buildout to finalize
this agreement. The proposed agreement would enable All West to utilize some of the City's
existing conduit infrastructure, allowing them to install their fiber network more efficiently
and potentially reduce the need for installing new conduit in rock-laden soil conditions, which
are a common challenge in Herriman.

The City would gain access to this network exclusively for municipal applications, including
facilitating communication between traffic signals and other city facilities. The Council
voiced overall support for the direction of the agreement, although there were some concerns
about safeguarding City interests within the terms of the deal, such as ensuring perpetual
January 22, 2025 City Council Meeting Minutes                                      Page 7 of 16


access to the fiber network, particularly if ownership or operational changes occur with All
West Communications.

Kirk Zerkle, a representative from All West, discussed their intentions to extend this network,
citing that they've already constructed around 25% of the necessary infrastructure across
approximately 39 miles, with the goal of eventually covering the entire city. The task force
conveyed that this agreement would not preclude potential future agreements with other
providers in areas not serviced by All West, offering flexibility should other networking
options present themselves.

Community Development Director Thomas noted the second item involved a proposed text
amendment dealing with franchise fees, prompted by discussions with Google Fiber, who
expressed concerns about fee structures. Currently, Herriman imposes a fixed 5% fee on
gross receipts for internet and broadband providers, which prompted concerns about
fairness among different service categories. Google argued that such imposed rates were not
consistent with what they incur elsewhere and suggested a negotiated fee arrangement to
accommodate rapidly evolving service models. Council consensus decided against adopting
a flexible negotiated fee model. Instead, they settled on maintaining a 3.5% consistency for
internet and broadband services, aligning with telecom rates, while keeping 5% for cable
services, which reflected a wider industry standard.

Director Thomas indicated his intention to proceed with finalizing the agreement with All
West, ensuring it addressed all potential Council concerns and returning for approval in an
upcoming meeting. He would also make necessary preparations for the draft franchise fee
text amendment as discussed.

    11.4. 11.3. Notice of Pending Ordinance related to Retaining Walls and Building Height –
          Todd Sheeran, City Attorney
City Attorney Todd Sheeran presented an overview of a proposed notice regarding a pending
ordinance focused on retaining walls and building height. He highlighted that the current city
ordinances lack sufficient regulations pertaining to retaining walls and the calculations for
building height, which has emerged as an issue in hillside development projects. City Attorney
Sheeran provided an example of a significant retaining wall that raised considerable concerns
related to safety, aesthetics, and the artificial elevation of building heights. He noted that staff
are in the process of drafting new provisions to address these challenges. The potential
measures include mandating stepped retaining walls and establishing clearer guidelines for
measuring building height on sloped properties. The announcement of the pending ordinance
would enable the City to temporarily suspend processing of relevant applications until
suitable regulations are enacted.

The Council discussed concerns regarding the potential safety risks associated with large
retaining walls, particularly focusing on the inherent risks of significant fall drop-offs for
pedestrians and stability issues over time as these structures age. City Attorney Sheeran
highlighted a particular case where a massive retaining wall was built to artificially modify
the property's elevation to support a sizable building structure, which captured the City’s
attention due to potential public safety issues and neighborhood aesthetics. He acknowledged
