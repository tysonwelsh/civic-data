       a.     Financial Audit Report for Fiscal Year 2019-2020 – Rob Wood, HBME, LLC.
Rob Wood from Hansen, Bradshaw, Malmrose & Erickson reviewed the Financial Statements and
Independent Auditor’s Report for the fiscal year that ended on June 30, 2020. Mr. Wood noted that
on page two there was an Opinion paragraph that indicated that there were no material misstatements.
Mr. Wood noted that was the best opinion he, as an independent auditor, can give an organization.

Mr. Wood discussed the Management’s Discussion and Analysis section of the report that included
comparative numbers from 2019 and 2020. During that time, the net position had grown from $92.2
to $93.4 million. The report also outlined program revenues and general revenues throughout the
City. Mr. Wood reported that there was a slight decrease in revenue from $19.6 to just over $19
million. However, the expenditures grew slightly, from $17.1 to $17.8 million.

Governmental Funds were discussed. Mr. Wood stated that the four funds the City operates on
consists of the General Fund, the Capital Projects Fund, the Redevelopment Agency (“RDA:”), and
Debt Service. The unassigned fund balance was $3.6 million, which meant those funds were available
to the organization to spend. Mr. Wood reported that the percentage in the General Fund compared
to the current year revenues should not exceed 25% as it related to State compliance. The City of
Holladay was at 22.9% and within the range accepted by the State.

Wasatch Front Waste and Recycling District General Manager, Pam Roberts reported that the CARES

Member Gibbons for his support. Ms. Roberts discussed the WFWRC District’s 2021 Budget. She
reported that the Administrative Council Board approved the budget that included fee increases and
a modified area cleanup.

The District raised the following fees:
   • Bulk trailer rentals were increased from $145 to $175 to help offset the full costs; and
   • Curbside green waste collections were raised from $9.50 per month ($114 annually) to $10.50

who participated. As a result, modified clean-up would continue into 2021.

VII.    Consideration of Ordinance 2020-15 Imposing Temporary Zoning Regulations Relating to
        the Enforcement of City Code Provisions Governing the Noise, Height, and Placement of
        Accessory Structures.
Council Member Petersen explained that a resident in her district was fined and told that they would
need to take down a skateboard ramp on their property before Thanksgiving. The main complaint
related to the noise caused by the ramp. Council Member Petersen noted that the family was willing
to make concessions. The moratorium would prevent the family from having to tear down the
skateboard ramp until the City Council reaches a final decision. She felt that the situation allowed the
City Council and staff to look at some of the ordinances and determine whether they were still serving
the residents in a positive way. City Attorney, Todd Godfrey suggested a moratorium on the
enforcement of this particular use. It would last for 180 days and allow the City Council and staff to
determine whether changes should be made.

Mayor Dahle wondered what codes were violated when someone built a skateboard ramp on their
property. Mr. Godfrey believed there were zoning regulations that governed the placement and height
of recreational structures. There were also noise regulations to consider. Council Member Petersen
commented that there were properties in the City that had more than enough room for recreational
structures. She noted that the decibel level may need to be looked at rather than the actual use. The
family that initially brought the issue to Council Member Petersen’s attention found that the decibel
level of a skateboard and skateboard ramp was lower than that of a private swimming pool in a
backyard. Mayor Dahle stated that the City’s noise ordinance would need to be followed, regardless
of the recreational activity.

Mr. Allred reported that Title 9.48.100 lists skateboard ramps as a prohibited use. Additionally, if a
structure is large enough, building codes would need to be considered as well. Council Member
Petersen reminded the Council that the purpose of the discussion was related to the moratorium.

had been a one-off issue in another city related to the construction site. It had been addressed and

with the City if there are issues.

Council Member Fotheringham wondered how the fiber service works. Mr. Brace stated that per the

IX.    Consideration of Resolution 2020-31 Accepting the FY 2019-2020 Moderate-Income
       Housing Report for the City.
Ms. Chamness reported that two years ago, the Legislature encouraged cities to develop affordable
housing in their communities by tying affordable housing provisions to eligibility for federal
transportation dollars. As a result, the City needed to do the following:
    • Amend the General Plan in chapters related to Land Use, Transportation, and Housing; and
    • Choose three strategies from a list of options.
A progress report was required by December 1, 2020, to discuss the implementation of the three items
chosen. Ms. Chamness noted that the City Council needed to approve the 2019-2020 Moderate-
Income Housing Report.

Mr. Allred informed the City Council that this was the first year they were required to report on their
progress. He noted that the chapters related to Transportation and Housing had already been amended
and sent to the State. Mr. Allred discussed the three chosen strategies, which included:
   • Reduced parking in residential areas;
   • The allowance of higher densities; and
   • Accessory Dwelling Units (“ADU”).



Holladay City Council Meeting Minutes 11-19-20                                               7
Mr. Allred indicated that the City was fully compliant in the first two areas but the ADU work was
still underway. They would inform the State that the third item would be finished as soon as possible
in 2021. Chapter 2 amendments also needed to be submitted to the State.

The Findings Report was summarized. Mr. Allred reported that the City of Holladay has studied
affordable housing for at least 15 years. Past studies showed that 18% to 28% of dwellings in the
City are moderately priced. He noted that there were many condos, townhomes, apartments, and
duplexes. One in five of all dwellings in the City are multiple family. Mr. Allred stated that those
numbers are not far off of the statewide average. Mr. Allred noted that housing costs are fairly stable.
Many of the dwellings in the City are attainable by people who make 80% of the Area Median Income
(“AMI”) in Salt Lake County. The State looked at the following numbers: 80% (moderate), 50%
(low income), and 30% (very low income). He noted that there was a lack of housing in many cities
at the 30% ratio. Based on the 2020 numbers, there was a shortage of housing in the 30% bracket in
the City of Holladay. However, there was an improvement when comparing data from 2016 and
2020. Mr. Allred noted that the challenge moving forward was related to building new affordable
housing. He commented that the State allows cities to work with one another. It was not out of the
realm of possibility that the City could work with a neighboring city to develop affordable housing
options.


Council Member Fotheringham discussed a constituent who lives in a historic house on 0.485 acres
of property. The City Council previously discussed amending the ADU ordinance as it relates to the
need for one-half acre. The discussion indicated that an amendment could be made to include a
provision where if the housing footprint within a zone allows the property to be split and subdivided,
even if it is under one-half acre, there could be an ADU in lieu of subdividing. Mr. Allred corrected
Council Member Fotheringham and noted that ADUs were not currently permitted in the City but
guesthouses are permitted. The distinction was that guest houses could not be rented where ADUs
could be rented. Council Member Fotheringham noted that ADUs may be preferable to subdividing
as they would allow the character of the neighborhood to remain intact and provide added
affordability. Ms. Chamness reported that in mid-September she discussed pending items that would
require considerable public engagement including stormwater fees, ADUs and Holladay Crossroads.
Given the fact that the Legislature was going to make changes, staff elected to hold off on the ADU
update.

Council Member Fotheringham moved to approve Resolution 2020-31. Council Member Quinn
seconded the motion. The Council roll call vote was as follows: Council Members Gibbons,
Fotheringham, Petersen, Quinn, Durham and Mayor Dahle in favor. Resolution 2020-31 was

Council requested information from other jurisdictions. Ms. Chamness reported that she sent requests

that there is not one clear standard from one city to the next. It would come down to what the City
of Holladay decides to do. The proposed policy for this item was that all employees would be eligible
to participate in the Retirement Health Savings Plan. Upon implementation, employees with an
accrued balance of 180 or more sick leave hours may be paid 50% for any hours above 180 at their
hourly rate of pay. There was an estimated cost of $12,000 to $20,000 for the first year. The annual
costs would be reduced in the future and likely be within the $5,000 to $6,000 range. The program
would incentivize people to not abuse sick leave and provide a way to save for retirement health.

Mayor Dahle noted that the intention was to clean up and standardize benefit structures in the City.
It was important not to have a system that incentivizes people to accumulate sick hours. The proposed
Retirement Health Savings Plan would be an asset to employees down the road. Ms. Chamness stated
that the City would buy out hours in excess of 180 annually. Mayor Dahle explained that employees
would have 180 hours to use in case of illness but anything in excess of 180 would be bought out at
50% and put into the Retirement Health Savings Plan.

If the City Council was comfortable with the approach, the Policy Manual change would be added to
the December 3, 2020, City Council Meeting agenda. Ms. Chamness noted that she would also need
authorization to enter into an agreement with a third-party administrator who would run the
administration side of things and help employees figure out how to make investment choices. Council
Member Gibbons felt this approach would be of benefit to employees and the City.

Assistant to the City Manager, Holly Smith reported that she had worked with a firm to come up with

over 470 public comments. In addition, there had been a virtual public input meeting with 30 people

        b.      Discussion on Storm Water Fee Ordinance and Policy Manual.
Mayor Dahle noted that the Storm Water Fee Ordinance and Policy Manual would be voted on at the
December 3, 2020, City Council Meeting. Council Member Gibbons compared the Equivalent
Residential Unit (“ERU”) to neighboring cities. If the City of Holladay does not move forward with
the Storm Water Fee, they would be the only municipality in Salt Lake County, other than Brighton,
not to pass a Storm Water Fee or have a Storm Water Fee pending. Council Member Gibbons stated
that he liked what had been presented.

It was reported that the proposed rate for Holladay is a $6.50 monthly base rate per 5,200 square feet.
The proposed model would include two residential areas and a non-residential area. The divisions
were based on the impervious surface area and use characteristics of the surveyed parcels. The
different customer classes included:

   •   Customer Class 1: Residential Area A (Typical Residential Property);
   •   Customer Class 2: Residential Area B (Estates); and
   •   Customer Class 3: Non-Residential.

Mayor Dahle commented that a constituent asked about properties without curb and gutter. He
wanted to know whether the fee would still be applicable. Mayor Dahle believed it would as it is was
City-wide Storm Water Fee. It was not possible to individually assess each lot. Mr. Bunch reported
that if there was an installed facility on the property that retained stormwater, the resident could apply
for a 50% reduction of the fee. He added that commercial sites under the current ordinance would
retain a significant portion of stormwater on-site. That formula in the policy program could be used
and the 50% reduction could be applied.

Council Member Gibbons wondered if wording should be added to the Policy Manual to specify that
Mr. Bunch would have the power to grant a credit if there is a low level of impervious surface. He
believed that if a lot is undeveloped, there should not necessarily be a fee imposed. Council Member
Gibbons suggested providing a credit or reduction in those cases.

Mr. Godfrey pointed out that that the fee related to the impact each property had on the storm drain
system. However, the City also provides a service. Even lots with low levels of impervious surface
would receive some level of service from the City’s stormwater system. Council Member
Fotheringham clarified that the fee was not just for stormwater that affects personal property.
Stormwater also needs to be dealt with once it reaches the public roads and public spaces.

Mr. Bunch discussed possible boundary changes for Residential Areas A and B. Based on discussions
from the previous City Council Meeting, staff took another look at the boundary between Residential
Areas A and B. Options were explored to either expand or reduce the boundary. The proposed map
was included in the packet. Mr. Bunch noted that the current boundary would likely lead to a number
of applications to change the designation from Residential Area B to Residential Area A. Instead of
handling the individual application reviews, one option was to adjust the boundary and move some
of the smaller one-half-acre lots into Residential Class A.
