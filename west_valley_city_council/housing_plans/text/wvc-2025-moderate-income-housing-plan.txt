INTRODUCTION
Affordable housing is a regional issue. As one of the most urbanized states in the nation with
roughly three-quarters of the population living along the Wasatch Front, it is common for a person
to live and work within two completely different municipalities. Persons searching for a home
consider price, commute time, school performance and neighborhood amenities over municipal
boundaries.

The Wasatch Front has experienced substantial population growth over the last 10 years. However,
the type of growth in municipalities along the Wasatch Front has varied greatly. Some are largely
bedroom communities with little housing diversity. Others are job centers with a mix of housing
types.

A few communities cannot support the majority of the moderate income housing for a region
without facing significant negative effects such as poor school and student achievement. However,
as this document will demonstrate, some communities in Salt Lake County have relatively little
moderate income housing. This is due, in large part, to the fact that such communities have a low
percentage of rental housing, which is generally more affordable than for-sale housing, and little
diversity of housing aside from single family detached homes. As a result, the amount of affordable
housing varies substantially from one community to another. For these reasons, this document
compares West Valley’s housing affordability with other municipalities within the County as well as
the County overall.

State code (10-9a-403(2)(a)(iii)) directs municipalities to include a moderate income housing
element in their General Plan that:
    • provides a realistic opportunity to meet the need for additional moderate income housing
       within the next five years;
    • selects three or more moderate income housing strategies described in State code for
       implementation, including two additional moderate income housing strategies for
       municipalities with a fixed guideway public transit station; and
    • includes an implementation plan.

In drafting the moderate income housing element, the City must do the following as outlined in
Utah Code Section 10-9a-403(2)(b):
    • consider the Legislature’s determination that municipalities shall facility a reasonable
         opportunity for a variety of housing, including moderate income housing to:
             o meet the needs of people of various income levels living, working, or desiring to live
                 or work in the community; and
             o allow people with various incomes to benefit from and fully participate in all
                 aspects of neighborhood and community life;
    • include an analysis of how the municipality will provide a realistic opportunity for the
         development of moderate income housing within the next five years;
    • include a recommendation to implement the required number of moderate income
         strategies from State code, including the station area plan requirements for each fixed
         guideway public transit station within the City.

this plan will utilize the most current ACS data to reflect changes in the housing market.

EXISTING SUPPLY OF MODERATE INCOME HOUSING
To understand the need for additional moderate income housing over the next five years, an
understanding of the existing supply of moderate income housing is necessary. Moderate income
housing is defined in Utah Code Section 10-9a-103(41) as “housing occupied or reserved for
occupancy by households with a gross household income equal to or less than 80% of the median
gross income for households of the same size in the county in which the city is located.” According
to the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), the 2023 median family income
in Salt Lake County was $106,000. A low (80% of median) income household earned $84,800.
Therefore, for the purposes of this plan, moderate income housing in Salt Lake County during the
year 2023 is defined as those housing units that were affordable to households that earn $84,800
(80%) or less annually. Such households represent approximately 44% of all households in the
County (ACS 2023 Table B19001).

According to HUD, “families who pay more than 30% of their income for housing are considered
cost burdened and may have difficulty affording necessities such as food, clothing, transportation,
and medical care.” Thus, the generally accepted definition of affordability is for a household to pay
no more than 30 percent of its annual income on housing. This 30% figure should also include
housing related expenses such as utilities, insurance, taxes, etc.

To estimate the supply of moderate income housing, the following targeted income groups were
evaluated – low income (80% of the median income), very low income (50% of the median income)
and extremely low income. The FY 2014 Consolidated Appropriations Act changed the definition of
extremely low-income to be the greater of 60 percent of the Section 8 very low-income limit or the
poverty guideline as established by the Department of Health and Human Services, provided that
this amount is not greater than the Section 8 50% very low-income limit. Table 1 below lists the
annual household income for a family of four according to HUD, the monthly income available for
housing, and the affordable home price for each targeted income group. For example, a very low
income household makes $53,000 annually, can afford to spend $1,325 monthly on housing and
could afford a home priced up to $135,000 in 2023.

A number of assumptions were made to determine how much money a family would need to afford
housing. For rental properties, ACS Gross Rent numbers, which include utilities, were used. For
owner-occupied housing utility expenses, $345 per month was used based on research from
move.org. For the purposes of calculating mortgage payments, a 6.81% interest rate on a 30-year,
fixed rate mortgage was assumed. An average Utah homeowner insurance amount is $95 monthly,
and this amount was divided by the Salt Lake County median home price to come up with a rate of
0.002353. West Valley City’s property tax rate was used for taxes.

Since affordable housing is a regional issue, this document considers not only the supply of
affordable units in West Valley, but also in Salt Lake County overall, as well as other municipalities
within the County.

Given double digit percentage price appreciation over the last several years and higher mortgage
interest rates, the affordability of owner-occupied housing in Salt Lake County has plummeted. In
the City’s last Moderate Income Housing Plan, 38.2% of all owner-occupied housing in the County
was affordable to low income households. This percentage has now dropped to 9.1%.

While West Valley City’s percentage of owner-occupied housing affordable to low income
households has also dropped considerably, the City still has a higher percentage than most cities
in the County. In fact, West Valley’s percentage at this price point is 3 to 4 times higher than other
less affordable municipalities like Bluffdale, Cottonwood Heights, Draper, Herriman, Riverton, and
South Jordan.

For very low income households, West Valley has the highest percentage (9.4%) of affordable,
owner-occupied housing units. West Valley City also has the highest percentage (7.3%) of owner-
occupied housing units that are affordable to extremely low income households.

There are several points to be made from Table 11. First, the three cities – Midvale, Salt Lake City,
and South Salt Lake – with the highest percentages of housing affordable to low income
households are the same cities with the highest percentages of renter-occupied housing. Second,

exceeds the percentage of housing units that are affordable to these same income groups. Third,
West Valley City’s housing is more affordable than 13 of the other 17 other municipalities and is
more than double that found in Bluffdale, Herriman, Riverton, and South Jordan.

It is interesting to note that the State holds all cities to the same minimum standards for addressing
moderate income housing with one exception – whether or not a city has a fixed guideway public

Strategy P: Demonstrate utilization of a moderate income housing set aside from a community
reinvestment agency, redevelopment agency, or community development and renewal agency to
create or subsidize moderate income housing.

In 2017, West Valley City entered into an agreement with the University of Utah that started the
Hometown Scholar program under the Business School Opportunity Scholars program. The
premise of the program was to use housing set aside dollars along with the other scholarship
dollars in the Opportunity Scholars system to scholarship West Valley City students at a “full ride”
level. Pursuant to this agreement, each year the West Valley City Redevelopment Agency provides
housing set aside funds to the University of Utah which they use to provide housing. In exchange,
the University of Utah aids our students to reach the full ride level. The housing is covered by these
funds in one of two ways. First, the student may be housed in one of 16 slots the City keeps in
apartments at Fairbourne Station. Second, the student may be housed in other student and
business school approved housing.

The students must be from West Valley City, first generation college students, and committed to
staying in the City after graduation for the same period of time as they were assisted.

From 2017 to 2021, the City’s Redevelopment Agency spent $150,000 annually to support this
program. Due to cost increases, the annual expenditure since 2022 has been $180,000.

Implementation Step 1: Annually administer the Hometown Scholars program using housing set
aside funds.

Timeline for Step 1: Annual administration and reporting on funds expended and students
assisted

Measure of Success for Step 1: First generation college students from West Valley City are
housed through college, graduate, and stay in the City after graduation.

ANALYSIS OF HOW THE CITY WILL PROVIDE A REALISTIC OPPORTUNITY
FOR THE DEVELOPMENT OF MODERATE INCOME HOUSING WITHIN THE
NEXT FIVE YEARS
Over the last five years, the City has issued permits for an average of about 390 dwelling units per
year. However, City staff anticipates a lower amount of permits issued over the next five years for
two reasons – higher interest rates and a limited supply of vacant land within the City.

City staff anticipates an annual average number of permits between 250 and 300 dwelling units for
the next five years. This would result in 1,250 to 1,500 new dwelling units. Based on approvals
already made or in process for apartment and condos units in five apartment projects, staff
anticipates approximately 725 new moderate income housing units will be constructed within the
next five years. This number is roughly 50 to 60% of the new units anticipated over the next five
years and does not include other moderate income housing units which may be approved and
constructed within the same timeframe.
