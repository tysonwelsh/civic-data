Historical Growth of Sandy City

Early settlers to the Sandy area in the mid-1800 s were primarily Mormon farmers sent out by
Brigham Young from Salt Lake City to help grow food for the residents of the valley.  It soon
became evident that the lack of water and the sandy soil would limit extensive cultivation and
food production.  By 1871, vast tracts of land remained uncultivated.

The shipping of granite stone for construction of the Salt Lake Temple together with the later
discovery of rich ores at Alta to the east and Bingham to the west caused a shift in Sandy¶s focus
from an agricultural community to a primary shipping point.  This, together with the smelters and
sampling mills, emphasized Sandy as an important railroad junction for the Utah Southern,
Denver/Rio Grande, and Wasatch/Jordan Valley Railroads.  The townsite in 1871 covered about
160 acres of land and had about 488 persons living here.

Both industries, mining and transportation, continued to play a prominent role in the early
development of Sandy.  Although the City realized an early geographical and political identity,
once the Big Emma and other mines at Alta and the mining activities in general declined in the
early 1900 s, the City reverted back to an agricultural community.

Initial efforts were made to call this townsite Emmaville  named after the big mine at Alta , but
with little success.  Many of the old-timers tell different stories of why the name of Sandy was
chosen.  The two most commonly known and discussed are  1  when Brigham Young saw the
soil, he declared that because of its sandy nature, the settlement should be called Sandy, or  2
after the sandy-beared, redheaded engineer of the Utah Southern Railroad, Alexander Kinghorn. 1

A movement to incorporate Sandy began in 1892.  Like many cities in Utah, Sandy incorporated
to exercise the police power.  At that time, the counties were considered subdivisions of the State
and were set up to administer state law  e.g., recording deeds  and to provide a few basic services
 e.g., county roads  required by a rural population.  The additional services required by urban
areas would presumably be provided through the incorporation of cities and towns.  Early cities
had considerable local autonomy entrusted to them.  Their powers authorized them to support 
and regulate schools, to borrow money on the town¶s credit, to make regulations to prevent the 
spread of contagious diseases, to provide water for the residents, to improve the streets, to license
and tax businesses, etc. 2

The elections of November 1893, ratified the incorporation of Sandy.  The government was
organized as a mayor/council form of government and the first municipal election in 1897 saw
the Independent Part  or lunch basket party  pitted against the Citizens¶ Party  or shovel and
broom party .  The town then covered 404 acres.

By 1919, Sandy was within one-half hour car or streetcar ride from Salt Lake City.  It had about
100 street lights, supplied with power from Utah Power and Light.  The land was pretty well
irrigated and considerable acreage was planted with sugar beets, grains, fruit, etc.  At this time,
the City¶s population was about 2,000.3

The City changed little between the first and second world wars.  Following the end of World
War II, cities across the nation began to feel the pressures of the increased demand for housing
and municipal services created by the servicemen returning from war.  The economic
µdepression¶ of the 1930 s had made devastating effects upon the budgets of many cities and most
conservatives feared the return of high unemployment and economic stagnation caused by the
winding down of the war effort.  Expectations of lower taxes led city officials to believe that they
would be hard pressed to provide the services needed by their existing communities, and thus
were reluctant to approve new annexations.  This conservative line of thought led to a µsit-tight¶
policy towards annexation.

While the cities were content to µsit-tight,¶ the demand for housing grew.  As a result of this
pressure for housing, Salt Lake County began increasing its municipal-type services to the
unincorporated areas, as well as providing planning for the major portion of the valley.  For
example, Salt Lake County had included Sandy in its Little Cottonwood Planning District and
had designated the unincorporated areas around Sandy City as a µresidential preserve.¶

The County became increasingly aware of the costs of µleapfrogging¶ development.  In an attempt
to control premature growth and to require that new development pay for its impact to County
services, the County refused to grant zoning changes within the residential preserve areas,
restricted the issuance of building permits, and required developers to bear the bulk of the costs
of extending necessary improvements to the developments.  As a result, developers found it more
expedient and less expensive to annex to the cities, which were much less demanding.  

By the mid-1950 s, the fear of growth had diminished and many cities started practicing an open-
door policy towards annexations.  But cities, like Sandy, were caught unprepared to deal with the
rapid growth.  Sandy¶s annexations began slowly, but increased so that by the late 1960 s, the
µboom¶ was here.  What had started as small additions changed in 1969 when 2700 acres were
annexed and every year thereafter major annexations have taken place.

population changes and characteristics, and some of the problems with community identity which

Annexation

Annexation has been the major determinant of population growth in Sandy City.  Each
annexation has a different character and thus a varied impact on a city.  For example, a particular
area may have high property values and be µripe¶ for housing development, but also may present
unusual geologic, development or drainage problems which may diminish some of the
advantages of development.  Or the city may choose to annex islands because they have created
burdens on the city¶s police and fire departments even though they are not within the city
boundaries.

Changes in municipal boundaries tend to occur in a very short period of time  e.g., 10-20 years
and tend to be permanent.  Often, municipal boundary changes are hard to manage because they
come primarily when the city is changing from basically a rural to an urban area.  The expansion
of the range of services to meet the rapidly multiplying population often has led the city into a
catch-up game, giving little time for land use and financial planning  e.g., capital program and
budgeting .  Table 3.1 illustrates Sandy¶s physical growth.

Annexation decisions also tend to be irreversible and permanent.  They determine what areas will
become part of the City¶s political community, which areas must be serviced by the city, what
areas will contribute to the city¶s tax base.  At the same time, the annexations may break up
communities already established, e.g., the original Crescent Community has been divide between
Sandy, Draper and South Jordan.  Thus critical annexation decisions are made in a setting usually
without plans and with the uncertainty of a constantly changing community  most of Sandy¶s
islands were created during this period .

Ideally, the annexations should be agreeable and advantageous to all governmental jurisdictions,
but politically, this may not be possible.  The following sections will review some of the
problems encountered with the state annexation law, the resolution adopted regarding the original
Granite Community, and the concern over the division of the Crescent Community.  The
conclusion outlines the major implications for an annexation policy for Sandy City.


                          UTAH LAW REGARDING ANNEXATIONS

In February 1979, the Utah State Legislature passed a new annexation law  HB 61  which
outlines the criteria, policy declaration, and standards required for annexation and provides for a
boundary commission to settle annexation disputes within each county.  The basic criteria
remain essentially the same as the old law:

        1.     the submission of a petition requesting annexation signed by a majority of the 
               owners of property in the area.  These owners must represent at least one-third of 
               the assessed valuation, according to the last assessment rolls

        2.     that the proposed area for annexation must be contiguous to corporate boundaries 
               of the municipality

        3.     that a plat or map of the area be drawn by an engineer or surveyor

        4.     that the approval be made by a resolution passed by a 2/3 majority of the 
               governing body

        5.     the recording of the plat by the County Recorder

In addition to these items required of those who wish to annex to a city, the new State Law
requires a city to adopt a policy declaration and standards for annexation.   If the area to be
annexed is more than five acres.   Such a policy declaration must include:

       1.      a map or legal description of an unincorporated territory into which the 
               municipality anticipates or favors expansion of its boundaries

       2.      a policy statement of the specific criteria pursuant to which a municipality will 
               favor or not favor a petition for annexation; these must describe:

               a.      the standards consistent with the state law
               b.      the character of the community
               c.      the need for municipal services in developed and developing 
                       unincorporated areas
               d.      the plans and time frame of the municipality for extension of municipal 
                       services
               e.      how the services will be financed
               f.      the tax consequences to residents in both new and old territory of the 
                       municipality
               g.      the interests of all affected entities

The standards required for an area to be annexed are:

       1.      It must be contiguous to the boundaries of the annexing municipality at the time 
               the annexation is approved by the governing body of the municipality;

       2.      It must lie within the area projected for municipal expansion under the annexing 
               municipality¶s policy declaration;

       3.      It must not be included within the boundaries of another incorporated 
               municipality, except as provided within the law as the powers of the boundary 
               commission;

       4.      It shall not create unincorporated islands within the boundaries of the municipality
               except that existing islands or peninsulas within a municipality may be annexed in
               portions, leaving islands, if a public hearing is held and the governing body of the 
               municipality adopt a resolution to the effect that the creation or leaving of an 
               island is in the interests of the municipality; and

        5.     If it includes urban development, the annexation of which would displace 
               municipal-type services presently being provided by an affected entity applying 
               for boundary commission review, the actual taxes and other revenue which would 
               be lost by the affected entity¶s actual delivery costs of services assumed by the 
               municipality.  In computing the tax and revenue loss and service delivery costs, 
               only the figures for the applicable budget year preceding the day on which the 
               petition for annexation is filed shall be used.

The new annexation law also provides for a boundary commission.  This commission would
consist of seven members: two representing the county and appointed by the county commission,
two representing the municipalities selected by a municipal selection committee, and three
representing the general public appointed by the other four members.  The municipal selection
committee is to consist of the mayor of each municipality within the county.

The boundary commission can:

        1.     upon application of an affected entity protesting a proposed boundary change, 
               review and approve or disapprove with or without amendment, wholly, partially 
               or conditionally, a proposal for a boundary change of a local entity;

        2.     apply for and accept financial assistance from the State of Utah and/or its local 
               entities;

        3.     adopt procedures for the evaluation of proposals and make and enforce rules for 
               the orderly and fair conduct of its proceedings;

        4.     authorize a member of the commission to administer oaths to any person when 
               necessary in the performance of the duties of the commission;

        5.     appoint and assign staff personnel and employ or contract for professional or 
               consulting services to the extent available funds will permit and to incur usual and
               necessary expenses for the accomplishments of its functions.

The local boundary commission gets involved only when an annexation by a municipality is
protested by another entity.  The protesting entity must file an application within five days of the
conclusion of the public hearing  now required for all annexations over 5 acres  held by the
municipality initiating the change.  The factors to be considered in the review of a proposal shall
be limited to the standards prescribed by law and the contents of the policy declaration of any
municipality proposing to change its boundary to include all or part of the area proposed to be
annexed.  The commission may approve the proposal with or without conditions or
modifications, or disapprove it.

The new law also provides for extraterritorial powers for cities within one-half mile of its
boundaries in the unincorporated territory which the city has proposed for eventual expansion. 
The property owners desiring to develop or improve property within the one-half mile must
notify the city in writing of his desire and identify all legal and factual barriers preventing an
annexation to the city.  At the end of twelve months of this notice to the city, the property owner
may develop.  Urban development beyond one-half mile of the city may be restricted or an
impact statement may be required by interlocal agreement.

Many problems with the state law and annexation practices have been evident.  The major ones
are:

       1.      that the assessment rolls may not reflect the current owners of the property;

       2.      that the incentives created are to encourage cities to fight over land;

       3.      that cities are not prevented from reaching out to capture high tax generators 
               without accepting responsibility for the surrounding residential areas  e.g., the 
               Brickyard by Salt Lake City ;

       4.      that peculiar µcherry stem¶ annexation along railroads, canals, highways in order 
               to make land contiguous are allowed, making planning and provision of services 
               very difficult;

       5.      that annexations often are passed by simple majority rather than 2/3 vote of 
               council; and

       6.      that annexations are approved with little thought as to how the areas will be 
               serviced.


Some of these issues can be addressed by the new boundary commission.  The commission,
when asked to mediate a protested annexation, can review the city¶s purpose for extending its
boundary  e.g., does it seize a large tax generator?  Does it at the same time assume responsibility
for surrounding residential property?  and determine whether the city is capable of extended
municipal services into the area within a reasonable length of time.

But several major concerns remain.  First, the state law has no clear definition of what county
and city services must be provided to residents.  Salt Lake County, in particular, has provided
many municipal-type services that were originally meant only for cities.  As the cities of the
County grow, they are slowly absorbing not only residences, but also the County¶s prime tax
generators.  This makes agreements with the County regarding ultimate municipal boundaries
uncertain.  Second, the City¶s policy on growth should be based on good planning principles

rather than solely on the based of the amount of tax generated.  Until the state annexation and tax

location of sale rather than by population  the incentives created by the present laws force
uneven, haphazard development and µleapfrogging.¶  Sandy City is working toward resolving

Population Growth
Many decisions made by government officials are largely based on population projections and
estimates of the community¶s physical growth potential.  A city¶s growth, however, can only be
view in context of the region and the state.  According to a recent Utah Foundation report, Utah
is the sixth fastest growing state in the Union.  As a result, the cities of Salt Lake, Davis, Weber
and Utah Counties are experiencing rapid growth.

The growth of the cities in Salt Lake County is shown in Table 3.2.  In the 1960 s, growth
concentrated in Salt Lake City, Midvale, Murray, Sandy and West Jordan.  In the 1970 s, the
focus of growth in the Salt Lake area has been concentrated in Sandy, West Jordan, South
Jordan, and Riverton.  The southern end of the County in 1978 had approximately 144,600
persons or about 25% of the county¶s population.

Sandy City is particularly inviting because of its easy access into the region¶s central business
area of Salt Lake City, its availability of land for development, its proximity to the ski resorts,
and its ability to provide good municipal services.

Three major variables are considered when attempting to determine the needs of the present
population and the growth potential of a city: the size of the population, its spatial distribution,
and its composition.  Population size gives some indication of the overall dimensions of the
physical environment and assists in establishing the space needs of the various categories of land
use.  Population distribution gives a city a fair idea where the various land uses and facilities
should be located within the urban area.  Population composition includes the age, sex,
educational and income characteristics, and assists the city when estimating the space needs of
the various categories of the population.

population:  1  the number of dwelling units in the city and  2  an estimate of the number of

The average number of persons per household may be obtained by a census or survey of the

However, using building permits in this manner has its limitations.  The permits do not indicate
demolished houses.  They do not reflect those existing houses brought into the city through
annexation.  In addition they include houses which were not built after a building permit was
issued.  Building permits closely approximate the growth, can be updated quickly, and can lessen
the distortion created by the county islands.  Aside from actual count, analyzing building permits
is one of the best guides to estimating dwelling units.

Because Sandy has grown so rapidly and because of the county islands within the city, it was felt
that a unit-by-unit count, the fourth major method, would be the most accurate way to estimate
dwelling units for planning purposes.  This estimate also can be used as a base year to which later
estimates using building permits or utility hookups can be added.

                                 POPULATION PROJECTIONS

After a city has decided upon an estimate of current dwelling units and population, it then must
determine what its future growth potential may be.  However, ther are many methods which can
be used to obtain this data.  Each projection method had its limitations, but they can be modified
so that they can be very useful units of measure.

Each method makes some assumptions about what economic conditions will exist in the future,
what the population characteristics and growth rate will be, etc.  There is no way to predict every
important development that could occur in the area¶s economy within a ten to twenty year
timespan nor to predict the various combinations of favorable and unfavorabel economic events
that actually will happen.  No matter ow careful one may be in calculating projections, the
assumptions upon which these predictions are based may become inappropriate to planning as
time passes and unexpected events occur.

Consequently, population projections are one-time predictions of future conditions.  This does
not mean that the population projections are worthless as a tool -- they serve a critical purpose as
one indicator of the future.  But it does mean that the projections must be updated continually
and that the decision-makers when basing decisions on these estimates must be careful to take
into account any recent development or changes in the community that might alter the
projections.

Land Use

Land use patterns are a result of a multitude of private and public decisions made over time
concerning how land should be used and where different activities should be placed.  The types,
intensities, and locations of land use affect every aspect of community life.  In addition, land use
is affected by such factors as the relationship of the city to the region, the economic and tax bases
of the city, the residential and nonresidential demand for and use of public facilities and services,
the social structure, and the overall quality of life in the city.

The three primary factors affecting the types of land uses are:  1  public opinion, e.g., citizens¶
goals and policies, public hearings, etc.,  2  the existing land development and historical trends,
and  3  land suitability and environmental considerations.

                                       NATURAL FEATURES

More and more cities are realizing the need to review natural and physical features as a major
ingredient in developing a comprehensive plan.  Such a review provides a better understanding of
ongoing natural processes occurring within the community and of the natural hazards which
might result from inappropriate development.



Page 3 - 26                                    Chapter III - Growth, Land Use and Community Identity
                                                           Sandy City General Plan




Environmental conditions within a city may differ greatly because of minor differences in
elevation or location.  Each environmental factor  e.g., geology, soils, slope, vegetation,
hydrology, etc.  Has various responses and capacity for a particular use or development.  The
ability to predict or to control the impact of a particular use on the environment requires detailed
information on all environmental factors.  The ultimate objective is to clarify the relationship
between the form of the land and different levels of environmental sensitivity to development.

For example, topography gives an overview of the character of a site and can suggest the most
suitable site for buildings, access locations, and activity areas.  Modern development practices
such as cluster subdivision techniques and curvilinear street patterns which follow the terrain
rather than alter it, have lessened many of the adverse effects of development on low or
moderately sloped areas.  In most cases, typical development patterns are inappropriate for
extremely hilly areas which should instead be preserved for open space or large lot development
which can be its nature be more harmonious with the natural terrain.

Often, when developers impose conventional development patters on variable topography, the
result is monotonous sites, dangerous conditions, loss of good views, or loss of good recreational
opportunities.  There also is concern regarding the grading for new development.  Sometimes the
natural vegetation is removed for development, but no rehabilitation plan is implemented.  Or
large tracts under construction may take more than one season to be completed, leaving the land
exposed to erosion caused by spring runoff.

Because of the concerns for the environment of Sandy City, a major policy recommendation for
the plan is to encourage a detailed study by a consultant who is an experienced geologist to
provide specific data and maps to guide future development.  Inventories of natural features are
combined with the data and studies of economic, social, housing, and the city¶s fiscal and legal
resources to serve as the basis for the plan.  By this process, the plan can identify ³special areas´
which singly or in combination establish the individualized fabric of a community¶s character
and those which are particularly vulnerable, sensitive, or hazardous to new development. 
Hopefully, the result will be a refinement of the community goals and policies regarding future
community development in Sandy City, and the avoidance of costly mistakes in land use
decisions.

Impacts of Growth: Effects on Community Identity

A general plan does not only attempt to make an urban area more efficient or better organized to
handle the practical social and economic necessities of urban life.  It also is responsible for
helping a community develop as a pleasant, attractive, and human place for people to live, work
and play.



Chapter III - Growth, Land Use and Community Identity                                       Page 3 - 27
              Sandy City General Plan




People develop a fond attachment for identifiable focal points in their ³hometown.´  This
attachment may develop through associations with a particular park, land form, public building,
an area of older homes, vegetation, or a distinct type of subdivision design.  Different
environments facilitate or resist the processes of creating a sense of community.  Each individual
person creates his image of the city.  As people interact with members of a group or
neighborhood, together, they form their ³public image´ of the city.

Sandy¶s growth has created special problems for the development of community identity.  The
abundance of young families new to the area and the small proportion of older residents makes it
difficult to pass on the history and sense of community shared by the older residents of original
Sandy, Crescent , and Granite.  Sandy¶s continual growth has prevented periods of stabilization
and results in an environment which hampers the formation and growth of the community
identity.  It also inadvertently has created pressures on existing community identities, such as
Crescent¶s and has caused these identities to diminish.  It will be a major task to reestablish
Sandy¶s ³sense of community´ and to maintain and enhance community feelings.

By concentrating on a systematic approach to the physical setting and by continuing a strong
citizen participation program, the City can lay the foundation for a distinctive and firm social
identity within Sandy City.


                    PHYSICAL SETTING AND COMMUNITY IDENTITY

If done carefully, planning can help alleviate some of the community identity problems resulting
from growth.  The major elements used to mold the physical environment emphasize planning
communities or neighborhoods, major focal points, landmarks, and streets and trails.  These are
defined as follows:

        Communities and Neighborhoods: Areas which are recognized as having some common, 
        identifying character.  In Sandy, these districts are the planning communities which may 
        be divided further into neighborhood groups.

        Major focal points or nodes: The hub of activities such as commercial centers and 
        community parks within the planning communities.

        Landmarks: Major public buildings, schools, mountains, other distinctive topographic 
        features, historical monuments, etc.

        Streets and trails: Channels along which residents customarily or occasionally move.  
        They may be streets, bus routes, canals, etc.  The streets, trails and walkways make up the
        ³skeleton´ of urban design along which all other elements of the physical setting are 
        connected.

Page 3 - 28                                    Chapter III - Growth, Land Use and Community Identity
                                                          Sandy City General Plan




None of these physical elements exist by themselves.  Communities are structured with major
focal points, penetrated by streets, and sprinkled with landmarks.  The major elements of urban
form are described below  also see Appendix Q: Community and Neighborhood Communication
Manual .

       1.      Communities or Neighborhoods.

               Neighborhoods are relatively large city areas which contain common character.  
               Traditional associations, which residents of original Sandy and Crescent share, 
               play a critical role in the identity development process and provide a basis upon 
               which to build a stronger identity within the communities of Sandy City.

               The neighborhood approach to establishing identity is enhanced if each is easily 
               visible as a whole or if it is separated from adjacent communities by topographic 
               features  e.g., as Dry Creek separates Bell Canyon .  Within these communities, 
               neighborhoods are formed where groups can interact and discuss common
               problems.

               Community names give identity even when the communities do not show a 
               striking contrast with other communities of the city.  Names crystallize identity, 
               give locational clues, and strongly reinforce suggestions toward identity or 
               structures. 

               Signs can serve a similar function.  For example, the Sandy City logo on street 
               signs was praised and supported by the citizens in their city-wide goals for Sandy 
               City.  Special signs also can be used to set an area apart and make it distinctive.  
               For example, the historical signs on the Avenues of Salt Lake City set that area 
               apart from the neighborhoods adjacent to it.




       2.      Major Focal Points or Nodes.

               The nodes are those areas where residents of the community focus their activities. 
               A major community park or shopping center provides opportunities for neighbors 
               to interact with one another.  A node is more defined if it has a sharp, definite 
               boundary, and does not trail off uncertainly on every side.  Examples of nodes in 
               Sandy City are Canyon Center, 9400 South and 700 East, Flat Iron Mesa Park, 

Chapter III - Growth, Land Use and Community Identity                                     Page 3 - 29
              Sandy City General Plan




               etc.


        3.     Landmarks.

               Residents of a city increasingly rely upon a system of landmarks as their guide.  
               The landmarks provide some aspect which is unique or memorable in its context 
               and which may give directional orientation or serve as a symbol.  Landmarks are 
               more identifiable and significant if they are place where they will contrast with 
               the areas around them.  For example, the water tanks atop Flat Iron Mesa Park, 
               although not a scenic attraction can be seen for miles in any direction.


        4.     Streets and Trails.

               A system of streets, trails, and walkways is the most potent means by which 
               landmarks, neighborhoods and focal points are tied together to create a city¶s 
               image.  To be effective, they should have a singular quality to mark them off from
               others.  For example, those streets which have well-known focal nodes or 
               landmarks along their paths and which are connected by a continuing image of 
               landscaping, type of housing or street lighting help unite the city and give the 
               residents a sense of belonging.

               The strategic locations of streets and trails are at major intersections and at the 
               entrances to the city.  The intersection is a key point of connection where one 
               must make a decision as to which direction to travel.  While doing this, the 
               traveler heightens his attention to surrounding details and perceives nearby

                      SOCIAL SETTING AND COMMUNITY IDENTITY

Citizen¶s reaction to decisions made by public officials often reflect the kind of experience they
have had with city officials in Sandy and in other governmental jurisdictions.  These experiences
influence local attitudes toward public actions, create expectations and can determine local
disposition to participate in the planning process, etc.  This view of government is complicated in

                                                            Sandy City General Plan




Sandy City becuase the City has grown so rapidly, bringing in new and varied people who
together have not yet established a common identity.  It is critical to the planning process and to
the stability of the community that efforts continue to emphasize and to encourage strong citizen
participation.

The citizens¶ committees in the 30 residential planning communities of Sandy City have laid the
foundation for a strong citizen participation program, yet most of those who participated
questioned whether the City would really listen to them.  To alleviate some of these fears and to
continue to build upon the enthusiasm of these initial efforts, it is crucial that the City keep
committee representatives involved in every stage of the planning and decision-making processes
and to encourage the committees to continue to meet on their own to discuss problems within
their community.

Specifically, citizen participation seeks to accomplish the following:

            - To Communicate: Citizen participation brings together viewpoints which are not             
              otherwise heard and gives each citizen the opportunity to broaden his perspective of the   
             community.

        - To Resolve Conflict: It assists decision-makers to clarify and to resolve conflicting 
          preferences among competing interests of the public.

        - To Provide Guidance: It guides administrative decisions to be consistent with the policy
          preferences and priorities of the effected public.

        - To Protect Interests: Citizen participation ensures that effected citizens and        
          organizations are given the opportunity to express their individual interests within the 
          larger public decision-making process.


There are two essential elements needed for an effective citizen participation effort and to make
the best use of citizens.  These elements are   1  the provision of a basic structure for each
committee and  2  the establishment of a system of communication  see Appendix B:
Community and Neighborhood Communication Manual .

Usually when committees flounder and/or fail, it is because they do not know their purpose or
how far they can and cannot go.  Before a committee is set up by the Planning Commission or
City Council, it is useful to define in general terms the basic goal the committee is to achieve
 e.g., a report, evaluate a proposal, etc. , how the committee is to be organized, and how long it is
to meet.  This is only a sketch because once formed, most committees by the very nature of the
individual members will enlarge upon the original purpose outlined for them by the Commission
or Council in order to add new and creative ideas.  The sketch is meant to help them get started.

Another major problem which commonly occurs in cities is that the same citizens are chosen for
several committees.  This creates a burden on these citizens because they get tired, lose interest,
and finally say enough is enough.  Or if the committee is limited in size, new residents are
prevented from getting involved in city government.  To alleviate this problem, many cities have
chosen to limit membership to one committee per resident  unless the person serving on two
related committees serves as a liaison between the committees .

Communication is the critical link in the entire participation process.  It is important both in
terms of receiving information or data for the report or plan and also in terms of getting word out
to the public so it knows what each committee is doing and what they are recommending to the
Commission and Council.

Representatives from the planning committees or community representatives can help serve this
communication purpose as well.  It is important that the City continually tie the planning
committees or community representatives into its ad hoc or long-term committees.  This is
essential for three reasons:

        1.     The citizen groups supply a direct communication link between city officials and 
               the groups meeting outside of city hall who are discussing city concerns;  

        2.     The citizen groups can ³spread the word´ to their communities faster than written 
               communications and often with more reliability than most word-of-mouth 
               communications; and

        3.     If the City takes into account the citizens¶ recommendations consistently, those on
               the committees can quickly report back to their groups the efforts made by the 
               City.  Thus the citizens¶ committees or councils can become a major tool in 
               changing the existing city image.

the formation of two other committees.

Planning of the physical environment can be used to help the community develop as a pleasant,

attractive and human place for people to live and work.  The major elements used to mold the
physical envrionment emphasize neighborhoods, activity centers, and landmarks.  Streets tie all
the elements together to create a city¶s image and a sense of belonging.  Because the City 
Council recognized the need to emphasize community identity, it responded to the request by
citizens to develop a streetscape plan.4

Specifically, landscaped streets:  1  give a city a human rather than separate or barren
appearance;  2  create a sense of place with landscaping on public grounds and around public
buildings;  3  soften the harshness of other man-made structures;  4  add unity to visually
disorganized or overly monotonous developments; and  5  reduce the adverse impacts of traffic
and commercial activities on residential areas.

Even though this report stresses the need for tree planting along streets, it is only one factor that
contributes to a good streetscape.  For example, the design and alignment of the street can project
a feeling of safety and cleanliness, or well-designed buildings and associated parking can project
a feeling of continuity.  Secondly, a good streetscape means that the treatment of a street should
be appropriate to the uses associated with that street, e.g., a street leading to a city hall would
have a different design character than an industrial frontage road.  Lastly, the streets¶ character
depends upon not only trees, but also upon the use of street lighting, bus benches, sign, etc.

The development of a landscape plan is the first and most important step of a streetscape plan. 
Many of the other elements of streetscape are primarily designed for safety considerations  e.g.,
lighting, sidewalks, etc. , and are discussed in more detail in the Transportation Section of the
Sandy City Comprehensive Plan.


Development of the Streetscape Plan

A basic streetscape considers elements of topography, lighting, pedestrian and bicycle
circulation, vehicular circulation and landscaping treatment, and costs of implementation.  These
elements are analyzed in the context of the street setting.  Each setting presents a distinctly
different visual context because of adjacent architecture, the relationships between open spaces
and buildings, and the scale of the buildings.

To evaluate the setting of each of the streets and in order to develop a tree list for these, the

       1.      Primary Streets: These streets carry the heaviest traffic in the City and in many 
               cases intersect at major focal points and commercial centers.  Because of the 
               heavy use and traffic speed, these streets are widened to accommodate the 
               movement of traffic.  Landscaping along the streets and within medians would 
               aesthetically improve the appearance of the streets and bring them down to human
               scale.

       2.      Secondary Streets: These streets carry residential traffic from subdivisions to the 
               primary streets, they need to be emphasized as a contrast to residential streets.  
               Landscaping on these streets in some cases may be functionally more important 
               than primary streets.  The emphasis of these streets will make the major paths of 
               circulation much more distinct.

       3.      Subdivision Streets: There are more residential or subdivision streets in Sandy 
               City than any other street type.  The overall character of a neighborhood depends 
               greatly on the appearance of its streets.

Once the setting is review a tree list is developed and the type of planing scheme is determined.

Major Focal Points of the Streetscape Plan

The strategic locations of streets are at major intersections at the entrances to the City, and along
the major arterials.  The intersection is the key point of connection where one must make a
decision as to which direction to travel.  While doing this, the traveler heightens his attention to
surrounding details and perceives nearby elements more clearly.

The entrances to the City will influence how the traveler perceives the City.  Therefore,
landscaped medians and park strips, well-designed welcome signs, heavy landscaping and other
elements of visual attractiveness at the major intersections and entrances should be made to
present the best image to the residents of Sandy City.
