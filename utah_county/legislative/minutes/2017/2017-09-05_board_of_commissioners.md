(Track A, 22:50)
Bryce Armstrong of the Utah County Community Development Office summarized his department was asked to
create a solution to citizen concerns with commercial mining in West Mountain. The process includes sending the
proposed Utah County Land Use Ordinance and Utah County Zone Map amendments to the Utah County Planning
Commission.

(Track A, 27:45)
Commissioner Ivie commented on the importance of agricultural production in Utah, pointing out Utah County
leads the state in production and economy. There are unique micro-climates and vital natural resources in the
southern part of Utah County which need to be protected.

(Track A, 31:10)
Julie Smith of Clegg Properties came forward to read a letter from Todd Cusick, manager of CMC Rock (a copy of
which can be found attached to these minutes). The letter expressed concern over lack of adequate notice for the
potential Land Use Ordinance and Zone Map amendments, and the negative impact on property rights and financial
investments. Ms. Smith summarized she and Mr. Cusick are both requesting no zoning changes be made to the
specified property.

(Track A, 34:30)
Joan Landis mentioned she is cancelling high-grossing appointments with clients to share her thoughts at this
meeting. She is in favor of changing the zoning to align more closely with agriculture instead of mining. Ms.
Landis referenced ‘Silicon Slopes,’ a nickname for the Wasatch Front metropolis of information technology,
software development, and research firms in the fashion of California’s ‘Silicon Valley.’ Pointing to a painting in
the room of the former Geneva Steel mill for emphasis, Ms. Landis explained future development possibilities will
evaporate if West Mountain is destroyed for short-term mining gains.

(Track A, 38:35)
Jamie Evans, owner of Evans Grader & Paving, talked about a county task force which was formed ten years ago to
inspect gravel pits and their impact on local orchards. The concluding recommendation from the task force was that
no action was needed. Mr. Evans requested the Board review the minutes and results of those task force inspections.
He also pointed out the irony of new residents in the area insisting no other new residents should be allowed. Since
expansion is inevitable, and orchards and gravel pits are both prosperous in the area, a zoning change would only
serve to give a competitive advantage to state lands. Mr. Evans noted there have been no registered county
complaints against his company for twenty-five years.

(Track A, 42:45)
Denene Jensen is a Lake Shore resident who can recall when the landscape surrounding ‘Ironton Hill’ was bare due
to the heavy industrial use of the area; she does not want to see the same thing happen near her home. Ms. Jensen
explained a company promised to purchase land from a local resident, but redacted the terms and instead contracted
with the United States Bureau of Land Management (BLM) once a road had been built – this prevented the
neighbors from protesting the pit, and demonstrates unethical business practices.

(Track A, 44:55)
Benjamin resident Clark Karas is in favor of a zoning change to protect the community. Pointing out there are
currently six mines operating within a seven-mile radius, Mr. Karas mentioned the government owns too much of
Utah – state rights and county rights should constitutionally supersede any rights of the United States Bureau of
Land Management (BLM). He provided background on the Karas family centennial farm and questioned when
leasing of BLM property to private companies became more important than private landowners’ rights. Mr. Karas
also spoke about population growth in the southern part of Utah County, referencing earlier comments by Jamie
Evans to emphasize a lot can change in ten years and the impact of semi trucks will alter the breeding habits of
nearby wildlife.

(Track A, 51:00)
Chelsa Bringhurst is the Chair for South Utah County Community Voice. She explained the community has
garnered over 600 signatures on a petition for something to change; they do not want additional mining. Ms.
Bringhurst met with the Utah Division of Oil, Gas and Mining (DOGM), and several other state offices, which all
recommended the area is no longer appropriate for mining. The best way to address this is through zoning changes.

(Track A, 52:10)
Melvin Meredith has lived on the south end of West Mountain since 1957. He commented on Kiegley Quarry,
calling them the only legitimate gravel pit and a good neighbor. Mr. Meredith explained this is because another
mining company placed a fence around their property which results in recreational vehicle users going through his
property to access four-wheeling land – a good neighbor would not do such a thing. He summarized the second
mining company does not follow any rules.

(Track A, 55:00)
Rich Bowman is a newer resident to Payson, relocating from California because the picturesque wine country is
becoming more and more commercialized. There are beautiful people and clean air here, and Mr. Bowman is
opposed to anything that will negatively impact the air and water quality, or the quality of life in the area.

(Track A, 57:05)
Shauna Phelps also resides in the West Mountain area. Ms. Phelps explained she and her husband chose to move
specifically to West Mountain, Utah, over several sites in Arizona and Colorado because they prioritized agriculture
and water rights. Ms. Phelps talked about the benefits of higher elevation for her orchard property, and how
customers find the “you pick your own” aspect alluring because of the natural feel – dust from the mining pits
negate the experience, and is harmful to both people and the environment. She noted the trucks are a danger to
cyclists and pedestrians, and voiced concerns of mining waste being leached into the water and contaminating food
supply.

(Track A, 1:03:55)
Val Kofoed described his property as, “my backyard is in the backyard of the pit.” As the Vice Chair of South Utah
County Community Voice, he was also involved in the meetings with multiple state offices as mentioned by Chelsa
Bringhurst earlier. Mr. Kofoed thanked the commissioners for taking the time to address this issue and not shirking
responsibility. He stated Kilgore Companies has been operating for several years without a water permit. Mr.
Kofoed also noted the Kilgore pit was approved under an air of deceit, as the original intent was a small operation
that would close after some improvements to Interstate-15. He and the community feel threatened because of air
quality, traffic, and property values. The Utah Division of Oil, Gas and Mining (DOGM) has expressed the pit
would never have been approved if this outcome had been known. Mr. Kofoed summarized he understands the need
for gravel, but there needs to be planning and management.

(Track A, 1:10:20)
Kurt Sorensen’s main concerns are related to the impact of mining on local water, and the long-term viability for
fruit production. He noted West Mountain is one of the most beautiful places in the world. Mr. Sorensen said it
would be a big mistake to allow the landscape to be scarred, and asked the Board to do anything possible to protect
the agriculture and legacy of the area.

(Track A, 1:12:45)
West Mountain resident Alan Long is also worried about water contamination, as the pit is less than three miles
from Utah Lake. Mr. Long encouraged use of authority to limit chemicals, pointing out he had to wait one year
before building a property because of a septic issue but the waste from an industrial gravel pit is incomparable to the
waste from a small family. Noting many chemicals are needed to make asphalt, Mr. Long stated he would rather see
grazing in the area.

(Track A, 1:14:30)
Julie Sainsbury is a West Mountain resident and board member of South Utah County Community Voice. She
provided figures on the projected growth of the county, noting it has already doubled since 1990, and will double
again by the year 2050. Ms. Sainsbury encouraged foresight in planning around rural communities, explaining it is
up to the commissioners to adjust zoning so the number of gravel pits does not continue to multiply. Ms. Sainsbury
shared a story of a semi truck ignoring the school bus stop sign and almost hitting her son. There are many instances
of near-collisions, and the national rate of traffic fatalities on rural roads is almost thirteen times higher than
suburban areas. Ms. Sainsbury summarized the commissioners have a responsibility to keep the community safe.

(Track A, 1:23:50)
Nita Burch moved to West Mountain with nine of her young children almost twenty years ago. She spoke of the
historic significance of the area, including ancient Fremont Indian relics at risk because of the Kilgore Companies
pit located on the north side. Ms. Burch noted it is impossible to know how much history has already been
destroyed by Kilgore. She also addressed the danger to residents of Benjamin, Lake Shore, Palmyra, and West
Mountain due to dust pollution negatively affecting Utah Lake, irreparable damage to well water, and traffic
interference with school bus operations. Ms. Burch provided 11 photographs for the record (copies of which can be
found attached to these minutes).

(Track A, 1:26:40)
Living on 5600 West, the north/south highway on West Mountain, Tanya Macdonald sees dozens and dozens of
gravel trucks travelling daily. Ms. Macdonald is very concerned about the high likelihood of a child fatality along
the road involving a school bus stop. She agreed with earlier comments that property owners are more important
than Bureau of Land Management commercial leases; the Kilgore Companies operation has been non-compliant and
should not be allowed to continue expanding. Ms. Macdonald also spoke up on behalf of cyclists, reiterating the
dangerous traffic conditions. She does not want West Mountain to become ‘Point of the Mountain’ in Draper,
where the mining and excavation scars on the landscape are visible even from satellite imaging.

(Track A, 1:29:20)
Stewart Lamb works for Kilgore Companies and is also a Utah County resident in the Spanish Fork/Salem area. He
mentioned raising cattle and understanding agriculture, but also understanding inevitable expansion. Mr. Lamb

8.     ADOPT (OR DENY) AN ORDINANCE TO AMEND THE UTAH COUNTY GENERAL PLAN
       LAND USE MAP FROM AGRICULTURAL/WATERSHED TO RESIDENTIAL, AND TO AMEND
       THE OFFICIAL UTAH COUNTY ZONE MAP FROM THE MINING AND GRAZING (M&G-1)
       ZONE TO THE CRITICAL ENVIRONMENT (CE-2) ZONE FOR PROPERTY LOCATED IN
       SECTIONS 10, 11, 15, 16, 22, 27, 28, 32, 33, AND 34, T8S R1E, AND SECTIONS 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 14, 15,
       16, 22, AND 23, T9S R1E, WEST MOUNTAIN AREA OF UTAH COUNTY
(Track A, 2:31:00)
Bryce Armstrong of the Utah County Community Development Office summarized this proposed ordinance is the
result of an application submitted by South Utah County Community Voice, in an effort to protect public lands. The
Utah County Planning Commission has recommended denial of the ordinance due to concerns over residential
density, federal land boundaries, and other potential uses. Mr. Armstrong quoted Section 1-9 of the Utah County
Land Use Ordinance: ‘Exemption of Government-Owned Property’ –
          Properties and land owned by districts, municipalities, counties, the State of Utah, and the United
          States Government shall be subject to all of the provisions of this ordinance unless specifically
          exempted from a provision by Utah State or United States law. Moreover, any person or entity,
          other than the State of Utah or United States Government, which may obtain State or United
          States property by purchase, lease, or other arrangement shall use such property in accordance
          with the provisions of this ordinance, unless specifically exempted from a provision by Utah State
          or United States law.

Bryce Armstrong explained federal lands are regulated through his office, unless there is an overruling law, such as
in the case of campgrounds on Forest Service property or cell phone service towers at the top of West Mountain that
are on Bureau of Land Management lands. Except for not being included in the County General Plan,
Commissioner Graves asked if this ordinance meets all other requirements for adoption. Mr. Armstrong talked
about updates to the General Plan, and other factors such as natural hazards. He summarized there may be a better
solution to prevent earth extraction than designating the entire area as Critical Environment Zone. Commissioner
Graves warned that the anticipated increases in population may see citizens in this room in ten years coming from
the other side of the argument – that agriculture is taking up too much room, and the farms must be reduced to allow
additional housing. He commented on Orem City being turned from orchards to residents during his lifetime. The
commissioners discussed population growth and unintended consequences.
