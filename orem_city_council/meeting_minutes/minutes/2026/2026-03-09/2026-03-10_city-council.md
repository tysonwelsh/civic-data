HomeServeUSA Waterline Insurance Presentation Presenter: Steve Bieranowski,
HomeServeUSA Manager of Partner Success

Mr. Nelson introduced Mr. Baranowski. He explained the partnership that has existed
since 2015, aimed at educating residents on their specific utility responsibilities. Mr. Baranowski
explained that many homeowners are unaware that they are financially responsible for the water
and sewer lines running between their homes and the city’s main connections. This often leads to
"unfortunate conversations" at City Hall when unexpected line breaks occur. To mitigate this, the
program offers optional, low-cost coverage—currently $4.07 per month for water lines and $6.57
for sewer lines—with no deductibles, no service charges, and no annual coverage caps. This
includes full line replacements to meet federal guidelines, which is particularly relevant for

Orem’s aging infrastructure where pipes often reach their life expectancy after 20 to 30 years.

The program’s success in Orem is evidenced by a high 90% retention rate among the
2,500 active customers, which is significantly above the industry average of 75%. To date, the
program has completed over 1,200 claims in Orem, saving residents a total of $1.7 million. Mr.
Baranowski also introduced "HomeServe Cares," a charitable foundation that provides no-cost
emergency repairs for low-income residents who are not currently enrolled in the program. He
maintained that the partnership remains a high-value, no-cost asset to the city, as the company
handles all marketing and administrative expenses while maintaining a 4.82 out of 5 customer
satisfaction rating.

The council’s discussion focused on the educational value of the partnership and the
physical reality of Orem’s aging neighborhoods. Council members noted seeing "chain
reactions" of line failures in subdivisions built 40 to 50 years ago, confirming the need for such
protection. While the city last sent a direct mailer to residents in 2021, the council expressed
interest in revitalizing communication through social media and the city website
(orem.gov/serviceline). City officials reaffirmed their support for the program, noting that it
provides a proactive solution for residents and reduces the friction often caused when
homeowners mistakenly expect the city to pay for private-side repairs.

Mountainland Association of Governments (MAG) Presentation Presenter: Bob Allen and
Kevin Feldt, MAG Representatives

Mayor McCandless introduced the presentation by emphasizing the critical nature of
regional collaboration, noting that MAG serves as the primary forum for Orem to access federal
and state transportation funding. Mr. Allen explained that as an Association of Governments,
MAG focuses on three core areas: aging services, community and economic development, and
transportation planning. Specifically, because Utah County has an urban population exceeding
50,000, it is federally mandated to operate as a Metropolitan Planning Organization. This
designation allows MAG to manage the Regional Transportation Plan (RTP), a long-range
blueprint looking out to 2055, and the Transportation Improvement Program, which acts as a
five-year capital facilities plan where actual project funding is allocated. Allen clarified that for a
project to receive any state or federal money, it must first be included in the RTP, which in turn
relies on being part of Orem’s own Master Transportation Plan.

The technical breakdown of funding revealed a significant gap between massive
state-level projects and local city needs. While the Utah Department of Transportation (UDOT)
manages billions for major freeways and interchanges, MAG administers a smaller "missing
middle" pot of approximately $110 million every two years. This fund is designed for regionally
significant roads that are too expensive for a single city to fund but are not high-profile enough
for UDOT’s primary budget. Recent and upcoming Orem projects mentioned as examples of this
partnership include the Lakeview Parkway, the new roundabout at 1200 South and 400 West, and
the planned widening of Center Street between I-15 and Geneva Road. Mr. Feldt noted that these
plans must remain "fiscally constrained," meaning the cost of proposed projects cannot exceed
the projected tax revenues for the 2055 horizon.

The City Council raised concerns about increasing traffic congestion stemming from
neighboring growth, specifically citing the Provo Canyon commute and the impact of the new
Lindon Temple on Orem's 400 West. Mr. Allen acknowledged that transportation is a "long
game," and because funding is often committed five to six years in advance, early identification
of these "fires" is essential to avoid playing catch-up. The discussion also pivoted to emerging

and the potential for autonomous vehicles to increase road capacity. MAG officials expressed
their intent to facilitate a "Regional Growth Forum" where Orem and its neighbors can create
unified regulations for micromobility and air mobility. The presentation concluded with a
reminder that MAG staff meets monthly with Orem’s engineering team to ensure the city’s
infrastructure remains a priority within the regional hierarchy.

Water Reuse and Nutrient Removal Facility Ribbon Cutting Recap Presenter: Chris
Tschirki, Public Works Director and Reed Price, Assistant Public Works Director

Mr. Tschirki and Mr. Price presented the culmination of a vision 25 years in the making,
celebrating a monumental ribbon-cutting ceremony held earlier that day. The facility serves a
dual purpose: treating wastewater for high-quality irrigation and removing harmful phosphorus
to protect the health of Utah Lake. Historically, Orem’s phosphorus discharge ranged from 1.5 to
4 milligrams per liter, but new state regulations effective January 1, 2026, mandate a drop to 1.0
mg/L or lower. The new facility not only meets this requirement but uses advanced filtering
technology that can be scaled down to 0.1 mg/L if future regulations tighten. This initiative is a
regional success, as the plant treats 100% of Lindon’s wastewater and serves portions of
Vineyard, with current plans for expansion in partnership with the Central Utah Water
Conservancy District.

On the water reuse side, the facility allows the city to "use water twice," saving millions
of gallons of precious culinary drinking water annually. The treated water is classified as "Type
1" reuse water, undergoing a rigorous double-disinfection process involving both UV light and
chlorine injection. This recycled water will be used to irrigate the Sleepy Ridge Golf Course and
Lakeside Sports Park, with Spring Water Park slated for future connection. Officials highlighted
that this water is "drought-proof" because wastewater remains a constant resource, and it acts as
a natural fertilizer by retaining beneficial nitrogen and phosphorus that would otherwise have to
be manually added to city parks.

Financially, the project overcame significant hurdles after the COVID-19 pandemic
caused costs to nearly double from an initial $10 million estimate to a final total of $21 million.
To bridge this $11 million gap, the city secured $13.5 million in outside funding, including a $7
million ARPA grant from the Utah County Commission, a $3 million federal appropriation via
Senator Curtis, and various grants from the Utah State Legislature and the Governor’s Office of
Economic Opportunity. The remaining $7.5 million was funded through bonding to be repaid by
utility users. Council members expressed appreciation for the "forward-thinking" generations of

Employee Appreciation Day Presentation Presenter: Amy Peterson, HR Manager

Ms. Peterson opened the presentation by reflecting on the previous Friday's national
celebration, using the opportunity to publicly recognize the "incredible people" who maintain

work occurs behind the scenes during early mornings and late nights, often escaping public
notice despite its critical impact on the community's mission. Ms. Peterson noted that Orem’s
internal culture is frequently described by staff members coming from other organizations as

recent cancer diagnosis. She described an "overwhelming" outpouring of support from the
"Orem family," which included flowers, gift baskets, and dinners brought to her family by city
firefighters, proving that the dedication of Orem employees extends far beyond their official job
descriptions.

Mayor McCandless and the City Council offered their own tributes to the municipal
workforce. Mayor McCandless expressed her profound gratitude for the "amazing environment"
created by the staff and offered her personal support for Ms.Peterson’s ongoing treatments.
Councilmember Gale, reflecting on her two years in office, remarked that she is continually
struck by the excellence of the city staff, noting that the city moves so seamlessly that residents
often don't realize how much effort is being exerted on their behalf. Councilmember Killpack
concluded the remarks by characterizing the city's employees as a "blessing" to the community,
stating that the strength of Orem’s collection of families is built directly upon the culture of
service and power demonstrated by those who work for the city.

tools like licensing, inspections, and parking standards to manage "bad actors." She contrasted
the well-maintained, quiet short-term rentals in her neighborhood with several neglected

Tauni Merrill supported short-term rental regulation, emphasizing that these properties
generate a 14% tax revenue for the city and state while providing essential quiet housing for
families and medical patients. She suggested that rather than a ban, the council should implement
standards such as mandatory host training, residency requirements for owners, and strict guest
limits. Ms. Merrill concluded that these proactive measures would ensure rentals remain a
beneficial, well-managed community asset.

Kris Hammond urged the City Council to adopt objective regulations rather than a ban,
noting that peer cities like Midway have successfully balanced property rights with occupancy
and parking standards. She challenged the claim that short-term rentals deplete housing
availability. Expressing concern over increasingly divisive local dialogue, Ms. Hammond called
on the Council to reject "prejudice" and preserve Orem’s "Family City, USA" spirit by allowing
diverse housing options to coexist respectfully.

Jeff Gaufin urged the City Council to convert the decommissioned North Union Canal
into a paved trail similar to the Murdock Canal. He noted that state and federal grants could
cover up to 80% of costs if the project is added to the Regional Transportation Plan via MAG.
Gaufin requested that the city immediately initiate feasibility talks with MAG and UDOT to
secure a spot in the current statewide funding pipeline.

Dean York advocated for professional standards and occupancy limits rather than a ban

high-stress family events.

parking at the City Center Park farmers market by encouraging bicycle commuting. Mr. Golding
offered his full support to help the city navigate the hurdles of creating this community
connection.

Bennett Cookson warned that the decommissioned North Union Canal will become a

Planning Commission Appointment - Jeff Reeves
Councilmember Millett emphasized the importance of broad geographic representation

on the Planning Commission. She argued that because the commission's zoning and land-use
decisions affect every neighborhood, the body should reflect Orem's diverse geography rather
than having multiple members from the same few blocks. Ms. Millett noted that drawing from
different parts of the city ensures all residents feel their specific neighborhood priorities are
represented, ultimately strengthening the credibility of the commission's recommendations.
Councilmember Muhlestein expressed concern over the "unusual situation" of replacing
five Planning Commission members simultaneously. She specifically questioned the decision to

experience is critical for the commission's effectiveness and expressed her support for the Chair,
who faces the challenge of leading a largely inexperienced body. Ms. Millett suggested that
retaining seasoned members during this transition would have provided necessary stability.
