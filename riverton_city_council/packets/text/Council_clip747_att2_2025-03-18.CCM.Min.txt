38          Elected Officials Compensation
39
40   Kevin Hicks, City Manager, explained that while the City code currently ties elected officials' pay
41   to the mid-grade of a Grade 20 position, this structure has resulted in a significant increase this
42   year—just under 10%—based on benchmarking comparisons with other cities. He
43   recommended that the pay adjustment structure be adjusted so that elected officials receive the
44   same annual percentage increase as City staff through the budgetary process.
45
46   Mayor Staggs provided background on the 2019 code change that tied the mayoral salary to a
47   percentage of the City Manager's salary and Councilmembers' salaries to 15% of that amount.
48   The intent at the time was to recognize the significant time commitment required for the Mayor’s
49   role and to provide some level of compensation for Councilmembers, particularly given that
50   participation on various boards often goes unpaid. He expressed appreciation for the previous
51   decision and the current recommendation to remove the salary tie to Grade 20, instead aligning
     Page | 2 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     March 18, 2025



 1   adjustments with the annual merit and cost-of-living increases (COLA) provided to City
 2   employees.
 3
 4   Councilmember Haymond expressed support for aligning elected officials’ pay adjustments with
 5   those of City employees, stating that doing so would prevent the issue from becoming a
 6   recurring political debate. He emphasized that keeping compensation at a reasonable level
 7   ensures that individuals pursue elected positions out of a desire to serve the community rather
 8   than financial gain.
 9
10   Councilmember McCay supported adjusting elected officials’ pay in line with City staff but
11   expressed concern about the disparity between the Mayor’s salary and the Council’s
12   compensation. She pointed out that the Mayor currently earns 95% of the average salary for
13   similar positions, while the Council earns 122%. Potential solutions included reducing Council
14   pay, freezing it for a year, or allowing only the Mayor’s salary to increase this year while shifting
15   all future adjustments to match staff increases.
16
17   Councilmember Buroker agreed with adjusting elected officials’ pay in line with City staff and
18   supported holding the Council’s salary for at least a year to allow other cities’ salaries to catch
19   up. She emphasized the importance of ensuring the policy is clearly defined to avoid repeating
20   the discussion in the future.
21
22   Councilmember McDougal expressed support for aligning elected officials’ pay adjustments with
23   City staff and emphasized the importance of consistency, particularly since some officials also
24   receive healthcare benefits. He supported holding the Council’s salary for a year to bring it more
25   in line while allowing the Mayor’s salary adjustment to proceed.
26
27   Mr. Hicks asked each Councilmember to again nod if they were in support of this proposal.
28   Councilmembers McCay, Buroker, Haymond and McDougal all agreed.

30            Traffic Calming and Congestion
31
32   Cary Necaise, Public Works Director, discussed the effectiveness of recently installed speed
33   dips in two subdivisions. A study conducted before and after installation showed minimal impact
34   on reducing speeds, with only a one-mile-per-hour decrease. The cost of installing each dip was
35   around $9,000, and correcting them would involve chiseling out the concrete and repaving.
36
37   Mr. Necaise discussed traffic access issues on 12600 South, particularly concerns about
38   residents exiting neighborhoods like Quail Lane near the Holiday gas station. The City’s traffic
39   committee reviewed the issue, and the City Engineer noted that UDOT is conducting an access
40   management study for the area, spanning from Bangerter Highway to Herriman. The study aims
41   to evaluate alternative routes, potential safety improvements, and possible median installations.
42
43   One proposed solution involved painting "Keep Clear" zones at key intersections to prevent
44   vehicles from blocking exits. However, implementing these zones could shift congestion to other
45   areas, particularly near Bangerter and Mountain View Corridor, potentially disrupting traffic light
46   timing.

 9            Property Tax Education
10
11   Nick Geer, Administrative Services Director, provided an educational overview of property
12   taxes, explaining that they serve as a stable revenue source for public services such as
13   education and public safety. He outlined the three key components of property tax: the entity’s
14   budget, the taxable value of properties, and the tax rate, which is determined by dividing the
15   budget by the taxable value. Riverton does not have a general property tax but has two taxing
16   entities—the Riverton Law Enforcement Service Area (RLESA) and the Riverton Fire Service
17   Area (RFSA)—each receiving about 11% of property tax revenue.
18
19   Mr. Geer addressed common misconceptions about property tax increases, explaining that the
20   certified tax rate is determined annually by the county auditor based on prior-year tax revenue,
21   adjusted for new growth. He demonstrated how property tax amounts remain stable despite
22   property value fluctuations and that revenue only increases through new growth or a Truth in
23   Taxation process. A five-year trend showed Riverton’s property tax budget growing from $5.8
24   million to $6.5 million due to new growth, while the tax rate declined as property values rose.
