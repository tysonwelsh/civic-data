Discussion Items

       Business License Code

Ryan Carter, City Attorney, explained that the changes to the business license ordinance focus
on modernizing the application process without making substantive policy changes. The
revisions aim to streamline procedures, require online applications, and close loopholes—such
as businesses applying under alternate “Doing Business As” (DBA)—to ensure more accurate
information is collected. He emphasized this is a practical update intended to improve efficiency
within the business license division.

Mr. Carter highlighted that much of the current update involves removing outdated and unused
portions of the business license fee schedule by deferring entirely to the annually adopted City
fee The alcohol licensing section has been cleaned up by removing duplicative references to
state law, which posed a risk if state laws changed. The City will now simply require proof of
state compliance for off-premise and restaurant liquor licenses, which are the only two
categories being emphasized. Provisions the City never had the resources to enforce, such as

          Animal Code

Mr. Carter explained that the proposed changes to the Animal Control code address
longstanding enforcement challenges and modernize procedures to improve public safety. A
key issue was overlapping administrative processes and unclear authority when animals
attacked people or were involved in rabies-related impounds. These situations were
complicated by legal ambiguities, especially regarding the seizure and holding of animals
pending trial. To resolve this, the updated code simplifies seizure authority by explicitly allowing
animal control officers to seize animals involved in attacks and, if owners do not cooperate, to
apply for a search warrant.

Another significant update clarifies that animal owners are liable when their animals attack or
are at large, even if someone else had custody of the animal at the time. The definition of
“animal under restraint” was also revised. Previously, vague language allowed owners to argue
that radio shock collars qualified as restraint. The revised definition eliminates this loophole,
requiring physical leashes or containment. Radio collars are no longer accepted, as they are
often unreliable and create public perception and safety issues. This policy change is supported
by Animal Control staff and the Police Chief.

Mayor Staggs asked for clarification regarding whether animal control officers have discretion in
impounding a dog after an attack, referencing a previous Council decision that may have
mandated impoundment after a single incident.

Mr. Carter responded by citing the current language, which states that an animal that bites or
attacks a person or another animal may be seized by the department without a court order. If
the animal control officer is unable to obtain the animal voluntarily, they shall pursue a search
warrant. He explained that recent complications in a municipal court case—such as confusion
over notice to the owner and requirements for written orders—led the City to shift its approach.
The City decided to standardize the process by using search warrants for seizure, which is a
routine legal mechanism already used for other types of evidence.

Mr. Carter emphasized that the City has the capacity to care for animals while a case is pending
and stated that allowing the return of an animal that has already attacked could present a public
safety risk. The decision to keep the code language as “may” rather than “shall” was to allow
discretion in cases where the injury was minor or not immediately apparent. He indicated that
discretion should depend on the severity of the incident and the determination of the animal
control officer. The primary duties of animal control include verifying rabies vaccinations and
determining if there is an ongoing threat.

Councilmember Pierucci reported on his role as a trustee for the Jordan Valley Water
Conservancy District (“District”), which is considering a 9.3% property tax increase as part of its
budget discussion. He expressed concerns about the increase, particularly regarding the
funding of the District’s conservation garden. He noted that while the garden serves residents, it
primarily benefits those living nearby, creating a park-like atmosphere funded by the entire

provided by the District. The analysis revealed that the public benefit, which is difficult to capture
through fees or rates, amounted to $20 million. In comparison, the District generates $30 million
in property tax revenue, leaving a $10 million discrepancy that was harder to justify from a
public benefit perspective.

Public Hearing(s)/Action Items

          Ordinance No. 25-08 – Amending Section 18.90.010 (Table of Commercial Users)
          clarifying allowed commercial uses, Amendments proposed by Riverton City

Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, discussed updates to the table of
commercial uses, which aimed to clarify and better define certain categories without significantly
changing permitted uses. Key updates included defining assisted living facilities, nursing homes,
and residential care facilities as conditional uses in all commercial zones; creating a new
category for building trades (such as electricians, plumbers, and HVAC) to address recent
questions, with restrictions on vehicle and equipment storage; explicitly including dance studios,
gyms, and martial arts studios; and clarifying the definition of bus terminals to differentiate them
from bus stops. These changes were recommended for approval by the Planning Commission.

Discussion/Action Items

          Ordinance No. 25-10 – Amending Riverton City Code Title 6, Chapter 5 to Revise
          Administrative Provisions of the Animal Control Ordinance, Amendments
          Proposed by Riverton City

Mayor Staggs allowed citizen comment on this discussion/action item:

Jill Walker raised concerns about the proposed animal control ordinance, particularly the
provision allowing someone to kill a dog if they reasonably feel threatened, noting the inclusion
of barking, growling, or baring teeth as justification and questioning why Riverton’s code would
expand beyond what Herriman and South Jordan allow. She also noted that Riverton’s three-
day hold period is shorter than South Jordan and Herriman's five-day policy and argued that five
days is more reasonable for owners to locate lost pets. Lastly, she requested clarity on who is
held criminally responsible when a dog is loose—the person who let the dog out or the
registered owner—and suggested that "dog at large" should be treated as an infraction, in line
with 2022 Utah legislation.

Shauna Walker expressed strong concerns about the proposed changes to the code,
particularly regarding provisions that allow someone to kill a dog if they feel threatened. She
cited the Fifth Amendment and the right to due process, arguing that allowing a person to kill a
dog based on subjective fear—such as barking, growling, or baring teeth—effectively makes
them judge, jury, and executioner, and violates constitutional protections.

Councilmember Pierucci voiced concern about the proposed language in the animal control
ordinance that would allow someone to kill a dog if they feel threatened. While he acknowledged
the intent—to permit self-defense during an actual attack—he felt the current wording may go
too far, particularly by including behaviors like repeated barking, growling, or baring teeth as
justification. He suggested revising the language to narrow the scope strictly to situations
involving protection of life.

Mr. Carter clarified that the proposed ordinance change includes a reasonableness standard,
which would prevent misuse of the provision allowing someone to kill a dog if they feel
threatened. He explained that simply barking behind a fence would not legally justify lethal
action, as a reasonable person would not perceive that as an imminent threat. He emphasized
that the ordinance aims to address those rare but serious circumstances and aligns with
broader legal standards for self-defense. He stressed that this ordinance does not significantly
change legal protections but provides clarity for cases where immediate defensive action might
be necessary.

Councilmember McCay expressed appreciation for residents who shared their concerns and
echoed those concerns regarding the proposed language allowing for the killing of a dog under
certain circumstances. She also questioned the current three-day hold for impounded animals,
suggesting it be extended to five days to align with neighboring cities and provide owners a fair
chance to recover their pets, particularly given the emotional attachment many have to their
animals.

Councilmember McDougal argued in favor of the proposed ordinance language, emphasizing
that it is a protection for individuals to act in self-defense before an attack occurs. He stressed
that no person, including children, should have to wait to be harmed before defending
themselves, and likened the provision to existing self-defense laws for human threats. He
clarified that the ordinance does not authorize someone to harm a dog simply for barking behind
a fence.

Councilmember Buroker shared a personal experience of being bitten by a dog, expressing
empathy for how frightening that can be. She emphasized that it is the City's responsibility to
ensure residents feel safe when using public sidewalks and trails. She concluded that if the
ordinance helps achieve that goal of protecting the public, then she supports it.

Councilmember Haymond echoed previous comments, stating that most people are generally
reasonable and that provoking a dog behind a fence would clearly show unreasonable behavior,
with the dog essentially acting in self-defense. He noted that although the ordinance allows any
person to kill a dog under certain circumstances, in reality, most people aren't carrying weapons
or tools that would enable them to do so. He expressed comfort with the ordinance as written,
believing it properly allows individuals to defend themselves or others if truly necessary.
Councilmember McDougal MOVED that the City Council adopt Ordinance No. 25-10 –
Amending Riverton City Code Title 6, Chapter 5 to Revise Administrative Provisions of
the Animal Control Ordinance. Councilmember Haymond SECONDED the motion. Mayor
Staggs called for discussion on the motion. Mayor Staggs called for a roll-call vote. The vote
was as follows: Buroker-yes, Haymond-yes, McCay-no, McDougal-yes and Pierucci-no. The
motion passed with a 3 to 2 vote.
