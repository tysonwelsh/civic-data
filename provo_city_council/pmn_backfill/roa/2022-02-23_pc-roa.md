APPLICANT RESPONSE
Key points addressed in the applicant's presentation to the Planning Commission included the following:
   • Ray Bernier gave some background and intent information to the Planning Commission for the proposed project
       and answered questions in regard to the parking lot and pedestrian access, building details, and bicycle parking.

PLANNING COMMISSION DISCUSSION
Key points discussed by the Planning Commission included the following:
   • Lisa Jensen asked the applicant to explain what superior design was being used in the parking lot landscaping to
       meet Section 15.20.020(2)(b). She also asked about the footprint of the proposed building in comparison to the
       existing HFAC.
   • Brian Henrie suggested the use of permeable pavers in the parking lot and asked about the bicycle parking.
   • Daniel Gonzales stated his concern for vehicle parking in relation to the music building and law building.
   • Dave Anderson asked for an update on getting a UVX stop on 900 East.
   • There was a lot of discussion on parking lot trees, soils, and shade coverage. There was some concern of the trees
       getting the necessary air and water to grow to anticipated heights. It was acknowledged that the proposed plantings
       for the trees was somewhat of an experiment so the Commission wanted to have assurance that the lot would get
       to 50% shade coverage. They suggested adding three additional trees in the south rows to make sure code
       standards are met.

FRONTAGE ZONE
The frontage zone describes the area between the building and the property line. The frontage zone provides a space for
sidewalk cafes, temporary signage, small planters, and a space where casual window shoppers may stop and gaze
without inhibiting the pedestrian through traffic.
Spatial Standard: The frontage zone is typically a minimum of two feet, but can be extended in width up to the
   maximum setback allowed by City Code.

PEDESTRIAN ZONE
Acting as the primary accessible sidewalk, the pedestrian zone provides a clear pathway adequate for pedestrian traffic
to pass through unobstructed from the other adjacent activities occurring nearby. The activities and installations in the
frontage and amenity zones should not encroach on this area.
Spatial Standard: A minimum of six feet should be maintained in all instances in the Downtown area. On streets
   identified as main pedestrian corridors (Univ Ave, Center St, 100 W 500 W, 300 S and 600 S), a width of eight feet or
   greater should be considered.

AMENITY ZONE
The amenity zone is critical in defining the pedestrian character of an urban street. This zone includes street trees and
other landscaping in appropriately designed planters, as well as interspersed street furnishings such as street lighting
and bike racks.
Spatial Standard: Five feet is a typical width for this zone, however, special circumstances may require a smaller width to
   ensure adequate pedestrian zone is maintained in tight corridors. Priority should be given to the pedestrian zone in
   this case.

BUFFER ZONE
Serving as a buffer between pedestrian-oriented areas and vehicular traffic, the buffer zone provides a flexible area to
be used for supporting items such as on-street parking, outdoor dining, and landscaped bulb-outs. These enhancements
can calm traffic and offer support for businesses through character defining elements at the street.
