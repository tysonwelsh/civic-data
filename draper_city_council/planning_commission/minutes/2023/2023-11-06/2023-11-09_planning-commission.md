       A.      Discussion Item: Town Center Station Area Plan.
               An Overview of the Station Area Plan Requirements from the State of Utah and the
               Process Taken So Far on the Town Center Station Area Plan. The Actual Plan will be
               Reviewed at a Future Meeting Date. Staff Contact is Jennifer Jastremsky,
               jennifer.jastremsky@draperutah.gov, (801) 576-6328.

Ms. Jastremsky reported that the above discussion will count toward the Planning Commission
Training. She commented on Station Area Plans and stated that state law changed in 2022. Over the
past year, the City has been working on a Station Area Plan for the Town Center area. In 2022, the
State Legislature passed HB-462 for cities with Fixed Guideway Transit Stations such as FrontRunner
and TRAX to do some master planning to be called a Station Area Plan. The goal is to allow transit-
oriented development next to these stations and allow cities to run the planning process. It is in lieu
of discussions the Legislature was having about mandating uses and densities next to stations. A

November 9, 2023

Station Area Plan is considered part of the General Plan, which will need to be amended to reference
the Station Area Plans. The deadline for the adoption of Station Area Plans is December 31, 2025,
for all stations. Draper City has three stations consisting of the FrontRunner Station and two TRAX
stations. There is also a station in Sandy on 11400 South. Half the radius for the Station Area Plan
is in Draper City, so they will have to plan for that area as well. This year they have been working
on the Town Center with the intent being to do one every year to meet the deadline. Once a Station
Area Plan is adopted, it is sent to the Wasatch Front Regional Council (“WFRC”) for certification. It
was clarified that there will be three separate Station Area Plans.

Ms. Jastremsky reported that once the WFRC has certified the plan, it must be reported to the
Department of Workforce Services. The plans require the City to look at all properties within a one-
half-mile radius of the station, which is the station platform. The four specific goals and objectives
that Station Area Plans have to meet include the following:

       1.      The availability and affordability of housing;

       2.      Sustainable environmental conditions;

       3.      Access to opportunities, transportation choices, and connections; and

       4.      Must have a vision and a map.

There must be a public engagement portion that identifies constraints and opportunities within the
area. There must also be a five-year Implementation Plan. If the area is already fully developed, the
City must analyze the uses. In the Town Center, several residential neighborhoods fall within a one-
half-mile radius. The City does not see those neighborhoods redeveloping.

Once a plan is adopted, the Zoning Code must be updated to allow for the development to take place.
It is a multi-step process for each Station Area Plan. A map was displayed to show the different
station locations. State law allows Station Area Plans to be combined if there is an overlap when they
are close.

FrontRunner is unique compared to the other two stations. Half of the property is within the City’s
Transit Station District and has Master Area Plans already in place. The intent in that case will be to
make those Master Area Plans more cohesive. For the Town Center, a grant was received from the
WFRC right before COVID-19 hit. Ultimately, it was used toward the Station Area Plan. The process
began in August of 2022. There have been two public open houses and they expect to have a third to
review the preferred concept. Online surveys have also been conducted. The Stakeholder Committee
was established to look at existing conditions and create multiple concepts. The concepts presented
at the public open houses were being reviewed to get feedback from the public. The Project Team
and Stakeholder Committee have been working to create a preferred plan that will be presented to the
Planning Commission and City Council. Another open house was also to be held to get public
feedback.

       B.      Discussion Item: 2023 Annual Planning Commission Training.
               State Required Annual Planning Commission Training.

City Attorney, Mike Barker, conducted the training and discussed a recent Court of Appeals Case that
touched on interesting legal issues the Planning Commission should be aware of. City Attorney,
Spencer DuShane gave a PowerPoint presentation and reviewed the facts of the case. Staff’s job is
to prepare the Staff Report and make recommendations. The County Council’s job is to make findings
of fact and support the conclusion based on what is in Staff Report and the evidence presented. The
job of the District Court is to review the County Council’s decision and determine whether it is
arbitrary, capricious, or illegal. The job of the Court of Appeals is to review the District Court’s
application of the law to the facts.

The 6.35-acre property is in Summit County in the Snyderville Basin and on a ridgeline. The lot was
subdivided and existed prior to the portion of the Summit County Code referred to as the Snyderville
Code. The application was for an accessory building. The home is large and would require a
significant amount of disturbance. The objective of the Snyderville Code was to make sure that
buildings do not protrude onto ridgelines that are visible from public roads. There is an exception to
this rule for lots that existed prior to the passage of the Snyderville Code. The law existed and the
property was subdivided but not built on prior to the passage of the Snyderville Code. For lots greater
than five acres, the site grading cannot exceed a disturbance area of 20,000 square feet. If the
applicant wants to exceed the 20,000-square-foot disturbance area, they must have approval with
special findings. Due to the specifics of the site, a special exception would apply.

It was noted that in order to build the home, a variance was needed from the Board of Adjustment.
The applicants came in a few years later wanting to build a 7,000-square-foot accessory building with
a site disturbance of 9,785 square feet. Special exception issues were discussed. DuShane and Barker
reviewed the Code and stated that the intent was to maintain the mountain character of the area with
suburban development and not eliminate the unique character of the Snyderville Basin. Reasons for
special exceptions were discussed. They could include possible omissions, unique situations, or
deviations. Support was expressed for special exceptions as long as the parameters for approval are
enumerated in the Code. Concern was expressed that the standards seem to be very subjective. The
comment was made that subjective decisions are easier to contest because there can be claims of
discrimination or bias.

Chair Adams stated that he has watched the trend of deviations, which can be an indication that the
Code is outdated or does not apply. Trends tend to reflect what the citizens want and the current
building standards. Deviations are important to watch. The comment was made that sometimes there
is a blind spot in the law that if applied, could result in something that is not desired.

Returning to the Snyderville Code discussion, Mr. DuShane commented that if there is another way
to allow the lots to develop that does not deter health, safety, and welfare and that can be done
equitably under another provision, it should be done that way. It was noted that limiting the special

exceptions will not result in a large number of homes protruding from the ridgeline in a mountain
environment. That is in keeping with the policy statement mentioned earlier. In this case, the first
home needed to disturb more than 43,000 square feet. 20,000 square feet was the amount that they

The Staff Report concluded, and the County Council agreed, that the 20,000 square-foot rule applied
on a per lot basis. Since the applicant had already disturbed 100,000 square feet, all of the surface
grading permitted by the Code had been utilized. Because they made this finding, they determined
to analyze the special exception of the Code. The counterargument from the applicant’s attorney was
that it is not a per lot basis rule that applies but rather a per project rule that applies. Various nuances
of the language were discussed.

Some of the findings in the Staff Report were referenced. One was that the proposed application did
not protect the scenic qualities of the Snyderville Basin. Another was that granting a special exception
would not be consistent with the policies and goals of the General Plan or the intent of the applicable
Codes that govern the Snyderville Basin. In the case described, the applicant did not provide evidence
showing why it was equitable and describing the unique circumstances. The applicant also knew that
the unique qualities of the lot would preclude him from building the second building because he had
already developed the first. He was on notice that he could not build it. The matter next went to the
District Court. The applicant’s attorney argued, and the District Court agreed, that the County
analyzed the issue using the wrong criteria. The matter was then remanded back to the County
Council who held another hearing. No new arguments were heard. The County Council again denied
the application using the previous Staff Reports and records of the hearings conducted including a
Reconsideration Hearing to support their finding that the Code criteria are not met. The applicant
appealed again and the District Court supported the ruling of the County Council.

The Appellate Court found that there is a presumption that land use decisions of a land use authority
on appeal are valid. That presumption is rebuttable if the appellant can show that the decision was
arbitrary, capricious, or illegal. The applicant’s attorney claimed that site grading is not defined and,
therefore, cannot be enforced. The applicant’s attorney argued that the site grading provision applied
on a per-project basis. If correct, he is entitled to approval. The Court found that the site grading was
not defined in the ordinance but not that the lack of a defined term made the decision illegal.
Mr. DuShane reported that Courts should define terms first by the terms that are set forth in the
ordinance. The arbitrary and capricious standard was discussed as well as the matter having been
remanded. It was noted that all four criteria must be met in order to grant a special exception. The
Council relied on the record they had up to that point and the court held that it was enough evidence
to support a reasonable conclusion by the Council that the request did not qualify for special exception
under the standards in the Snyderville Code.

Mr. DuShane commented that to approve something, especially if there are a lot of people present to
speak out against it, they analyze whether it meets the Code. He explained that an applicant is entitled
