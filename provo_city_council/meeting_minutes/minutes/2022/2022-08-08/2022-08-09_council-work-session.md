vehicles last the most miles and years, while electric or hybrid vehicles last about 7 years before
the first battery replacement. Mr. Eyo outlined further data bout fueling and charging time.

5.      A presentation regarding the "Safe Streets For All" grant opportunity-
        Background and Discussion. (22-065) (0:52:07)

Dave Decker, Public Works Director, and Rob Hunter, Engineer, presented. Mr. Decker wanted
to clarify some of the intentions of bringing this item to the Council. There are some specific
qualifications for the grant application that require Council action. The biggest concern as Public
Works Director, that he wanted to make sure the Council understood, is that they will be
referring to the Transportation Master Plan (TMP), but the matter at hand was simply this
appendix to the plan that is a component of their grant application. Mr. Hunter continued. The
Safe Streets For All (SS4A) grant is a program that has come out of the bipartisan infrastructure
law passed in Washington. The grant will award $5 billion dollars over 5 years ($1 billion each
year), with a 20% local match required for regional and local governments that receive the
grants. There are two tracks for the grant application and Public Works is submitting a project
grant application. Grants will be awarded in amounts of $5 million to $30 million, so Public
Works is looking at large-scale projects which would meet that minimum requirement and best
meet the priorities of this grant funding, which places a focus on street safety issues. Mr. Hunter
outlined a timeline of the grant application process and Public Works’ internal review process.

indicated the plan did not have an existing component that would meet the grant eligibility
requirement of a Safety Action Plan, although there is a chapter in it on safety which identifies

6.      A presentation on Community Development Block Grants (CDBG) programming
        and processes. (22-038) (1:49:12)

Melissa McNalley, Community Grant Administrator, presented. Their division oversees 2 grants
from HUD: HOME Investment partnerships grant and CDBG, which they will present today.
She played a short video on how CDBG works. Provo is an entitlement city, which means Provo
can set its own goals and receives funds from HUD every year. In 2020, that was the last time
Provo did a consolidated plan. Throughout the presentation, Councilors asked questions and
made clarifications about the content. Councilor George Handley asked Ms. McNalley to clarify
what they meant by “decent housing.” She indicated it was any housing that was not substandard
and met HUD criteria (i.e., no code violations, have working appliances, sufficient rooms).
Suitable living environments have more to do with recreation opportunities in the area.

Funding priorities in the 2020 Consolidated Plan were determined by the Council and citizen
participation: housing, public services, public facilities, and economic development (with a list
of specific sub-goals for each). Ms. McNalley highlighted project examples for housing projects
that have been carried out over the last two years. A lot of these were conducted with HOME
dollars, and some with CDBG. Public services receive 15% of the total CDBG entitlement.
Economic development project examples include façade grants (which have been extended
beyond the downtown area), assistance to small businesses, and 108 loans. Public facilities
projects include adding infrastructure or improvements to local resources like the Alpine House
and Children’s Justice Center, or other non-profits providing community services. Ms. McNalley
showed a breakdown of the project accomplishments through Year 2 of the Consolidated Plan.
This breakdown highlights job creation and direct financial assistance for economic
development; homeownership assistance, code enforcement, and residential rehab for housing;
populations served with improvements to public facilities and services; and for public services
includes a breakdown of people served and income levels for families helped.

Ms. McNalley reviewed the details of CDBG funding and the process for how it is allocated. The
program income is comprised of funds the City receives back from projects that had received
CDBG funds as loans. HUD allows cities to keep that funding so they can use it again. The
entitlement from HUD is entered as a reimbursement to the General Fund for dollars paid out to
the various projects. Right now, they have about $3.5 million committed to current projects with
about 3 years to spend these promised funds. In August and September, they reach out to
community partners, stakeholders, and the Council about the funding priorities for the upcoming
year. Then a notice of available funding is released with details about the priorities. Councilor
David Shipley recalled that this process has not been collaborative with the Council in the past;
he just recalled hearing about what the committee decided (perhaps in line with the previously
adopted Comprehensive Plan). Keith Morey, Development Services Assistant Director, said that

Once applicant presentations are made, the committee grades projects on established criteria,
including available funding, how successful the project will be, funding already in place, and
whether projects meet national objectives and Provo’s goals. The CDBG committee then makes
a formal recommendation based on the scoring results and this recommendation is presented to
the Council. The Council will hold two public hearings and then make a final decision. In May,
HUD releases the final entitlement amounts and staff submit an annual action plan to HUD as
well as a Comprehensive Annual Performance and Evaluation Report (CAPER) mid-year to
report on how the projects performed. Ms. McNalley indicated the Council could send her any
questions they had regarding the CDBG process, HOME, the committees, and scoring sheets.

Ms. McNalley wanted to identify the Council’s funding priorities for the next year. The Council
was welcome to observe the committee meetings, however their input about specific projects
would need to wait until after receiving a formal recommendation from the committee, after

program. Several Councilors commented and thanked Ms. McNalley for explaining the CDBG
program so clearly and providing valuable insight on the process. Mr. Morey said they were
excited about the change in the program management and had received feedback from external
agencies and the Council that the process is improving.

Mr. Shipley asked how funding is distributed geographically within the eligible cities. Ms.

Restrooms near Playground at Memorial Park:
Most restrooms are on the CIP schedule, but this park does not have a restroom. The playground

Sidewalk repair work:
Gordon Haight and Dave Michelsen, Engineers, addressed this item. The City budgets about
$500,000 on an annual basis to address sidewalks in the city. Mr. Shipley asked how far behind
that put them in terms of sidewalk replacement throughout the City. Mr. Haight explained that
specific projects in specific areas of the City often would require complete sidewalk replacement
for a neighborhood area, which could be upwards of $5 million per project. They are currently
working with a group from the State to do a sidewalk survey of the City including trip hazards
and other issues. At the current rate, they probably reconstruct about one mile of sidewalk per
year. Based on the list of current complaints, they estimate they are about $5.5-6 million short,
which would take about 10 years to complete at the current funding rate. Mr. Handley asked
about CDBG funding; Mr. Haight explained they have some CDBG-funded projects occurring
now, including replacing ADA ramps and a pedestrian bridge at 100 West.

Ms. Whipple was excited about the sidewalk survey. She asked if there was a priority level or
scale for addressing different complaints and issues; for instance, if a resident had an
accessibility issue whether that is addressed more quickly. Mr. Haight explained that because of
the requirements of the ADA, those are addressed at a higher priority level. That type of
information is useful when residents are submitting sidewalk issues. Several Councilors were
interested in revisiting the conversation after the survey is completed in order to make a long-
term plan to address the sidewalk backlog.
