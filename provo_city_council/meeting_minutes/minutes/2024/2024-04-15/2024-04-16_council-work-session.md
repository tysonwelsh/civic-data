1. An ordinance amending Provo City Code regarding certain trades and
encumbrances of Real Property (24-036)

Tara Riddle, Ombudsman/Property Manager, presented the ordinance proposal, explaining her
responsibility for managing all city-owned properties, including leases and easements. She
emphasized that property management is governed by city and state codes, with a Utah Supreme
Court ruling mandating that property disposition be handled administratively rather than
legislatively. Consequently, the council is required to pass an ordinance defining how municipal
property is bought, sold, traded, encumbered, or otherwise transferred.

Ms. Riddle clarified that the term "encumbered" has created challenges for staff in maintaining
accurate records of city property. To address this, she proposed a process for managing
encumbrances, trades, or transfers, such as easements, rights-of-way, leases, licenses, and
boundary line adjustments. The ordinance specifies that transactions exceeding $50,000 require
council approval, while those below this threshold may be approved at the mayor's discretion.

Councilor Garrett asked about the rationale for the $50,000 threshold. Ms. Riddle acknowledged
that it replaced a $15,000 limit and was chosen arbitrarily, suggesting it could be revisited. City
Attorney Brian Jones added that thresholds in similar ordinances are often adjusted over time to
maintain relevance. Councilor Bogdin expressed interest in an automatic adjustment mechanism,
but Mr. Jones cautioned that such systems can lead to misalignment if not monitored. Councilor
Handley, supported by Councilors MacKay and Whipple, emphasized that automatic adjustments
to an arbitrary figure might not be prudent.

4. A discussion regarding rental dwelling licenses & related fees (24-027)

Michael Sanders, Policy Analyst, presented on rental dwelling license fees, explaining that
without a fee study, the base fee could be set at $60, and the multiple unit fee structure could
reach $65.50. These figures exclude costs related to code enforcement inspections. He
emphasized that increasing inspection frequency would not cover the new full-time employee
salaries due to restrictions in UCA 10-8-85.5(2)(c), which prohibits municipalities from charging
fees for rental dwelling inspections.

Mr. Sanders explained that Provo requires inspections for every license issuance, with renewal
every three years or upon complaint. He compared practices in other cities: Orem inspects only
on a complaint basis, St. George follows similar regulations as Provo, and Salt Lake City
requires licensees to certify code compliance, inspecting a percentage of units based on their
total number. Provo, however, currently inspects every unit.

Councilor Bogdin inquired about this comprehensive approach. Bill Peperone, Development
Services Director, explained it provides greater thoroughness. Brian Jones, City Attorney,
clarified that while inspection fees cannot be charged, a certification fee for compliance review
may be permissible, potentially reducing city subsidies.

Laramie Gonzales, Customer Operations Business Manager, outlined three options for
addressing the fee structure:

    1. Adjust the Base Fee: Set the fee at $65.50 for immediate revenue without additional
       overhead.
    2. Conduct a Fee Study: Engage Zions Bancorp for a comprehensive study to align fees
       with management costs and avoid frequent adjustments. This would cost $20,000 and is
       recommended by staff.
    3. Hybrid Option: Increase the fee immediately to $65.50, with further adjustments based
       on the study. This could lead to citizen confusion due to multiple changes.

Vice-Chair Whipple questioned the return on investment for the fee study, and Ms. Gonzales
confirmed it would recoup costs within a year. Chair MacKay estimated there are at least 2,000
single-family rental dwelling licenses issued annually. Questions about funding were raised, and
Mr. Borget indicated the general fund would cover the appropriation.

Motion:

Councilor Handley motioned to proceed with Option 2, the fee study. Vice-Chair Whipple
seconded, and the motion passed unanimously, 6-0 (Councilor Christensen absent).

5. A discussion regarding an ordinance amending Provo City Code regarding
landscape design standards (PLOTA20230206 / PLOTA20240071)

Hannah Salzl, Planner/Sustainability Coordinator, presented a proposed amendment to the
landscape design standards in Provo City Code. The amendment would treat artificial turf like
other non-vegetative ground covers, with regulations supported by the Development Services,
Parks, Public Works, and Energy Departments. The key provisions include:

       Applicability limited to yards adjacent to streets and parkstrips/parkways.
       Exclusions for backyards, non-street-side yards, and rooftop terraces.
       Continued allowance for the Reasonable Accommodation process for exemptions related
        to disabilities.

Concerns with Artificial Turf:

       Requires water (albeit less than turf grass).
       Absorbs and radiates heat, creating hazards for children and pets.
       Detrimental to insects and wildlife.
       Causes increased stress and water loss for nearby vegetation.
       Releases microplastics into the stormwater system.
       Reduces stormwater permeability if improperly installed or maintained.

Ms. Salzl also showed specific code changes proposed by the amendment.

Council Discussion:

       Councilor Handley asked about residents with existing artificial turf. Ms. Salzl clarified
        they would be grandfathered in, except for cases where entire front lawns are covered,
        which is allowed under current code.
       Councilor Hoban commented on the perceived water-saving benefits of artificial turf but
        noted that this is not a determining factor in the proposal.
       Chair MacKay emphasized the negative environmental impacts, such as microplastics
        and PFAS chemicals entering aquifers, arguing against artificial turf's benefits.
       Councilor Bogdin pointed out the chemicals used in traditional lawn care as a
        counterpoint, adding that artificial turf is often chosen for aesthetic reasons.

Ms. Salzl highlighted the 60% compromise, which allows for part of the yard to include non-
vegetative ground cover. Discussions followed on whether the alternative of rock landscaping
might pose its own challenges.

       Councilor Garrett confirmed that the code does not apply to city parks or stadiums.
       Vice-Chair Whipple raised concerns about waste generated by artificial turf and
        expressed a preference for deferring to department expertise, which strongly opposed its
        use.

11. Presentation on Tiered Water Rates Recommendations (24-045) 02:12:54

Keith Larson, Consultant, presented recommendations for adjusting Provo’s water rates,
advocating for a transition from the current seasonal rates to a tiered rate structure. He presented
data comparing Provo’s rates with other cities, showing that Provo’s tiers are fewer and less
steep. He explained that tiered rates aim to promote water conservation and ensure alignment
with the cost of service.

Challenges Identified:

       Ensuring revenue stability.
       Addressing non-residential water use variability.
       Considering residential lot size differences.

Proposed Three-Tier Structure:

       Tier 1: Covers most indoor water use.
       Tier 2: Accounts for moderate outdoor use, covering up to the 80th percentile of users.
       Tier 3: Applies to the top 20% of users with extreme outdoor use.

Mr. Larson discussed the limited elasticity of water demand, noting that low costs and its
essential nature mean price changes have minimal effect on usage patterns. He highlighted how
tiered rates align costs with consumption and detailed a plan to bring Provo’s water system to
financial sustainability by 2033, targeting the lower end of cost projections. Without changes,
Provo could face significant annual shortfalls, potentially requiring steep rate increases in future
years.



Provo City Municipal Council Work Meeting – April 16, 2024                                Page 8 of 13
Council Discussion:

       Chair MacKay raised concerns about the fairness of the proposed tiers for larger
        households, which may use more indoor water despite minimal outdoor usage. Mr.
        Larson responded that even large families are unlikely to reach Tier 3, as outdoor use
        typically accounts for 70% of overall consumption. He acknowledged that creating
        variable first-tier thresholds based on household size, as done in some California
        communities, is impractical to implement.
       Vice-Chair Whipple expressed strong support for tiered rates, emphasizing that they
        place greater costs on high water users, particularly for outdoor use, and provide financial
        relief for basic indoor needs. She noted this could benefit Provo’s renters, who often do
        not control outdoor water use.
       Councilor Hoban inquired about expected behavioral and structural changes. Mr. Larson
        explained these could include reducing watering frequency, shifting watering to
        nighttime, or replacing lawns with alternative landscaping like desertscape.

Mr. Larson presented alternatives for rate adjustments:

       Alternative 1: A 3% increase.
       Alternative 2: A 5% increase.
       Alternative 3: A 15% increase. He recommended Alternative 3 to meet financial goals
        while maintaining service quality.

The council discussed fairness, with Councilor Bogdin pointing out that Provo might fall to the
bottom of rate comparisons as other cities raise their rates. Public Works Director Gordon
Haight added that infrastructure must handle peak summer demands, and behavior changes
during summer could reduce strain.

Motions:

    1. Motion 1:
       Councilor Handley motioned to support transitioning from seasonal rates to a tiered rate
       system. Vice-Chair Whipple seconded.
       Vote: Passed 4-3, with Councilors Bogdin, Christensen, and MacKay opposed.
    2. Motion 2:
       Councilor Handley motioned to explore rate increases between 11-15%, equating to a 4-
       7% increase for the average user. Vice-Chair Whipple seconded.
       Vote: Passed 4-3, with Councilors MacKay, Christensen, and Hoban opposed.
    3. Motion 3:
       Chair MacKay motioned to include revenue increase scenarios of 5% and 0% for
