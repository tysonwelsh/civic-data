1.     Planning Commission Training by the Utah Land Use Institute.

Deputy Community Development Director, Sean Lewis, introduced Craig Call and Wilf
Sommerkorn from the Utah Land Use Institute who were in attendance. He noted that the State of
Utah requires Planning Commissioners to receive four hours of training on land use and Mr. Call
and Mr. Sommerkorn provide the necessary training. A book entitled Ground Rules was provided
to the Commission Members.

Mr. Call conducted the training and reported that the Utah Land Use Institute was created about 23
years ago. They offer training and have publications on their website. They also offer conferences
and seminars throughout the year. There is continuing education credit for Planning Commissioners,
attorneys, planners, real estate agents, and appraisers. He recently published a book called Ground
Rules: Your Handbook to Utah Land Use Regulation, which has spurred interest in the seminars.

The of of the Property Rights Ombudsman provided funding for the training program from the 1%
surcharge on all Building Permits in the State of Utah. Programs of the Utah Land Use Institute are
funded, in part, by generous grants from the S. J. and Jessie Quinney Foundation. They are also
sponsored by the League of Cities and Towns (“ULCT”), the Utah Chapter of the American Planning
Association, the Salt Lake Board of Realtors, the Home Builders of Utah, and the Real Property
Section of the Utah State Bar.

Mr. Call explained that as the population of the United States grew, the original settlers, primarily
from England, adopted a representative democracy. Citizens would sit on a council and make
decisions regarding land use. As situations became more controversial and complex, it became
necessary to create limits and rules based on the U.S. Constitution as well as State Constitutions.
The representatives are bound by State Statutes, Federal Statutes, Case Law, and Local Codes and
Ordinances. Although some of this information may be elemental, it is critical to understand.

Mr. Sommerkorn reported that land use involves property owners and their desire to do what they
wish with their property. While property owners have the right to mostly do what they want on their
property, there are limitations to those rights. Local Planning Commissions and City Councils
monitor, advise and regulate land use according to the City Codes and Ordinances that have been
adopted. Additionally, neighbors and community members get involved in making decisions by
expressing their opinions and concerns. City staff is in place to ensure that all entities understand
the rules and what is allowed. They help bring all parties together in the best possible way.

Mr. Call explained that it is important to distinguish between Legislative and Administrative Acts.
He described the roles of each, which entities are involved in making decisions, and if they require
a public hearing. In response to a question raised, Mr. Call stated that there is a lot of flexibility,
and the State Code specifies that the Legislative Body cannot make a final decision without the
advice of the Planning Commission. They can, however, discuss an item before it goes before the
Planning Commission because of the flexibility. This would be done in an ordinance.

Ms. Bateman commented that there have been some administrative actions that the legislative body
did not know about. Mr. Sommerkorn stated that the legislative body has no role in administrative
matters. Mr. Call agreed that that is generally true but there is nothing in the State Code that would
prohibit more structure that would allow more communication in these cases.

Mr. Call stated that if an Administrative Act is challenged, the court is instructed by the State Statute
to decide whether the decision is arbitrary, capricious, or illegal. However, the legislative side states
that if an ordinance can promote the general welfare, is reasonably debatable, or is in the interest of
the general welfare, it will be upheld. This means that they have the broadest amount of discretion
that the law allows. It is not appropriate for public policy to be set by anyone other than the
legislative body. As a result, the courts give leeway to legislative bodies to do what they deem
reasonable. He cited a recent article in The Salt Lake Tribune regarding political signs and when
they are allowed.

Mr. Call shared a story regarding a land use zone change in a neighborhood in the valley where a
developer wanted to build a grocery store in a Commercial Residential zone. The Planning

Commission recommended approval and the City Council held a public hearing where the neighbors
expressed great concern over the grocery store. The City Council ultimately had a split vote and
denied the request. The developer then went to the Court of Appeals and explained that their
application lines up with the City Plan and claimed that the Council’s decision was unfounded. He
read the court’s decision, which said that there has been no Utah case where a zoning classification
was reversed because it was unfounded. Ultimately, public comment can be a determining factor in
a legislative decision.

Mr. Sommerkorn added that the General Plan called for this area to be a Commercial Zone. That
does not mean that the City has to adhere to the plan. General Plans in the State of Utah are advisory
only and the legislative body is not obligated to follow that plan. He explained that there may be
other factors, like timing, which come into play as the City makes decisions on growth and
development. Mr. Call further explained that in this case, the developer came back with a different
plan that was ultimately accepted by the City. It is ultimately within the City’s discretion to approve
or disapprove land use applications and it will almost always be upheld by the courts.

Mr. Call shared another story about an investment company, Smith Investment, which owned
property in Sandy that they planned to develop. In the meantime, Sandy City rezoned the property
from Commercial to Residential. When Smith Investment took the city to court, the court
determined that the City has the right and flexibility to change its zoning as they see fit. Mr. Call
explained that the examples he has presented are legislative issues, and the definition of a non-
conforming use would be an administrative issue.

Mr. Call shared another situation involving design standards in Park City in Summit County. He
showed a picture of the Home Depot that is there and how it differs in design from a typical Home
Depot store. Mr. Sommerkorn explained that some communities feel that it is important for
developments to have a specific look or appearance so they add design standards to reflect that to
their ordinances. As long as the design allows for reasonable use of the land and development, the
standards are permissible and can differ from city to city. As long as the development goes through
the appropriate process, they are legal and not based on a specific project. These are general policy
rules and are not usually specific to a single development. Many times, these policies are created to
preserve historic and landmark districts.

A question was raised regarding Homeowner’s Associations. Mr. Call stated that they are not subject
to the same rules and are a completely different entity.

Mr. Call cited another example in Summit County where there was a legislative act to change the
zoning in an area to allow for high-density housing. The State Legislature has allowed local
governments to make these types of policy changes at their discretion. Legislative Acts are
recommended by the Planning Commission and decided on by the City Council or the legislative
body. They cannot take action until the Planning Commission has had a chance to comment. The
standard is if the ordinance can promote the general welfare or it is reasonably debatable that it is in
the general welfare, the court will uphold it.

Mr. Call cited an example in Saratoga Springs and showed a picture of the city in relation to Utah
Lake. He identified a 75-acre parcel that is surrounded by a golf course with beautiful homes on
large lots. A developer came in and proposed a townhome project on the property. The City allowed
the zone to change to a higher density to allow for the townhome project. The neighbors were
opposed to it and filed a referendum with the City that then went to the Utah State Supreme Court.
The Supreme Court indicated that it would go back to be decided by the voters. The voters
overturned the decision and disallowed the townhome project. The State Constitution specifies that
the decision by the City Council – a legislative body – can be reversed, changed, altered, or initiated
by another legislative body within the City limits, which was the voters in this case.

The City of Holladay approved development on the Cottonwood Mall site for several hundred
residences, some of which were single-family, office space, apartments, retail, and mixed-use. The
zoning on the property was mixed-use. The surrounding residents asked that it o be put on the ballot.
The City and the developers argued that it was an administrative act because they were following
the mixed-use zoning. The court determined that the site plan looked like a zoning map and was so
complex that they termed it a legislative act and sent it to the voters. Mr. Call explained that voters
can reverse decisions of the City Council and even bypass the Council and adopt ordinances by vote.

Mr. Sommerkorn reported that people think that if they do not like a land use decision they can just
take it to court. The courts do not override legislative decisions. The best way to challenge those
types of decisions is to get the legislative body to change its mind, vote them out with an election,
or gather signatures and get the issue on a ballot so it will be put to a vote. Administrative decisions
are not subject to the same rules. The courts are protective of the citizens’ rights and will usually
rule in their favor.

Commissioner Dahlberg asked about the ability to change laws after they have been put in place.
There was discussion regarding the process and examples were given of how that can and does
happen. She inquired about the Cottonwood Mall Project and if that was moving forward with a
redesign.

Mr. Call suggested that when making zone changes if cities do so without a specific project in mind,
then when a project comes forward, it will be administrative in nature. Mr. Sommerkorn stated that
that was what the City of Holladay did but they were so vague that it required legislative action to
define it.

Mr. Call described the difference between legislative acts (creating the law) and administrative acts
(deciding if an application complies with the law). He explained that an administrative land use
decision will be upheld if it is supported by substantial evidence on the record and is otherwise legal.
A determination should be made as to whether it meets the criteria in the ordinance and if it is legal.
When making administrative decisions, Findings of Fact and Conclusions of Law are presented to
support the decisions. The land use decision of a land use authority is an administrative act even if
the land use authority is the legislative body. This can be complicated when there is a separation of
powers like in South Salt Lake with an independent mayor. In these cases, it is more likely that the
legislative body is only involved in legislative acts.

Mr. Sommerkorn explained that legislative actions involve the Planning Commission and the City
Council. The Planning Commission makes the recommendation, and the Council makes the ultimate
decision. In administrative actions, the State Code says that the decisions are made by the Land Use
Authority. The Land Use Authority can be the Planning Commission, Staff members, or another
body that is created specifically for that purpose. It can also be the City Council. The difference is
that the Land Use Authority makes the decisions, and that will be discussed further in the meeting.

Mr. Call referenced a law that is unique to Utah called Vested Rights. It states that an applicant is
entitled to approval of a land use application if the application conforms to the requirements of the
applicable land use regulations, land use decisions, and development standards in effect when the
applicant submits a complete application and pays applicable fees. At that point, the laws are locked
in and cannot be changed. It simply means that the policy discussions need to happen before the
application is presented.

Mr. Sommerkorn described the exceptions that apply to the Vested Rule. The first states that if the
Land Use Authority finds that a compelling, countervailing public interest would be jeopardized by
approving the application, they can deny the vested right. He cited an example of a subdivision built
on a hillside where some of the homes started to slide. If another lot owner wanted to begin
construction, the Land Use Authority could deny the application because it would be ultimately
detrimental to the property owner. The second exception is sometimes referred to as a moratorium,
but that is not the correct term. It is called the Pending Ordinance Rule and states that if City officials
have previously decided to change the rules of an ordinance and have taken appropriate action by
noticing it and having it on an agenda of a public meeting, there is a six-month time period that the
City has to decide if they do indeed want to change the rule. In a case such as this, an application
that would be subject to the proposed rule change must wait until the six months have passed or the
rule has been finalized before moving forward.

Mr. Call stated that once there is a vested application, the administrative decision is whether legally
and factually the application complies. If it does, it should be approved. If it does not, it should be
denied. In either case, an explanation is necessary on the record. Whatever decision is made must
be supported by evidence in the record. With a typical zoning ordinance, there are permitted uses,
prohibited uses, and conditional uses. Conditional Uses are permitted, but the City gets the
opportunity to put conditions on them to deal with what the Code says is reasonably anticipated
detrimental effects of the use. They should be approved if reasonable conditions are proposed or
can be imposed to mitigate the reasonably anticipated detrimental effects of the proposed use in
accordance with applicable standards. Those detrimental effects should be identified and determined
if they can be mitigated, and if so, it would be decided what conditions to impose.

Mr. Call cited an example in West Jordan where Dannon Yogurt built a beautiful campus. Next
door there is a construction storage yard with heavy equipment and leftover construction materials.
It is unsightly and neither Dannon nor the City liked it. In the meantime, an application came to the
City for another outdoor storage yard on the remaining vacant land around Dannon. Dannon was
strongly opposed to the proposed storage yard and both the Planning Commission and the City
Council denied the application. It was noted that construction yards in the zone are conditional uses.
The matter was appealed to the Court of Appeals.

The City entered the Findings of Fact for the basis of denial, which included the fact that the
attributes of the area that attracted Dannon in the first place needed to be maintained. They stated
that the outdoor storage area is detrimental to the area, making it less attractive and injurious to the
goals of the City. Outdoor storage is detrimental to the existing and future businesses in the area
and is not harmonious with the goals of the City. The construction company won the appeal because
the reasons stated were based on legislative decisions and this was an administrative decision. The
ordinance already allows for outdoor storage and the City did not show that the outdoor storage
would pose a nuisance. If Dannon had a problem with the Code they should have asked for a zone
change or an amendment to the zone to disallow outdoor construction storage. Administrative
decisions must determine whether, legally and factually, the application complies. The reasoning
needs to be on the record.

Mr. Sommerkorn described the meaning of the term, ‘Substantial Evidence.’ He stated that when
making administrative land use decisions, it is necessary to look at the evidence to determine if it
complies with the Code requirements. The basis or standard is Substantial Evidence. It just means
that there is some evidence that the requirements are being met. It does not have to be much but
enough to be reasonably adequate to support a decision. Mr. Call added that substantial evidence
should be relevant, independent, and provided by an expert or be otherwise credible.

He shared a story about a situation in Moab, Utah regarding Short-Term Rentals as a Conditional
Use. An application came in for a property within the zone for a Conditional Use Permit and it was
denied by the Planning Commission. The case went to the Utah Supreme Court which concluded
that the Planning Commission’s refusal to furnish written findings, or at least a basis for their
decision, tended to suggest that there was no rational basis for the Planning Commission’s decision.
He stressed the importance of including substantial evidence for land use decisions on the record.

Mr. Call shared a case in Davis County regarding two houses on Main Street. The proposed use
would be an alcohol treatment/rehabilitation center to be located across from a middle school. Mr.
Sommerkorn was the City Planner for Davis County in Clearfield City at the time. Davis County
Mental Health wanted to use the two homes for an alcohol rehabilitation center and group home,
which was allowed as a conditional use in Clearfield City.

The neighbors strongly opposed the use, and one comment that was repeated several times pertained
to the increase in crime in neighborhoods that house these types of facilities. The Police Chief was
at the meeting and the Chair asked him if that was the case. The Chief stated that when the facility
is well managed and well operated, the crime rate does not go up in the neighborhood. The Planning
Commission ultimately denied the application, stating as one of the reasons behind increased crime.
Another reason cited was that the neighbors did not want it. As it went forward to appeal, the court
stated that the Planning Commission is required to have a rational basis for the decisions and actions,
and there was no rational basis in that case. They relied on public clamor. The courts have indicated
that public clamor is not a rational basis for denying an application. Mr. Call commented that if the
Planning Commission had backed up its decision with viable evidence, the decision would have
been upheld.

Commissioner Pechmann asked where the burden of proof lies. Mr. Call stated that the burden is
on the developer or applicant to meet the requirements in the ordinance. If the Land Use Authority
asks questions and wants evidence, there is an opportunity to provide more information. Citizens
can bring in evidence countering the proposal, which would be considered at the time.

Commissioner Holscher asked if all cities have a City Attorney to advise the Planning Commission
on what they can and cannot do. Mr. Call responded that he does not have any stories about cities
that have attorneys. They can be very helpful in making certain decisions. Elected officials can still
go against the recommendations of the attorney, but then it usually goes to appeal. Mr. Call stated
that they would distribute Certificates of Completion at the end of the training for those present who
would need them for continuing education.

Mr. Call next addressed interpretation of an ordinance. He reported that a land use authority shall
apply the plain language of land use regulations. If it is unclear, the land use authority shall interpret
and apply the land use regulation to favor the land use application. Sometimes when writing the
Code, the intent does not come through clearly in the language.

Mr. Sommerkorn cited an example where a property owner wanted to be annexed into a city but
some of the neighbors did not. Because of the way the Code was written, the property owners who
did not want to be annexed won the case, even though the intent was different. Mr. Call addressed
non-conforming uses and explained that when it first came into effect, it was legal and in
conformance with the ordinance. It is no longer legal under the current ordinance but if it has been
maintained continuously, it is grandfathered into a legal use. He showed a picture of a yard where
an auto shop was operated. The City determined that since they were no longer selling auto parts, it
should be shut down. However, the ordinance includes selling parts, storing vehicles, and
dismantling vehicles. Because the ordinance says and instead of or, the property owner won the
case.

Mr. Call cited another situation regarding short-term rentals in Sandy. In this case, the City was
unable to show in the ordinance that a short-term rental was not allowed. As a result, the property
owner won. When writing ordinances, it is important to ensure that the intent is clear.

Commissioner Carter asked if there is a significant difference between the words should and shall.
Mr. Call explained a case where the City used the word shall, meaning that it is required when the
intent was should or even may. In this particular case, citizens who complained about the infraction
were not harmed, so the case was dismissed. He stressed that wording is critically important in Code
language.

Mr. Sommerkorn added that the rights of property owners are important and should be carefully
considered. Cities have rights based on what is in the Code but even then they have limits on rules
they can impose on property owners. Whenever a reasonable interpretation can be made it is
typically decided in favor of the property owner.

Mr. Call explained that someone with standing is someone who is an adversely affected party other
than the applicant who owns real property adjoining property that is the subject of a land use

application or decision or will suffer damage or an injury different from the general community as a
result of the land use decision. He shared a case that occurred in Salt Lake County where the County
allowed the development of a shopping center despite the concerns of a property owner who had a
house in the middle of the development. The property owner had standing and followed all the
appropriate actions while the County allowed the developer to continue with construction.
Ultimately, the property owner won the case because the County had violated its own ordinances.

Mr. Call stated reiterated that an administrative land use decision will be upheld if it is supported by
substantial evidence in the record and is otherwise legal. There are the Bill of Rights, Federal
Statutes, State Statutes, Local Ordinances, Court Rulings from the past, and the Building and Fire
Codes that all need to be considered and complied with.

Conditions on Development state that a development exaction may only be imposed on a land use
application if:

       1.      An essential link exists between legitimate governmental interest and each exaction;
               and

       2.      Each exaction is roughly proportionate, in both nature and extent, to the impact of
               the proposed development.

Mr. Call explained that the following questions should be considered:

       1.      Do we have the authority to impose this requirement?

       2.      Does it address some burden imposed on the community by the development?

       3.      Is the burden imposed on the development roughly equivalent to the burden the
               development imposes on the community?

He shared a story about a property owner who wanted to build a beach house on their beach-front
property. The Coastal Commission wanted to impose the condition that people have access to the
beach through their property. The family took the matter to the Supreme Court which ruled that the
Coastal Commission had no authority to impose the condition. It also interfered with the property
right to prohibit others from coming onto a person’s property. He explained that the first test of a
legal exaction is if it advances a legitimate state interest within the authority of the entity to impose.
The second issue is if it is proportionate.

Mr. Call explained that the difference between a condition and an exaction is that an exaction
requires the dedication of land, the building of public improvements, or the payment of money. A
condition is a requirement placed on an applicant to make the use more compatible with the
surrounding uses. He then cited an example of an exaction imposed by Salt Lake County on a
developer and how that turned out in court.

If an application is denied and the applicant has completed an application, paid the applicable fee,
and demonstrated that the application complies with the ordinance legally and factually, the
applicant can appeal the Land Use Authority’s decision to the Appeal Authority, or ask the Appeal
Authority for a variance. If it is denied, the applicant can then appeal the Authority's decision to the
District Court.

Mr. Sommerkorn explained that an Appeal Authority is required by State Code. The Appeal
Authority can be an individual but one who has experience in land use and possibly some legal
background because appeals are similar to court hearings. Appeal Authorities can be asked to
interpret an ordinance or decide on variances. Having an Appeal Authority is required and it is
recommended that they have experience so that they can be defensible as possible.

Mr. Call reported that with appeals people tend to either not understand the importance of the timing
or are given incorrect information. Appeals must be filed within 10 days (or the length of time
specified in the Code) of the ruling. It is critical to recognize that the purpose of the Legislature
setting deadlines is to have predictability and finality in land use. The goal is to create finality in
decisions, whether that is 10 days or 30 days depending on what the City has determined. There are
a few exceptions for those who are unaware of a violation. The 10 days begin as soon as they should
have known.

Mr. Sommerkorn described variances and when they are necessary. Variances can be granted if:

       1.      There is an unnecessary hardship;

       2.      There are special circumstances attached to the property;

       3.      There is a substantial property right;

       4.      Is consistent with the public interest;

       5.      The spirit of the ordinance is observed and substantial justice is done.

All five conditions must be met to approve a variance.

Ms. Bateman asked if there are fees associated with variances. Mr. Sommerkorn stated that the cities
typically charge a fee similar to an application fee. It covers staff time to review the request and the
time it takes to make a determination.

Mr. Call shared a story about a landmark building where the owners were requesting a variance to
convert it to a restaurant. It fell within the ordinance guidelines except that a 10-foot-wide landscape
strip needed to be installed around all sides of the building. In this case, the building was almost
directly on the north property line, which could not be landscaped. The west side was the proposed
trash area. The request was for a variance to allow for two of the four sides to be landscaped. The
City granted the variance but after nearby residents sued, the court asked the City to go back and
cite the five requirements so that it was in the record.

The Rights of Due Process of Law include:

        1.     Notice;

        2.     To be heard and present evidence;

        3.     To respond to the evidence presented by others; and

        4.     Impartial decision maker.

The City must ensure that these rights are protected.

Mr. Call reported that State Code does not require a public hearing on administrative issues. There
may be times when it would be easier to not hold a public hearing when discussing administrative
items. However, when a hearing is held, it is important to treat the public with respect and allow
people to be heard.

Mr. Sommerkorn explained that the reason the Code does not require a public hearing for
administrative issues is that they are to be decided by elected officials. Mr. Call stated that there are
resources that the City can use when necessary. The ULCT maintains the Land Use Academy of
Utah and there are short videos and presentations there that can be viewed that will cover the last
hour of training required. There are also videos on UtahLandUse.org which is the website of
Messrs. Call and Sommerkorn. The Utah Land Use Library houses a topical review of land use
regulations and detailed summaries of law on specific topics. Mr. Sommerkorn curates a BLOG that
is accessible on the website as well.
