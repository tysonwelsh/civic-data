Item 1: An ordinance amending the zone map classification of real property, generally
located at 1050 West 100 North from the one-family residential (R1.6) zone to the very low
density residential (VLDR) zone. Dixon neighborhood. (PLRZ20250332) 00:01:06

Jessica Dahneke, City Planner, presented this item to the Council. She explained that the
property at 1050 West 100 North is currently zoned R1.6, which allows for accessory dwelling
units. The property owner wishes to subdivide the property to add an additional dwelling unit. If
rezoned to VLDR, the property would still be surrounded by R1.6 zoning.

Ms. Dahneke noted that the applicant would need to upload an updated plat as a condominium
plat, meaning each home would be its own parcel with the remainder of the area designated as
common area shared between the two homes. She explained that the Planning Commission
recommended denial of the request due to concerns about maintenance of common areas, owner
occupancy, and the creation of a spot zone surrounded by R1.6 zoning.

Item 2: An ordinance amending the zone map classification of real property, generally
located at 2525 West 620 North, from the R1.8 (one family residential) zone to the A1.1
(agricultural) zone. Lakeview South neighborhood. (PLRZ20250348) 00:03:42

Nancy Robison, Planner, presented this item. She noted that after the staff report was written, it
was discovered that the property lines had changed by seven feet, as the property is part of a trust
where land is being split. The acreage sizes in the Council’s memo and Exhibit A reflect the
correct legal definition and zone size.

Ms. Robison explained that the entire parcel is currently 9.61 acres and will be divided into two
parcels: one of 3.66 acres and another of 5.95 acres. She displayed a map showing how the
property would be split, with a portion currently zoned R1.8 and another portion zoned
agricultural. The applicant wishes to ensure that one parcel is entirely within the same zone.

It was clarified that the property owner plans to move his property into the agricultural zone,
where there is already a house. The remainder of the property will go to siblings. Councilor
MacKay noted that this was a rare instance of land being rezoned to agricultural for residential
purposes.

Ms. Robison addressed a question about the lower parcel’s development potential, explaining
that while it cannot be developed as it currently stands without another subdivision proposal,
there are plans for future development. She pointed out that there are three stub roads in
preparation for future development.

The Council discussed the unusual nature of rezoning to agricultural and the family
circumstances driving this request. No further questions were raised, and it was noted that this
item would be revisited in the evening session.

Item 3: A discussion regarding the "Owner occupant" definition in Provo City Code
Chapter 14.06.020 (25-087) 00:07:54

Melia Dayley, Policy Analyst, presented this item. She explained that the discussion arose from a
current accessory dwelling unit (ADU) application for a property on Bannock Drive. The
Planning Commission recommended approval based on a clause in the definition of owner
occupancy, which staff had interpreted differently.

Ms. Dayley outlined that the current definition includes a clause that could be interpreted as a
loophole or exemption to the owner-occupancy requirement. To address this, the Council Office

reside on the property to be considered an owner-occupant.

Brian Jones, City Attorney, provided additional legal context. He emphasized that the current

Item 4: A presentation regarding the FrontRunner 2X Project (25-088) 00:35:43

Brian Allen, Transit Project Director from the Utah Department of Transportation (UDOT),
presented information about the FrontRunner 2X project. He explained that the project aims to
double-track strategic sections of the FrontRunner line to increase capacity and frequency of
service.

Mr. Allen outlined the benefits of the project, including increased ridership, reduced congestion,
and improved travel times compared to Interstate 15. He presented a video explaining the
concept of double-tracking and why it is necessary to increase train frequency.

The presentation focused on the section south of Provo, which involves complex right-of-way
acquisitions and potential impacts to existing properties. Two main alternatives were discussed:
relocating Union Pacific tracks or adding a Utah Transit Authority (UTA) track to the west. The
western option was presented as the preferred alternative due to lower costs and fewer impacts.

Mr. Allen addressed concerns about noise and vibration impacts, explaining that mitigation
studies are ongoing. Michael Richardson, Right of Way (ROW) Agent, also discussed the right-
of-way acquisition process, emphasizing that affected property owners would be compensated
fairly and provided relocation assistance if necessary.

The Council asked numerous questions about the project’s impacts, timelines, and
communication with affected residents. Concerns were raised about the initial lack of
communication with the City and residents regarding potential property acquisitions.

Item 5: A presentation regarding conservation easements (25-086) 01:42:01

Wendy Fisher, Executive Director of Utah Open Lands, presented information about
conservation easements. She explained that Utah Open Lands is the oldest local land trust in
Utah, holding over 64,000 acres in conservation easements.

Ms. Fisher discussed the benefits of open spaces, noting that outdoor recreation opportunities
contributed to economic growth during the pandemic. She emphasized that conservation
easements can help balance development with preserving quality of life and protecting natural
resources.

The presentation covered the legal definition of conservation easements, their components, and
how they are tailored to each specific property. Ms. Fisher explained that conservation easements
can codify community intentions for land use, protect against private benefit on public lands, and
provide long-term protection for important natural and recreational areas.

Council members asked questions about the permanence of conservation easements, funding
sources for Utah Open Lands, and the organization’s role in enforcing easement terms. Ms.
Fisher addressed concerns about the perpetual nature of easements and explained how the
organization plans for long-term stewardship and potential transfers of easements if necessary.

The presentation provided context for the Council’s upcoming discussion about a potential
conservation easement in Bunnels Fork.

Item 6: A presentation regarding a conservation easement in Bunnels Fork (25-086)
02:15:52

Melia Dayley, Policy Analyst, presented information about a potential conservation easement for
city-owned parcels in Bunnels Fork. She explained that this initiative stemmed from a request by
Councilors Handley and Christensen to explore protecting some or all of the city-owned parcels
in the area through a conservation easement.

Ms. Dayley outlined the legal framework for the City to enter into a conservation easement with
Utah Open Lands at no cost to the City. She referenced the recently passed amendment to the
Hillsides and Canyons Plan, which calls for protecting city-owned lands outside city boundaries.

Mayor Kaufusi expressed strong support for the conservation easement, emphasizing the area’s
natural beauty, critical water resources, and recreational value. Scott Henderson, Chief
Administrative Officer, discussed the logistics of moving forward with the process.

Item 7: A presentation regarding certified tax rates for Provo City (25-089) 02:40:55

Rod Mann, Utah County Auditor, presented information about an error in the certified tax rates
for Provo City. He explained that due to a mistake in the County’s process, over $500 million of
nonexistent taxable value was erroneously added to Provo’s property values, resulting in a tax
rate approximately 5% lower than it should have been.

Mr. Mann outlined the options for correcting the error: fixing it now or deferring the correction
until next year. He recommended fixing it now, which would result in an increase of about $20
per year for the average residential property and about $82–$83 for a business property valued at
$1 million.

The Council discussed the implications of both options, considering the impact on residents and
the City’s budget. It was noted that the Provo School District had already voted to fix their rates
now.

After discussion, the Council reached a consensus to fix the rates immediately. Brian Jones, City
Attorney, informed the Council that a resolution with the updated rates would be presented for a
vote in the evening meeting.
