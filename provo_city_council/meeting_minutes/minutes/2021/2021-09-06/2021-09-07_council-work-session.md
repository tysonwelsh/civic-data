4.      A discussion on the American Rescue Plan Act parameters and priorities. (21-096)
        (0:24:00)

Kelsey Zarbock, Budget Officer, presented. She clarified the differences between the State and
Local Fiscal Recovery Fund (SLFRF) versus the overall American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA)
passed by Congress. Provo’s specific allocation is SLFRF money, but the City may be able to
apply for ARPA funds through the State. Provo has been allocated $33.89 million from SLFRF,
the first half of which was received in June 2021, with the second half scheduled for June 2022.
Ms. Zarbock explained that the spending timeline is more prolonged than the disbursement
timeline—the encumbrance deadline is December 31, 2024, while the actual spending deadline is
December 31, 2026. Ms. Zarbock referred to a list of references and resources on SLFRF which
are hyperlinked in the packet. The interim final rule is the official legislation about this
funding—she noted that it is still titled “interim,” to indicate that the official final ruling is yet to
come. The Treasury Department collected feedback from local municipalities and states, which
they are incorporating into their final rule.

6.      A presentation from the Kem C. Gardner Institute: "Diversity in Utah Data Book"
        (21-094) (1:39:14)

Jennifer Robinson, Kem Gardner Institute, presented. Zions Bank covered expense for this
project, which examines statistics and data for race, ethnicity, and sex in the State. Using that
information, it offers further breakdowns into demographics, economics, education, health, and
housing. Ms. Robinson clarified that they are not examining causality but sharing the differences
illustrated in the data. She shared a breakdown of ethnicity data for the State, noting that Utah is
the 34th most diverse state in the country and is more diverse than many states in the Midwest.
San Juan County is the most diverse, on account of its significant Navajo population. Provo’s
minority population is 29% of the city population.

Data on education levels shows that for bachelor’s degrees or higher, in ages 18-24 and 25-34,
women outpace men. This trend reverses for the over 35 group as men surpass women. Ms.
Robinson reviewed data on poverty in the state, noting that about 9% of Utah’s population lives
in poverty and there are significant differences for certain racial and ethnic groups. In response to
a question from Councilor David Harding on median household income, Ms. Robinson explained
that the term “non-family” refers to roommates, and that the term “household” could be made up
of family or non-family.

There are several different metrics with education and student achievement which they have data
for, including literacy, English language proficiency, and testing scores. There are disparities
between racial groups, with Asian and White populations tending to do better on these
proficiencies. Ms. Robinson also reviewed the data on educational attainment by race and
ethnicity. Councilor Bill Fillmore asked how Utah compared to other states in terms of
educational attainment; Ms. Robinson did not know offhand but indicated that she could refer
that question to their staff.

Ms. Robinson reviewed health data for Utah, noting that women in the state live about 3.5 years
longer than men, with disparities seen in racial and ethnic groups. For the infant mortality rates,
there are several racial and ethnic groups which have a quite higher rate than the national
average. For depression rates in adults, there is a significant difference between men and
women—about 29% of women and 16% of men in Utah have depression. Ms. Robinson noted
that this data comes from an annual survey by the Utah Department of Health. The same survey
is done in every state.

7.      A discussion regarding the Flood Plan maps and the Levee Plan for the westside of
        Provo City. (21-079) (2:15:14)

Dave Decker, Public Works Director, presented. He displayed a map showing the floodplain
areas of the city. There are two different types of events or sources of flooding: Utah Lake, and
the Provo River (as impacted by facilities upstream of the Provo River). A potential flood of
Utah Lake impacts less than 25 homes on the west side of Provo. The elevation of 4498” is key –
new homes must be built to a height of at least one foot above that elevation. Some older homes
are built below that elevation, but all the newer homes are or will be built to higher elevations.
Mr. Decker reviewed a map graphic illustrating the homes in the different floodplain areas of the
city. In response to a question from Councilor David Shipley, Mr. Decker said they estimated
about 300 houses would need flood insurance.

Mr. Decker highlighted an important flood term, cubic feet per second or cfs, which is used for
measuring flooding volume. Mr. Decker further clarified the nature of terms like “100 year
flood,” explaining how this related to a statistical analysis of the likelihood of peak flows. Mr.
Decker explained the specific cfs rates which marked each type of flood event and 2325 cfs is a
100-year event in Provo. Mr. Decker highlighted some actual river gauge data from recent years,
noting that in 2017, there was a flood event which approached 2000 cfs, not far off from what is
described as a 100-year flood. Similar events have been seen in Provo over the years. Mr. Decker

noted that may of the flood events on the Provo River are manmade, resulting from decisions

purpose for Provo’s ASR program, but it is certainly a benefit of it.

Councilor George Handley has read that some dams release larger quantities of water in order to
mimic natural river ecology. Mr. Decker indicated that he has heard the same thing in the context
of the Colorado River. Some of the aquatic life need these flood events. Mr. Decker found two
studies by USGS dated 1905 and 1911—both done before Deer Creek and Jordanelle were built.
Neither of those years had a major flood event of the magnitude of recent years (rather, those
flood events were 1200 cfs). Mr. Decker thought that while it may still be important to have
flood events for sediment and for ecological purposes, it probably could be at lower levels than
the types of human-caused flooding that is typical on the Provo River.

Councilor David Shipley asked whether there were any other outlet or contingency for Deer
Creek, or another way for reservoir management to release pressure without causing flooding on
the Provo River. Mr. Decker indicated that there was not another outlet, be it a river or another
reservoir, which could naturally take the quantity of water necessary. Mr. Decker thought that
ASR represented some significant opportunities to address this issue, but facilitating that process
would require time. Of about a half dozen active ASR sites in the state of Utah, only one is in
Salt Lake County, and while Provo has a planned program, as yet there are no active sites in
Utah County. Mr. Decker suggested it would require a paradigmatic shift in the guiding
philosophies surrounding surface water, groundwater, and how these two sources interact. In
response to another question from Mr. Shipley, Mr. Decker indicated that the capacity of the
Central Utah Water treatment plant in Orem was 150 cfs or 100 MGD (millions of gallons per
day); Provo’s planned treatment plant is one-fifth of this capacity at 30 cfs or 20 MGD.

10.     A discussion on the westside development pipeline. (21-067) (3:27:32)

Councilor David Harding presented. The Planning Commission and planners have been asked to
clarify several provisions of the City’s development policies for west Provo. Mr. Harding was
concerned that an application or project may come through before the policies were refined and
there may be some question of what policies would apply in that situation. He floated the idea of
an intent statement which would communicate the general expectations to stakeholders. The
Council still plans to apply those clarifications to projects which came in during the interim
period. Mr. Harding read the mission statement:

        "The Municipal Council of Provo City desires to put developers, property owners, and
        other stakeholders on notice of its intent to clarify and implement policies contained in
        the Westside Development Policies and Southwest Area Future Land Use Map
        documents in the near future. While those policies are under consideration, we anticipate
        that it will be difficult for the Council to determine that proposed rezones in that area are
        appropriate. We do not desire anyone to incur expenses and spend time on proposed
        applications without being aware of the process that we are undertaking. While all
        applications received will be handled in accordance with the law and will receive full
        consideration, we encourage developers with proposals in the areas affected by those
        policies to hold those proposals until after our effort has concluded so that they may have
        the full benefit of the final policies to consider in making their plans."
