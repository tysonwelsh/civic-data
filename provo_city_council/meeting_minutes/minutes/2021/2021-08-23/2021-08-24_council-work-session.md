Mr. Parker reviewed the recommendations from Casey Gifford’s study from the BYU MPA
program. The study recommended connecting individuals with medical professionals, make
vaccine process convenient and easy, and offer incentives. Mr. Parker shared some of their
thinking surrounding incentives and why they do not plan to pursue incentives as part of the
plan. While incentives have been shown to be effective, using incentives in this scenario creates
some problematic situations which could set a bad precedent for future public health
administration as well as introduce controversy. The data suggests that offering incentives
represents a high cost for a low return on investment of tax dollars.

Mr. Parker outlined the guiding principles for the plan:
    Utah County Health Department takes the lead, supported as needed by City resources
    Focus on messaging through trusted health professionals; trust in government is
       inconsistent and for health subjects, they prefer to rely on trusted professionals
    Leverage effective community partnerships with UCHD, parent associations, medical
       associations, and other relevant community partners
    Focus on improving access to vaccines by removing barriers and engaging the medical
       community, such as transportation and convenience
    Provo and Utah County should both provide resources from available funding sources to
       “prime the pump”
    Outreach efforts should amplify the messages already being promoted by UCHD, the
       Utah Department of Health, and other reputable sources of information

Mr. Parker outlined the key objectives of the plan: to engage healthcare professionals, to research
the key barriers to vaccines locally, to support UCHD’s efforts to improvement outreach and
engagement with target audiences, and to share the resources developed with other Utah cities.

2.      Presentation on Provo River Delta Restoration Project and possible impact on
        Provo Airport. (21-083) (0:56:21)

Dave Decker, Public Works Director, presented. Steve Gleason, Airport Manager, is present on
Zoom, and Mr. Decker was also joined by Brian Torgersen, Public Services Division Director,
and Jimmy McKnight, Public Works Budget Analyst. Mr. Decker’s wanted to help the Council
understand the potential impacts to the airport of the Provo River Delta Restoration project. He
clarified that his intent was not to alter the direction of that project, but to inform the Council of
their concerns. Mr. Decker spoke about their ongoing concerns about bird strikes, which is a
major issue that is top of mind for airport staff. They also have concerns about the ongoing
maintenance responsibilities of the natural and vegetative elements of the delta area. Staff are
exploring the possibility of installing wildlife monitoring systems to protect the airport.

Question from Shannon Ellsworth. Dave decker responded that maintenance will likely tie to the
likelihood of bird strikes in the future; they are keeping an eye on how the project is managed
through the Division of Wildlife Resources. Ms. Ellsworth asked what the Council should do to
proactively ensure the results needed for the City. Mr. Decker indicated that ongoing monitoring
of the project, keep an eye on management, and to bring any items not being adequately
addressed to the property owner or responsible party.

Councilor David Sewell asked about long-term maintenance and how the project would be
transferred to a local entity to manage. Mr. Decker indicated that the mitigation commission’s
typical process is to transfer projects to one of the state agencies. He indicated that the project
managers for these entities have typically never had projects in such close proximity to a
regional or international airport, so that raised some concerns. Presentation only.

5.      A presentation regarding the Development Process: "Context: How design, density,
        and aesthetics interact" (21-080) (3:00:02)

Robert Mills, Planning Supervisor, presented. He hoped this would be an interactive discussion
and he planned to address several important parts of planning and how communities come
together: density, diversity, and context. There are many connotations about the concept of
density. Mr. Mills indicated that it could be measured in the number of units per a designated
area or in number of persons per area. In Provo, most commonly we use the measure of number
of dwelling units per acre. Density is simply division. It can be manipulated very easily by
changing the number of people or the amount of space being discussed. Mr. Mills displayed two
images and asked which had a higher density. These images actually have the same density—it’s
division based on number of units or people and the space. More than the number, the question
of how it looks, feels, and functions is more important. High density is often associated with a
particular type of product, but that is not necessarily the case.

Another important element is diversity—diversity in aesthetics allows for a more visually
pleasing neighborhood, but also allows for cohesion. This also contributes toward the vitality of
a neighborhood, as a diverse housing mix allows for a broader cross section of demographics.
Context refers to the right place, the right time, and the right way. Context changes based on
location. There are some great examples in Provo of contextually correct developments in Provo.
In conclusion, Mr. Mills reiterated that density is not a problem, it’s simply a measurable result.
Design plays a large role in the perception of density. Diversity in aesthetics is more than just
good looks. A diversity of housing types allows for integrated neighborhoods and aging in place.
The appropriate context for developments makes a difference.

Councilor Shannon Ellsworth thanked staff for preparing this presentation—it represents best
practices in the planning industry and what staff are trying to promote and develop as ideal
circumstances in Provo. Cliff Strachan, Council Executive Director, asked how to bring the
community along in understanding these concepts. The Council has a responsibility as the land
use authority, but perhaps they could better engage the community in understanding the elements
at play. Mr. Mills said this was a great question. He thought planning should be a very publicly
engaged process. Education plays an important role. Educating the community about why a

6.      Presentation on return of Winter Olympics to Utah. (21-090) (3:16:54)

Fraser Bullock, Salt Lake City-Utah Committee for the Olympic Games President and CEO,
presented. Mr. Bullock explained that they are in an active bid for the Olympics. He shared a
snapshot of the current bid environment, which has changed dramatically from the past. Mr.
Bullock reviewed the steps they have taken so far to become a contender in the bid for the
Olympics—currently they are deciding between whether to aim for the 2030 or 2034 games. One
concern of bidding for 2030 is the recency of the 2028 games in Los Angeles. Domestic
sponsorships are an important revenue source for the Olympics host city, so it may be more
advantageous to put space out two United States games. Strong contenders for the host city are
Sapporo, Japan; Vancouver, Canada; and Barcelona, Spain. If Vancouver wins a bid for 2030, it
is unlikely that the International Olympic Committee would locate the next winter games in
North America as well. Mr. Bullock shared details of the operating team for the Utah committee.
He also outlined the technical work required which they are currently working through.

Mr. Bullock reviewed the key elements of a Venue Use Agreements, which they need with each
of the Olympic venues, including periods of use (either exclusive or non-exclusive), use of
spaces, control of commercial rights, economics (rental and other costs, revenue offset for the
venue to keep them economically whole), and the roles and responsibilities of each party. They
hope to take a collaborative, partnership approach with the City so that use of the Peaks Ice
Arena is a mutually beneficial arrangement. Mr. Bullock shared an update on the VUAs status
for the different types of venues throughout the Wasatch front.

There are several proposed improvements for the Peaks Ice Arena. The primary purpose is
significant improvement for the community’s everyday use. Another benefit is that the
improvements would meet the requirements for hosting the future Olympic Games. The Utah
committee is currently sourcing funding for the improvements. Specific improvements will
include expanded seating, expanded multi-use space, improved locker rooms, and a new
entrance. Mr. Bullock invited comments or questions from the Council.

Councilor Bill Fillmore hoped that Mr. Bullock could share a breakdown of where the $8 million
in project costs would be coming from. At a high level, Mr. Bullock indicated that they
envisioned $2 million from Provo City, $2 million matched by Utah County, and then the final
$4 million as a match from the State. Cliff Strachan, Council Executive Director, asked about the
timeline on the funding and the construction of the improvements. Mr. Strachan also wondered
whether there would be expanded parking. Mr. Bullock indicated that by the end of the year they
hope to know whether they are aiming for 2030 or 2034. The VUAs should be completed by this
fall. He thinks that the sooner the improvements are done, the sooner that is a benefit to the City.
Showing the commitment of funds to the deciding authorities added legitimacy to Utah’s bid.

Councilor Shannon Ellsworth expressed her support for this committee and what they are hoping
to accomplish by bringing the Olympics to Utah. She was curious whether this was the only

7.      A presentation regarding the allocation of Recreation, Arts, and Parks (RAP) tax
        funds. (21-091) (3:40:19)

Doug Robins, Parks and Recreation Assistant Director, presented. Mr. Robins presented
information regarding the Recreation, Arts, and Parks (RAP) Tax program. Provo’s RAP tax
originated from recommendations in the Parks and Recreation Master Plan process, as well as
community interest. A second source of capital was to collect parks and recreation impact fees.
Mr. Robins shared images of some of the park amenities which needed updates or refurbishment.
In 2015, there was a ballot initiative to implement a sales tax. The tax is 1/10 of 1%, or one cent
per $10 of all taxable purchases in Provo. Mr. Robins referred to a chart showing project
expenditures. For the first few years, they simply accumulated RAP tax funds; after enough
funds had been accrued, they did significant parks projects on a pay-as-you-go basis. Mr. Robins
reviewed the specifics of several past projects, including Rotary Park pickleball courts and Provo
River Trail improvements, and highlighted their future plans as to how they would like to use
this and other funding sources in the coming years to fund additional projects. Mr. Robins
invited questions and comments from the Council.

Councilor David Harding thanked Mr. Robins for the excellent presentation and for being good
stewards of the RAP Tax funds. Mr. Harding asked what “The Show” referred to in the list of
projects. Mr. Robins indicated that this was their moniker for the Little League facility at Fort
Utah Park to create a sense of place. Mr. Harding asked about the timeframe for the renovations
for the north portion of the Provo River Trail or whether it would be deferred. Mr. Robins
indicated that the 2230 North extension beneath the bridge over the Provo River and moving
north was scheduled to begin in the fall in order to utilize MAG funding. Mr. Robins confirmed
that this was expected to happen within the current RAP authorization.

Councilor Bill Fillmore thanked Parks and Recreation leadership for how much they can do with
the budgets they are given. He thanked them for their great work and expressed the Council’s
support in the future of their initiatives. Presentation only.
