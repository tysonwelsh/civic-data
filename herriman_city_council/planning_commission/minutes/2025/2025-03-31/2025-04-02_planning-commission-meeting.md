     1.2. Review of Agenda Items – Planning Staff

Planning Director Maloy provided a general overview of the agenda items. He explained that there
were several applications where the city was the applicant. He noted that these were part of a multi-
pronged, interrelated project that the city council was exploring, but no commitment had been made
yet. He reminded the commission that their role was to make recommendations to the city council
on each item, as the council would be the final decision-maker.

He explained that for efficiency, the interrelated items would be addressed through a single
PowerPoint presentation to streamline discussions and considerations.

Planning Director Maloy highlighted that for items where the city directly acted as the applicant,
Community Development Director Blake Thomas would address questions and essentially represent
the city in these matters. Both Director Thomas and Assistant Manager Thomas were substantially
involved in discussions with development partners as well as meetings with the city council and city
management. This involvement positioned them as well-prepared to address and elucidate any
detailed issues that might arise during the commission meeting. The commission and staff
addressed queries about the K Park obligations and the underground drainage systems, clarifying
the developer's responsibility with respect to these elements. Commissioners expressed specific
interest in understanding how the elimination of the K Park expansion responsibilities would impact
the overall development and the open spaces available to the residents within the project area. The
developments soil contamination issues and impacts on schools and roads were touched on.

Planning Director Maloy mentioned that the last three items on the agenda would need to be
continued to a later date. However, they aimed to open the public hearing for these items during the
current meeting to avoid the need for renotification, which would streamline future handling of the
agenda items and keep the process efficient. This approach was part of a broader strategy to
manage the city's planning efforts effectively while respecting the community's involvement and
input through public hearings.

The commission engaged in a thorough discussion on various facets of the proposal concerning lot
sizes, density, and the proposal's overall impact on the community. Several key points and concerns
were raised during this discussion. Director Thomas offered valuable insights regarding school
capacity, explaining that the school district was experiencing shrinking populations in most schools
except those in the Mountain Ridge area, indicating a trend of houses generating fewer students per
household district wide. This data suggested that Herriman Elementary could accommodate the 41
new students generated by the proposed Sorrento development.

Additionally, Director Thomas relayed traffic impacts were evaluated with input from City Engineer
Bryce Terry. He projected that the Sorrento development would create 536 new Average Daily Trips
(ADT), which would be approximately 6.4 percent of the projected 2050 volume of 9,000 ADT on
5600 West. The capacity for that road was stated to be 17,580 ADT, suggesting that the road would
operate at around 50 percent of capacity at the 2050 build-out, thus not posing a concern for
congestion.

Director Thomas also addressed concerns about lot sizes, explaining the zoning limitations in
Herriman, emphasizing the lack of an intermediate zoning category between A-1-10 (agricultural) and
R-2-10. The existing zones are considered quarter-acre lots or move to the R-2-10 multi-family zone,
which gives flexibility in clustering density through planned unit developments (PUDs) or similar
agreements.

The planning staff reiterated the city's moderate-income housing obligations, which require new
rezonings to contribute towards meeting state housing goals. This demand for moderate-income
housing influences zoning decisions due to potential state penalties affecting transportation tax
dollars if goals aren't achieved.

In terms of housing affordability, Director Thomas emphasized that smaller lots aim to reduce
housing costs, crucial given the region's high housing prices, averaging around $650,000, thereby
presenting challenges for potential homebuyers, especially young families. Chair Powell and others
acknowledged the need for a balance between supporting growth without overburdening schools

addressing state mandates.

   4.6. Consideration and recommendation to amend the Olympia Master Development
        Agreement (MDA), removing obligations related to the “K-Park” and off-site
        improvements, modifying open space requirements, and adding one hundred fifty (150)
        additional residential units to the MDA. (Public Hearing)
        Applicant: Ryan Button (property owner)
        Acres: ±933
        File Number: M2025-029
   https://herrimancity-meeting-files-pc.s3.us-west-1.amazonaws.com/4-2-25/Item+4.8.pdf

Planner Howa presented the proposed amendments to the Olympia Master Development
Agreement (MDA). He explained that there were four main amendments:

Remove requirements related to the development and expansion of K Park: The amendment
involved eliminating the obligations the developer currently has regarding the development and
expansion of a 7-acre section of K Park. This park is located in Hidden Oaks, within pod 14 of the
Olympia development. According to the original MDA, the developer was obligated to expand the
park and provide additional financing for its improvements.

Modify open space obligations: Although the developer’s responsibility to maintain 20% open
space within Olympia remains, the amendment adjusts the calculation by excluding the 7-acre
portion associated with K Park from the total open space obligation. Overall, the amendment
results in a reduction of approximately 3% in open space coverage for the development. Notably,
this change does not restrict the developer from deciding how to utilize that 7-acre parcel, which
could potentially be developed under a residential neighborhood designation, allowing 4 to 8 units
per acre.

Increase overall density by 150 units: The amendment proposes an increase in the total number of
residential units permitted in the Olympia development by 150 units, raising the overall unit count
to 6,480. This adjustment increases the overall density to approximately 6.9 units per acre.
Importantly, the amendment does not specify where these additional units would be distributed
within the development, allowing some flexibility in layout planning.

Remove obligations for the developer to pay for off-site infrastructure impacts: Historically, the
Olympia development was required to contribute to the costs associated with off-site public
infrastructure enhancements. Director Thomas further clarified that these changes result from
substantial efforts and negotiations where the developer has already significantly contributed to
other infrastructure projects, leading to this adjustment in obligations. Additionally, once 12600
South is jurisdictionally transferred to UDOT, the responsibility for future roadway improvements
falls to UDOT, not the developer.

Applicant Ryan Button relayed these amendments are their part in consolidating the area into a
regional park that the city can create an athletic park.
