4. Presentation and Discussion of Attainable Housing
   The Mayor presented a proposal for an attainable housing overlay zone that would provide
   incentives for developers to build smaller, more affordable homes. He emphasized the
   importance of providing attainable, starter home options for the community, drawing on his
   own experience growing up in a small, 900 square foot home. He felt this type of housing was
   currently missing in Lehi and could give young families an opportunity to build equity. The key
   elements of the proposal include: Offering a 50% density bonus for developers who build homes
   1,200 square feet or less on 6,000 square foot lots. This is intended to offset the lower profit
   margins on smaller, more affordable units. The city would work with engineering to ensure the
   increased density does not exceed infrastructure capacity. Requiring the homes to be deed-
   restricted for owner-occupancy to prevent them from becoming investment properties. The
   Mayor suggested keeping the eligibility open rather than restricting it to certain professions.
   Adopting a new sub-local street standard with a 30-foot road and mountable curbs to reduce
   costs. Allowing the home owner to be responsible for sweat equity items like garages and

   simplified, incentive-based process to facilitate the development of attainable, starter homes

   The discussion covered topics like density bonuses, infrastructure impacts, deed restrictions,
   and engaging the community. Overall, the presentation explored ways the city could facilitate
   the development of more attainable housing through zoning, incentives, and partnerships.

   The Council members engaged in a discussion, raising various questions and concerns about
   the attainable housing proposal. Key topics included the potential impact on rental prices, the
   need to balance different housing types, and the importance of not exceeding infrastructure
   capacity. There were discussions around the mechanics of the density bonus. Councilors
   Stallings and Condie questioned whether a bonus density approach was necessary or if a new
   zoning designation would be more appropriate. Also discussed was how the overlay would
   allow developers to participate in the affordable housing program voluntarily through a
   development agreement. There were discussions about the need for deed restrictions to ensure
   owner-occupancy and flexibility for future home expansions. There were concerns raised about
   ensuring fair distribution of density among multiple property owners and the potential for one
   owner to benefit disproportionately.

   Cameron Diehl, Executive Director of the Utah League of Cities and Towns, provided insights
   during the discussion. He explained that the state had created several new tools last year to help
   address the infrastructure challenges that have been hindering housing development. Diehl
   noted that the league had brought data to the governor and legislative leadership showing the
   large number of planned housing units that were stuck due to infrastructure issues. Diehl
   expressed that the concept of a density bonus in exchange for affordable, owner-occupied
   housing has generated significant interest among city leaders across the state. He indicated that
   many Mayors he has spoken with are open to exploring this type of approach, as they are all
   concerned about the future of homeownership in Utah. He emphasized the importance of cities
   working together to find solutions, rather than just reacting to state proposals. He suggested that
   Lehi's attainable housing model could be replicated in other communities if the mechanics can
   be worked out to balance the needs of both cities and developers.

   Councilor Newall expressed support for the overall direction of the attainable housing proposal,
   stating that it addressed many of the concerns she had with the previous affordable housing
   plans. Regarding the bonus density approach, Newall emphasized the importance of ensuring
   the density bonus is tied to the requirement to build the smaller, more affordable home sizes.
   She felt this was key to actually achieving the goal of creating attainable housing, rather than
   just allowing developers to maximize profits on larger homes. Newall also highlighted the need
   to carefully consider community amenities and architectural differentiation, even in these
   smaller, more affordable developments. She wanted to make sure livability and aesthetics were
   not sacrificed in the pursuit of affordability. Overall, Newall was generally supportive of the
   overlay zone concept and bonus density incentives, as long as they were structured in a way

   that truly delivered attainable, owner-occupied housing options for the community. She wanted
   to ensure the details were well-crafted to meet the city's goals.

   Councilor Stallings was generally supportive of the direction. She emphasized the importance
   of protecting the city's existing affordable housing stock, not just focusing on new construction.
   Stallings questioned whether the bonus density incentive was truly necessary, suggesting the
   city could simply create a new zoning designation that allows the smaller, more affordable home
   sizes without the added density. She was concerned about the city potentially overstepping by
   offering the bonus. At the same time, Stallings acknowledged the need to be very thoughtful
   about where this type of attainable housing overlay is applied, to ensure it fits well with the
   surrounding neighborhoods and does not create undue burdens on infrastructure or traffic.
   Overall, Stallings was open to the attainable housing concept, but wanted to ensure the city's
   role was appropriate and that the implementation was carefully considered to benefit the entire
   community.

   Councilor Condie expressed support for the overall direction of the attainable housing proposal,
   but raised some important considerations. Specifically, he would like to know the impact this
   may have on rental prices. Regarding the bonus density approach, Condie understood the
   rationale, but was concerned about the potential for developers to request exceptions and try to
   apply the overlay in areas that may not be appropriate. He emphasized the need for careful
   analysis to ensure the increased density can be supported by the existing infrastructure.

   Councilor Hancock raised several considerations about the details of the attainable housing
   overlay. He was concerned about validating the assumption that smaller home sizes would
   automatically result in reduced infrastructure demands, noting that overall household size and
   fixture units may not decrease as much as expected. Hancock suggested the Council should
   consider putting limits on how much the density can be increased, especially in areas currently
   zoned for larger lots, to avoid dramatic changes without proper analysis. He liked the idea of
   providing pre-designed expansion options to help make the smaller attainable homes more
   livable long-term. Hancock also noted the importance of ensuring adequate community
   amenities, even in these higher density developments, to maintain a good quality of life. Overall,
   Hancock was supportive of the general direction but wanted to ensure the specifics were
   carefully crafted to address infrastructure, density, and livability concerns.

   Paige Albrecht expressed general support for the direction of the attainable housing initiative,
   stating that she felt it was heading in a good direction. She appreciated the plan to have the
   overlay on the general plan, as it would make the opportunity known to property owners upfront.
   Albrecht raised a question about whether there should be limits on how much the zoning could
   be changed, particularly if a developer wanted to apply the overlay to an area currently zoned
   for larger, half-acre lots. She was concerned about dramatic changes to the zoning without
   proper analysis. Regarding the potential for home occupations and nuisances in the smaller,
   denser attainable housing areas, Albrecht suggested the Council may need to review the
   permitted uses and consider any necessary adjustments to the city's nuisance ordinances.
   Overall, Albrecht was supportive of the attainable housing concept, but wanted to ensure the
   implementation details were carefully considered to maintain quality of life and community
   character, especially in areas where the overlay would allow for significant density increases.

3. Discussion of the Central School District including appointments made by the Interlocal
   Board to the Utah County Redistricting Advisory Committee and Naming of the new
   school district.
   The Council had an extensive discussion about the process for naming the new school district.
   Mayor Johnson expressed concern that the naming process often becomes an emotional and
   contentious issue that can distract from more important priorities. Heather Newall emphasized
   the need for a temporary name other than "Central," as she felt it would be problematic for
   school board candidates to have to campaign under a placeholder name. Newall advocated for
   involving the community through surveys and a naming committee led by the Alpine School
   District. She believed this would help build ownership and avoid negative discussions about the
   name during the election. Michelle Stallings argued against the city taking the lead on naming,
   stating that it should be reserved for the elected school board members. She was concerned
   about the city spending money on the process and potentially not being reimbursed. Stallings
   felt that the new board should handle the final decision on naming. Councilor Condie suggested
   sticking with "Central" and allowing the new school board to go through a more inclusive
   process of gathering public input and selecting the final name. He felt this would provide an
   important community engagement opportunity for the elected board members. Paige Albrecht
   agreed that the new board should have the opportunity to name the district, but was open to the
   Interlocal Board providing a temporary placeholder name to avoid the issue becoming a focus
   during the election. Paul Hancock stated that if the Council was just going to use a temporary
   placeholder name, then he thought they should stick with "Central." Hancock used an example
   to illustrate how a placeholder name, even if not intended to be permanent, can become
   entrenched and very difficult for the new school board to change later on, so he suggested they
   simply use "Central" as the placeholder rather than trying to come up with something more
   creative.
