© Expense Reports (Warrants) for February 2020 were presented to the Clerk.
(Attachment 1)

Legislative Update — Executive Buttars commented the session has been relatively
quiet after the repeal of the tax referendum.

© Hyrum Road Facility Update — Footings for the maintenance building have been
poured and the vehicle storage building is about 50% complete. Construction meetings
are Thursdays at 1:00 p.m. Utility placements have been coordinated with Rocky
Mountain Power, CenturyLink and Kilgore.

© REP for Insurance Broker has gone out and responses have been received from five
organizations.

© Corona Virus Update — Executive Buttars and Human Resource Director Adams have
met to discuss: policy for paid leave time, hospitalization scenarios, option to work from
home, and Senior Citizen Center Meals on Wheels. Executive Buttars will meet with
Bear River Health tomorrow to go over their recommendations.

O EMS/Fire Study — Executive Buttars introduced Ken Riddle, Senior EMS/Fire Advisor
for Public Consulting Group and Charles Hurley, Associate Research Specialist. They
have been reviewing the current CCEMS system and are meeting with those associated
with CCEMS. They hope to come back March 25, 2020 for a series of town hall
meetings throughout the county to share their findings and receive public input.
Executive Buttars thanked Riddle and Hurley for their willingness to spend time in Cache
County and acquaint themselves with the CCEMS operations and individuals who have
been involved with CCEMS.

ITEMS OF SPECIAL INTEREST

o Disposition of Surplus Property at Sheriff's Office ~ Sheriff Chad Jensen asked for
Council approval to donate firearms to the State Forensics Lab for the purpose of

developing a firearms library and test. Attorney Luthy explained there are three options
available for the county — sell, destroy or donate firearms accumulated through the years
via evidence or lost items turned over to the Sheriff's office.

e Development Services Department Report — Development Services Director Chris

Harrild reported his department is focused on trying to improve the processes for various
permits, applications, etc. The department’s website has been completely revised and
updated. The next step is priorities and goals and the creation of a spreadsheet of goals
for 2020.

Vice Chair Worthen asked if Development Services had done its own website revisions.
Harrild said it is a map-based site and Cary Jenkins, GIS Administrator, contributed to
the design.

Council member Tidwell asked if Harrild would like to be invited to meetings of the Logan
Homeless Committee. Harrild replied with a definite “yes” as Development Services has
been instrumental in drafting a moderate-income housing plan.

PUBLIC HEARING: March 10, 2020 at 6:00 p.m. — Resolution No. 2020-04 — Open the 2020
Cache County Budget — Finance Director Jensen reviewed the adjustments to the budget

including:

-2020 payroll increases

-Purchase order carryovers
-Allocation to tax administration fund
-Victim Advocate grants

-Road facility construction

-Lease proceeds for patrol vehicles
-Senior Center grants and requests
-Ambulance

Chairman Ward opened the Public Hearing and invited public comment.
Bill Bower prefers that county vehicles be kept under protective cover to prolong the life of the vehicles.
There was no other public comment.

ACTION: Motion by Council member Zilles to close the Public Hearing. Worthen
_ seconded the motion. The vote was unanimous, 6-0. White absent.

¢ Resolution No. 2020-04 —- Amending the 2020 Cache County Budget — Chairman
Ward remarked he hoped their constituents understand and see how much detail is

involved in the County budget and how much study is put forth by the Council members
to be certain where every penny goes.

Some areas of concern or interest were how CCEMS dollars will be affected in the future
and roads and a needed transportation plan. Council members hesitate to spend too
much money on fixes at the Senior Citizen Center as they feel a new facility is needed;
however, they agreed repairs must be made to ensure the safety and functionality of the
Center and its grounds.

(Attachment 5)

ACTION: Motion by Council member Zilles to waive the rules and approve Resolution
No. 2020-04 — Amending the 2020 Cache County Budget. Borup seconded the motion.
The motion passed, 5 aye — Borup, Erickson, Tidwell, Ward and Zilles &1 abstention —
Worthen. White absent.

Ditching

One of the purposes of a roadway drainage ditch is to prevent unsafe accumulation of rain water on the
roadway surface. An open ditch allows water to move fully away from a road surface. An open ditch also
provides land surface run off to be moved correctly away from road surfaces. Without proper maintenance
these ditches can become a problem.

For many years drainage ditches along county roads have not been maintained to proper standard. With the
continued growth of homes, traffic and stormwater coming from adjacent cities, the need to build an annual
ditch cleaning program has become critical. Over the past two years we have started to implement such a

program.

Ditches that have not been unmaintained for many years can become a drainage problem for both the road
and field drainage. With the development of new infrastructure, both in the county and the cities, have go
adjust to these changed drainage patterns. Stormwater is a critical issue that will have to be dealt with in the
future.

Ditches, after cleaning, will help preserve both road surfaces and land from water issues. Water can be the
most destructive element on road surfaces. Without proper drainages, road life is changed dramatically.

County Chipping

Why does the county chip seal?

The county has two types of road structures. These include new construction paved roads using Hot Mix
Asphalt (HMA) and double chip roads.

A majority of Cache County Roads were constructed as double chip surfaces. This was the most cost
effective process allowing for large areas to be treated in an era of shrinking budgets. Understanding
the process of a double chip road will also show the importance of a good chip seal program.

Double chip roads are constructed by grading and prepping an existing gravel road. When this process
is complete, a soft, flexible, polymer, modified asphalt emulsion is applied directly to the road surface
followed by an application of 3/4” aggregate. The think asphalt membrane water proofs and bonds the
new aggregate to the surface, providing a new skid resistant wearing course. Once the first application
is cured, a second layer of polymer modified asphalt emulsion is applied to the road surface followed by
an application of 3/8” aggregate. This second application bonds the first creating a hard non

permeable surface. This process is known as a double chip.

To complete the double chip process, a single layer of polymer modified asphalt emulsion is applied
again directly to the road surface followed by an application of 3/8” aggregate the following year of

the double chip. This process is called a chip seal.

Benefits of single chip seal:

Extends the pavement life by protecting it from oxidation and deterioration from moisture

e@ Easy and quick application causing minimum disruption to the traveling public

© Cost effective treatment stretches maintenance dollars by covering large area for the least money
¢ Seals and prevents reflection of small surface cracks and reduces moisture infiltration

© Improves skid resistance and winter traction by providing a textured surface

Routine Maintenance Program

Once a road is constructed either paved or double chipped, these roads must still have routine
maintenance. Chip seals are the most versatile and widely used pavement preservation process and are

the most effective when preserving rodds in good conditions.

The county at this time has 196 miles of hard paved surface roads to maintain. Based on our rotational

cycle, we chip seal between 32 and 34 miles of road each year. By maintaining this amount of road


each year, all road surfaces are treated within that 5-6 year rotation allowing us to keep these roads in
good traveling condition. This management system also allows the road department to maintain a long
term maintenance outlook knowing the condition, age and maintenance needs of each road. This allows

for better budgeting and cost analysis.

City Chipping Program

Each year the county spends between 10-12 weeks starting from middle of June to late August in the
actual chipping process.

The first six weeks of chipping is contracted work, conducted for the cities throughout the area. This past
year we had contracts for the following cities: Newton, Trenton, Clarkston, Cornish, Lewiston, Richmond,
Smithfield, Hyde Park, North Logan, Providence and Nibley. The county plays an important role in
helping these government entities to meet their road maintenance needs in a cost effective way.

Snow Operations

The county road department is responsible for providing snow removal for approximately 392 paved lane
miles and over 180 miles of gravel roads along with five county facility parking areas. Planning for storm
events is difficult. The differences in locations, rate of snow fall, and moisture content, temperature, time of
day and wind velocity make each storm unique. Decision making and assignments is a very fluid process
based upon hour by hour storm conditions.

Snow removal is conducted with seven large plow trucks: three graders and four small trucks. The large plow
trucks are divided into seven different zones covering all paved surfaces in the unincorporated county.
Graders are split into three zones covering all gravel roads in the unincorporated county. Four small trucks
cover five county facility parking areas and then areas of paved surfaces too difficult for large trucks to
plow. During the snow season, all county employees make themselves available for on call duty. These
employees are ready to take care of snow removal duties at all times and all days needed.

1) Snow Removal Assignments
a) Snow removal will be divided into four operations:

Grading and Road Resurfacing

With the wear and tear that affects gravel roads on a regular basis, effective maintenance can pose a
unique challenge. Ruts, pot holes, and displaced gravel are a natural side effect of heavy traffic, and
this damage may require costly resurfacing in problem areas or even along entire lengths of road.

To efficiently maintain this work load, the roads have been classified into two categories: valley floor
roads and mountain roads. To accomplish getting all gravel roads maintained, three graders will split the
responsibility of both the valley floor grading and the mountain road grading.

The valley floor contains roughly 185 miles of gravel and dirt roads. Of the 185 miles of roads, 160
miles have been identified as of needing annual grading. Of those 160 miles, 151 are on a twice
annual grading schedule.

Valley roads will be split into three zones with one grader responsible for that zone.
e Each operator will cover around 53 miles of road.
e If one grader operator finishes a zone first he will then help other operators finish their zone until
all gravel roads have been graded.

The mountain roads contain roughly 215 miles of road. Of the 215 miles of mountain roads, 80 miles will
be done annually with the remaining being graded on a rotational basis.

Mountain roads will be split into two different grading operations: annual roads and rotational roads.
e Each operator will have an average of 27 miles of annual road assigned to him to maintain.

Pavement Markings

For roads, edge lines and centerlines are important, and they are extremely critical if traffic will be
traveling at a brisk speed and/or multiple lanes move in the same direction. Markings that indicate
mandatory turns, warn of upcoming stop signs, denote pedestrian crossings or segregate bike lanes are

highly desirable.

For parking lots, pavement markings should include arrows to indicate the direction that traffic should
follow and to identify crosswalks, the aisles borclering accessible parking spaces and no-parking zones.
Large parking lots may need pavement markings to supplement stop signs as well as markings to
differentiate between exits and entrances. Bumps, dips and other potential hazards should be marked as

well.

Biocontrol Monitoring:

Four sites were monitored for biocontrol efficacy.
Two sites are new and have never been monitored.
Two sites have had biocontrol releases since 2017 on
Canada thistle. Monitoring is conducted using the
US Forest Service SIMP protocol. We modified the
SIMP protocol to allow for random sampling
instead of a straight line transect. It is too difficult
with a large group of students to conduct line
transect monitoring. Plots are often trampled and
students seem to lose focus by the time we reach the
end of the transect. We have adapted the SIMP
protocol by having students break off into several
groups over a one acre area. Students stand with
their back to an infestation of noxious weeds and
gently toss monitoring frames over their head. This
allows us to select noxious weed patches, but still
take a random sample.
