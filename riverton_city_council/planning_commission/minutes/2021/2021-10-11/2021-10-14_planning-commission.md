      A.     CONDITIONAL USE HOME OCCUPATION, “BASIN MASONRY”
             LOCATED AT 13711 LINDA MARIE WAY. APPLICANT, CLARK ROUNDY.

City Planner, Tim Prestwich presented the staff report and stated that the matter was
continued from the September 23 meeting due to questions raised. The subject property is
situated on the south end of the City at 13711 Linda Marie Way, which is a dead-end street.
The zoning is RR-22, and it is one-half acre in size. The applicant operates a masonry
business and runs a consulting office. He has requested that 10 employees be allowed to
come. Two work trucks are stored at the home.

The Ordinance allows non-resident employees if they can meet the criteria including vehicles
being screened and out of public view. In addition, the floor space should not exceed 25%
of the upstairs or 50% of the basement. The Parking Plan was presented. Mr. Prestwich
stated that there are markings for parking but there may not be sufficient parking. It was
noted that the parking is not screened.

Questions were raised previously regarding the Building Permit and the existing Home
Occupation License. The Building Permit for the basement was issued in 2017 and no Final
Inspection had been requested. The last inspection included framing, plumbing, mechanical
systems, and roughed in electrical. It is the applicant’s responsibility to request a final
inspection. It was not clear whether the plan called for the basement area to be an office.
Additional information was requested from the applicant regarding how much of the
basement will be used for the business. They were told that it would be 50% but staff still
did not know how it will be used or how they arrived at that number.

6.       TRAINING

Mr. Carter presented training regarding the Peterson vs. Riverton City case. The case
illustrates legislative decisions and the Commission’s role. He presented a before and after
photograph of a development project that includes 20 to 30 acres of property located at
approximately 3600 West and 12000 South on the east side of 3600 West. The property is
surrounded by R-2 zoning.

The General Plan for the City provides general parameters regarding usage for a given area.
This area called for low-density residential, but the General Plan specifies that it included R-
22, R-2, and R-3 zoning. The biggest difference with the R-3 zoning was the absence of
large animal rights. That difference ultimately determined the outcome of the case heard by
the Utah Supreme Court. The original lawsuit was filed in 2008, with the decision filed in
October 2010.

Mr. Carter emphasized the following specific points about the case:

        The importance of the record in the case and how that information ultimately saves
         the City from a lawsuit.

        Some items are beneficial to the City, as well as discriminating items on the Record.

The Planning Commission typically makes legislative decisions rather than administrative
decisions; however, there are several cases where administrative decisions are made by
Riverton City Planning Commission Meeting                                                 10
October 14, 2021

the Commission. The level of evidence necessary to support the basis of a decision is low
when making a legislative decision. It is much higher when making an administrative
decision. The intent of this discussion was to address Legislative decisions.

Mr. Carter reported that a legislative decision is the most general policy decision that an
elected body makes. As long as there is a reason on the record to serve as a basis for a
decision, nothing else matters. He emphasized that elected officials make policy-based
decisions that have political ramifications. Elected officials are held accountable for these
types of decisions. Therefore, the decisions should rest solely on them. If a person wants
to reverse this type of decision in court, it is difficult because of accountability issues. The
courts grant a great deal of deference to elected officials when making policy decisions.
Occasionally, Planning Commission Members can forget when they are making a legislative
decision as opposed to an administrative decision.

Mr. Carter reported that the property was worth $5.5 million in the contract price before the
housing crisis. Because the zoning was not approved as requested, the purchaser, D.R.
Horton, backed out of the deal. This occurred before the crash at which time the property
ultimately lost the majority of its estimated value.

Additionally, at the same time, the City needed to manage its stormwater system and
determine where to place a regional detention pond west of the canal, which bounds the
property. The City discussed with D.R Horton the need for the pond and the probability of
purchasing a portion of it. D.R. Horton was neutral about the issue and agreed to pass the
cost on to the City. That discussion happened at a Staff level before the matter was heard
by the Planning Commission. Later, when the issue went to court, this topic became a
negative for the City.

Mr. Carter referred to the written opinion and described the process of the decision. The
proposal was heard by the Planning Commission who vetted the issues, and the record was
created. At the hearing, the Planning Commission noted that the proposed plan would be
consistent with the General Plan. Local citizens raised concerns about the impact of the
ability to continue to raise large farm animals in the vicinity of R-3 zoning. As insignificant
as this issue may seem, the courts considered it to be relevant.

Prior to this case, and based on prior rezoning decisions, the reasons to support the City
Council’s decisions were more compelling than anything noted in the record. The basis for
the decision was that the neighbors were concerned about large animal rights, traffic, and
the desire to preserve the residential character of the area. The traffic issue was
inconsequential to the Regional Transportation Plan, but the courts noted that there was a
concern about the traffic, and they deferred, which was the outcome of the case.

It was also noted that Riverton City discussed the need for a retention pond, and a Council
Member, not a Planning Commission Member, weighed in on the issue. Mr. Carter stated
that Council Members should only comment on issues when they are acting in the capacity
of a Council Member in a Council Meeting. An elected official giving an opinion to seemingly
sway the decision of the Planning Commission can be very detrimental in the event of a
Riverton City Planning Commission Meeting                                              11
October 14, 2021

court challenge. The Council Member, in this case, claimed that what was proposed
constituted spot zoning, which was erroneous.

In essence, the two main facts on the record that were detrimental to the City were that the
Council Member made an error in opinion about spot zoning. Mr. Carter explained that up-
zoning increases the value of the property. The lawyers for the applicant then crafted the
argument that the City Council was faced with a conflict of interest when they made the
decision not to grant the rezoning because it would increase the value of the property and
the City would have to pay more for the portion they needed for the retention pond. This
resulted in litigation in the Utah Supreme Court and Federal District Court.

The positive outcome for the plaintiff in a court hearing would be a reversal of the decision
to deny the rezone. There would be no monetary damages involved. However, in the
Federal District Court, the property owner raised equal protection claims. The only
information the City had to defend itself was the official record that was presented to the
Planning Commission and the City Council. The property owners tried to show that they
had been discriminated against because there had been other decisions made in Riverton
City where the applicant was successful in rezoning to R-3 from R-22 or R-2. Ultimately,
they were unable to produce significant evidence to that effect.

In the Standard Review and Analysis, the District Court correctly applied the reasonably
debatable decision because the Council decision was legislative. Mr. Carter explained that
‘reasonably debatable’ means that the Council makes a decision based on what makes
sense and is reasonable. It is not based on petition or vote counts. As long as the decision
is ‘reasonably reasonable’, the courts will not reverse it.

Mr. Carter read through a portion of the decision and reiterated the power of the legislative
decision. He brought up the toxic issues of spot zoning and up-zoning that became issues
in the City’s defense. Mr. Carter explained that the idea of spot zoning should not be based
on the size of the property. The more important issue is whether the use is harmonious with
the surrounding uses. There is no legal way to defend a decision based on spot zoning.

Ultimately, the property owner failed to show evidence of malice or bad faith on the part of
the Council. The court realized that, although the toxic issues had been mentioned in the
meeting, there were other reasons to deny the proposal, which is what the Council decided.
The court determined that the Council’s decision was acceptable and that there were other
reasonable points on the record to support the Council’s decision.

The City won in this situation because the record was complete. Mr. Carter reemphasized
that the supporting reasons for the rezone were more unlikely than any other rezone appeal
that has been contested in the Utah Court of Appeals. The City has not had another rezone
challenge since this case was decided.

Mr. Carter emphasized that the primary duty of the Planning Commission is to build a record
when making these types of decisions. Often elected officials and Council Members discuss
issues off record, which ultimately harms the record. The record is the most important tool
the City has to defend a decision. Another example was given of a member of the
Riverton City Planning Commission Meeting                                                12
October 14, 2021

community who has something to say about an issue but does not want to come to a meeting
to speak publicly. They might instead go to a neighbor who serves on the Planning
Commission and talk to him or her off the record. There are no laws against this, but it
ultimately eliminates the record on which to base a decision in the event of a challenge.
Mr. Carter suggested that if someone approaches a member of the Planning Commission
regarding an issue that is on the agenda, the first response should be to tell them to attend
the hearing. If that is not possible, sending an email is effective.

Mr. Carter stated that the Peterson vs. Riverton City case also helped demonstrate the Class
of One concept. When the Commission departs from its duties and makes decisions that
are not compliant with Code, the law, or the record, the Class of One lawsuit is viable and
can be instituted.

A question was raised regarding indemnification and situations that may arise. Mr. Carter
recommended that this topic be discussed in a closed session.

A question was raised to clarify the difference between legislative and administrative
decisions. Mr. Carter explained that when making a legislative decision, the standard of
evidence to rely on is housed under the phrase, ‘reasonably debatable standard’. For an
administrative decision, the ‘substantial evidence standard’ must be followed. This issue
was to be discussed in greater detail at a later date.

A hypothetical question was raised regarding the Peterson case and the potential for a
Planning Commission Member to have an adjacent property that would be impacted by a
zone change. If that person were to vote based on what would favor him or her, it could
have a detrimental impact on the case. Mr. Carter stated that the applicant has the right to
have his or her application reviewed impartially. The applicant must build a case showing
that the Planning Commission Member acted in a personally animus way. This
circumstance would call for the Commissioner to recuse him or herself from the vote. He
stated that if during the course of the interaction, an elected official or Commission Member
implies that they are basing their decision primarily on personal issues, they would be at risk
of a lawsuit. Mr. Carter cited other situations and concluded that conditions must be
imposed to eliminate discretion to the extent possible.

A question was raised regarding building the record. Mr. Carter explained that in this case,
public comment was a legitimate source of information on the record to serve as a basis for
the decision. It is best practice when making a motion or discussing the decision to state
the reasons for or against the decision. He also mentioned that putting elected officials in a
deposition is unpredictable and not recommended. It is best to ensure that the record
provides reasonable reasons for a decision.

Another question was raised regarding the Planning Commission delaying a decision to get
clarification on a topic. Mr. Carter made it clear that the Commission has authority to delay
or continue a public hearing. Continuing the meeting and keeping a public hearing open is
different than closing the meeting. The wording needs to be clear on the record. He warned
about delaying meetings strategically and cited instances where a Commission in another

he creates ordinances to stop that practice.
