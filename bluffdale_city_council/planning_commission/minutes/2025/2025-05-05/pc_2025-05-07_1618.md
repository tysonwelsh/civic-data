       Proposed Text Amendment to Sections 3.10.040 and 3«80.050 of the Blnffdale City
       Code to Provide a Process for New or Unlisted Businesses to be Classified as Required
       by State Law, to Require all Businesses to Comply with Adopted Zoning
       Requirements, Building Codes and Fire Codes, and to Amend the Definitions of
       Home-Based Microschools and Micro Education Entities FoUowing Changes to State
       Law During the 2025 Legislatiye Session. (Application 2025-06) City of Blnffdale.
       Applicant Caitlyn Tubbs. Staff Presenter.


Ms. Tubbs presented the Staff Report and reported that the Text Amendment was in response to
two bills passed in the most recent Legislative Session.

Senate Bill ("SB") 179 requires each municipality and county to enact a Land Use regulation
establishing a process for reviewing business uses not currently listed as an Approved Use in
existing ordinances. Blnffdale City Code includes a section on administrative interpretation.
However, out of an abundance of caution, the Text Amendment was proposed to clarify the
process.



Staff determined that the current Business Licensing Code allows the City to revoke or deny a
Business License for non-conformance with building or zoning codes, but it does not specifically
require compliance. The Text Amendment specifies that no license or permit will be issued ifthe
proposal does not fully comply with the adopted building code, fire code, and zoning regulations.

Applicants for a new or unlisted business Use may apply for an Administrative Interpretation. If
determined to be a new or unlisted Business Use, the applicant may submit an Ordinance Text
Amendment for review by the Planning Commission and City Council. If approved, the City
Council would designate the zone(s)in which the Use would be permitted or prohibited. Ifdenied,
the applicant would be notified ofthe reason for denial.


Senate Bill 272 amended the State's definition ofhome-based micro-schools and micro-education

entities. Language regarding the minimum square footage per student was removed. Blnffdale
Home Occupation Code must be updated to incorporate that change.

8.     Planning Commission Training- Daniel Tracer. Staff Presenter.

Mr. Tracer conducted stormwater regulation training on Low-Impact Development, which
minimizes the impact development has on stormwater.


Stormwater is water that runs off the landscape during storm and melt events. Pre-development,
approximately one-quarter ofthe stormwater soaks deep into the ground, one-quarter soaks in at a
shallower level, and 40% is soaked up by plants or evaporates back into the atmosphere.
Approximately 10% of the stormwater runs off into rivers and streams. Post-development, the
amount ofrunoffincreases to approximately 55%.

Traditional stormwater treatment involves collecting the water in pipes and moving it away from
the location and into water bodies. Issues with that method include the distribution of pollutants
like oil, gasoline, and trash into rivers and streams. Quantity of water is also an issue as it causes
flooding. Impacts include algae blooms in Utah Lake due to nitrogen and phosphorus loading
from fertilizers and insufficient sewer treatment from older treatment plants as was seen in recent
years. Those issues led to a change in philosophy.


The current strategy is to reduce the impact ofstormwater on receiving waters through low-impact
development, keeping the water onsite, and letting it out slowly to lessen the impact on water
bodies. Keeping the water onsite also keeps pollutants onsite.


Low-Impact Development is a strategy to mimic natural stormwater systems with natural-looking
features that hold stormwater so it can be soaked up by plants and the ground and utilize filtration
to remove contaminants. It can require a bigger park strip with a depression to hold stormwater

and let it soak into the ground. Low-Impact Development for parking lots would be to include a
depressed landscaped area between asphalt parking stalls to capture stormwater. This tj^e of
development provides a large benefit without being obtrusive.          Landscaped areas include
stormwater storage to allow stormwater to soak into the ground, and certain plants also soak up
pollutants in the water.


Low-Impact Development also helps preserve open space. Developers often see landscaping
requirements as a hindrance, but Low-Impact Development features fulfill the stormwater
treatment requirement while beautifying the area.

Cluster development can also preserve open space to mimic natural pre-development hydrology.
Instead of developing an entire parcel, some natural features are retained, and people will often
pay a premium to have those features in their neighborhood.

Impervious areas can be reduced by utilizing permeable pavement and pavers in driveways and
parking lots. Bluffdale has successfully implemented this strategy in Vintage Park and other areas.
The City has also implemented grass and vegetated swales along roadways that convey water on
the surface rather than in a pipe below ground, which provides an opportunity for the water to be
slowed down and filtered out by existing vegetation. The vegetated swales on Bringhurst
Boulevard have been very successful. The developer was hesitant to install them, but they now
plan to utilize them in all future phases of Bringhurst Station.

Other Low-Impact Development strategies include infiltrated wetlands, constructed wetlands,
bioretention, rain gardens, green roofs, and rainwater harvesting and reuse. Limitations of Low-
Impact Development include higher design,installation, and maintenance costs, and each site must
be analyzed to determine site conditions and current area water quality.


All Municipal Separate Storm Sewer System ("MS4") municipalities, including all major cities
along the Wasatch Front, receive a Stormwater Discharge Permit fi*om the Utah Department of
Environmental Quality that includes requirements and limitations around stormwater systems.

Mr. Nelsen reported that Bluffdale has successfully implemented rain gardens and bioretention
features. However, most stormwater projects in the City are developer-driven, and Staff has
limited control over the system design. Developers are guided to comply with Low-Impact
Development characteristics through standards that set clear expectations upfront.

Developers were previously allowed to install stormwater retention ponds in backyards, but most
of those features were removed by the homeowners, leading to stormwater collecting in other
areas. They are no longer allowed on private property and must be on public property so they can
be properly maintained. As a result ofthis experience, the City created a Stormwater Maintenance
Agreement that property owners are required to enter into and outlines their responsibility to
maintain the stormwater features. Ifthe property owner does not comply with the agreement, the
City steps in to perform maintenance at the property owner's expense. If stormwater systems are
on City property, they can be properly maintained.


Approximately 90% ofcommercial properties now implement a buried system ofperforated pipes,
which are usually buried below the parking lot. They do not occupy buildable land while allowing
stormwater to soak into the ground. Mr. Nelsen noted that these systems were not common eight
years ago, but the cost of land has made them very popular. They need to be in a proper spot to
be maintained, as was learned when inspection ports were inadvertently left off of a Stormtech
installation on 1000 West. Treatment units are also included on most commercial properties to
collect trash, dirt, and sediment in a structure that cleans the water prior to leaving the site.

Standards were revised to reduce maintenance requirements. Previously, all stormwater ponds
included grass. Some larger ponds still have grass, but smaller ponds are now xeriscaped to reduce
the City's long-term maintenance costs.
