decisions about city trees, tree selection, care, and pest and disease management. There are a
number of trained arborists among the city employees. Councilor Katrice MacKay asked about
the percentage of trees within the purview of Parks and Recreation versus Energy. Mr. Robins
indicated about one-third of the city trees are in city parks. Councilor George Handley asked

Chaz Addis, City Forester, presented. He shared background information on the Utility Forestry
division, which has three utility forestry crews. Each three-person crew is responsible for one-
third of the City, and they try to service every area once every three years. He shared details
about the purpose for the aesthetics of their line clearance methods, which promote pest control
and photosynthesis. They also have a tree replacement program—if a tree needs to be taken out
directly beneath a powerline, they can work with homeowners on selecting a new tree and
planting it. They also have an annual tree giveaway to promote energy conservation with
strategic tree-planting. They also run a Tree Line USA program for Arbor Day. Mr. Addis
outlined the requirements for participation in this program. In the last few years, they have
greatly reduced their tree-caused outages through their routine maintenance.

The City Forestry division includes the street tree program and inventory report, public education
programs, and the Tree City USA program. The street tree program deals with trees in the park
strip (if the park strip is over 4 feet wide). Provo City has maintained its Tree City USA
designation for 38 years as of 2022; Mr. Addis outlined the criteria for the program. He also
shared a graph of the city’s tree inventory and the carbon sequestration resulting from city trees
(over the trees’ lifetime). The City facilitates tree-planting projects including tree-planting for
Arbor Day. There are two three-person teams maintaining over 43,000 trees—one crew focuses
on power line clearance and the other crew focuses on street trees. Mr. Handley asked if there
was an update on the 1000 trees initiative. Mr. Addis indicated that the 353 trees planted were
outside of the development protocol and would go toward that 1000. They only planted about 65
trees otherwise, so they are a little over 400 at current count. Mr. Handley hoped the City could
achieve that goal and utilize the resources that were dedicated toward it.

Councilor Shannon Ellsworth is passionate about the benefits of the urban forest and tree
canopies. She lives in a neighborhood with older trees and fallen tree branches are a concern. She
submitted a request through customer service and got a response that it had been addressed; she
thought this was problematic to say it has been completed if it hasn’t been. There is a bit of a
communication disconnect she hopes they can address. Mr. Addis said within the customer
service system, they should be able to update the item as being in progress. Mr. Addis also
shared more context about how requests are received and processed.

Ms. Ellsworth asked about any tree types that are not permitted in the park strip; Mr. Addis
indicated that any fruit-bearing trees or conifers are not permitted in the park strip. Ms. Ellsworth
commented about an area she had walked with a tree canopy that was quite low into the
pedestrian headspace. Councilor Rachel Whipple asked if they do not plant any trees that drop
things such as horse chestnuts. Mr. Addis indicated that they haven’t planted any horse chestnuts
recently. Ms. Whipple also asked about Russian olive which are an invasive species. Mr. Addis

Water Management
A water treatment plant is not a new concept for Provo – specifically mentioned in a 1965 water
master plan. At the time, the city chose instead to pursue developing the city’s groundwater. Mr.
Decker shared a graph from a 2013 water master plan that shows all the City’s water sources and
all the water rights the City has, including springs, consolidated groundwater rights, Provo River,
and surface water in reservoirs such as Deer Creek and Jordanelle. This graph also displays
historic water production, which is well below the State’s water conservation goal. Mr. Decker
shared several graphs showing the water levels in several of the City’s wells. Data shows a
decline in the water levels of these wells over time. Water rights on paper do not always
materialize into the water that is physically available, particularly in a dry year. Mr. Decker
talked about the impact of groundwater on water levels in Utah Lake and potential negative
ramifications. Mr. Decker shared some projections based on actual water compared to the City’s
water rights. To continue using groundwater beyond what is actually available has serious
ramifications for Provo’s water sources.

Councilor David Shipley asked about how the water table in Provo works relative to the west
side, which has a higher water table. Mr. Decker talked about the different soil compositions to
the west and east of I-15 in Provo—the eastern area of the City has much more rock content in
the soil, which allows for very effective aquifer storage and recovery to take place. Mr. Decker
clarified that liquefaction relates to earthquake activity, rather than water issues.

Mr. Decker displayed a graph showing projections of Provo’s water production with aggressive
growth in Provo as well as continuing droughts. A water treatment plant is essential in order to
meet water production needs into the future. Mr. Decker shared more projections and data on the
flow of the Provo River as well as the water flowing into Utah Lake. The outflow figures show
water that leaves Utah Lake to go further downstream. About 75% of that outflow is for water
rights located downstream in Salt Lake County. Very little of that water reaches the Great Salt
Lake. The 1995 interim Utah Lake agreement was published by the state engineer and addresses
water rights that occur after Utah Lake; water stored in Utah Lake has an impact on what is able
to be stored in Jordanelle and Deer Creek reservoirs. Mr. Decker receives many questions about
preparing for droughts and drought management. Mr. Decker displayed a 2005 graphic from a
state study on historic droughts from the late 1800s to the early 2000s. He also shared a tree ring
study done in the Weber area that includes data on droughts back to the 1400s. The Utah
Division of Water Resources estimates that there is a significant drought event about every 30
years. Mr. Decker indicated he would continue with this topic on February 28.

Water Treatment Plant Financial Update
Mr. Decker shared an update on the water treatment plant. He first highlighted the aquifer
storage and recovery (ASR) projects that are underway. Rock Canyon is one of the best ASR
sites in the entire state. Underground water moves relatively slowly once it is in the ground. This
project has four components for a total cost of $17 million including: Rock Canyon pump
station, Rock Canyon waterline, 36” waterline, and the Provo River Water Treatment Plant. Mr.
Decker highlighted the funding they have already secured for this project, including ARPA

a public hearing on March 14 regarding the loan and related bonding. Mr. Shipley asked if there
was an ability to save the city money from some of the processing that happens through Central

bonds, so they will be repaid from the revenues that are generated by the water system (as
opposed to general obligation bonds, for which a tax is levied for repayment of the bonds). They
do not anticipate that water rates will increase to meet the debt service payments at this time. A
public hearing will be held at the Council Meeting on March 14, 2023, and details of the meeting

wondered if these details could be added to the notice to better clarify the purpose and benefits.
