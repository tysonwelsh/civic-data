        1.2      Mayor’s Report

      Mayor Kristie Overson gave a huge shout-out to the city’s facilities team, snowplow drivers,
the police department for helping residents and the residents themselves for their patience, as it
took time to deal with such a large storm.

She recently had a training opportunity thanks to a VirTra simulator and TVPD Officers Kyle
Andrew and Dan Christensen. The simulator allowed her to respond to simulated crises involving
various tactics, crisis communication, de-escalation techniques, decision-making, all in a safe,
simulated environment. The mayor found it to be an eye-opening experience to see some of the
many things officers had to think about during critical moments on the job.

As Chair of the Unified Fire Service Area (UFSA) Board, Mayor Overson had recently witnessed
the demolition and groundbreaking of Fire Station No. 102 in Magna. (The UFSA was the funding
mechanism for the Unified Fire Authority). She appreciated the city’s membership in both the
UFA and UFSA.

The Saturday with Santa event had been very successful the previous weekend. She thanked the
many people involved, including the Historic Preservation Committee, the Parks and Recreation
Committee, the Cultural Diversity Committee, the Youth Council, scout troops and
councilmembers for their assistance in organizing the event.

A holiday open house had been held the previous week for community partners around the valley.
It was attended by legislators, contractors, developers, elected officials, business community
members, public safety and more. The mayor explained it had been the perfect opportunity for
people to connect and to highlight all the good things that had happened in Taylorsville in 2021.

6.      FINANCIAL MATTERS

        6.1      Audit Report – Scott Harrington

       Scott Harrington explained that earlier in the day he had sent the councilmembers an
electronic copy of the Comprehensive Annual Financial Report (CAFR) which he and his
department had been working on for several months. He then turned the time over to Rob Wood
of accounting firm HBME.

Mr. Wood began his comments by congratulating the council and commending the citizens that
sat on the Budget/Audit Committee. He explained there was more citizen involvement in
Taylorsville’s budgeting process than in any other city he worked with.

He stated that he and his firm had reviewed the governmental activities, business-type activities
(storm drain fund), each major fund, the discretely presented component units (the Arts Council
and the Taylorsville Dayzz Committee) and he did not believe there were any material
misstatements within the financial statements. Included within the audit were comparisons of
records of UDOT, the tax commission, the city’s bank, and bonds.

Mr. Wood commented that it was clear the city had been busy and had undergone many
construction projects. Records showed “other” liabilities of $8.9 million, half of which represented

ARPA grant funds (American Rescue Plan Act) instituted in response to the COVID pandemic.
The city had healthy net positions which had increased. The storm drain fund had increased by
approximately $155,000 and “governmental activities” increased by about $6.2 million. A single
audit was still in progress regarding CARES Act funds but speaking preliminarily, he did not see
a problem with how those funds had been spent. He expected that audit to be complete within a
month.

Mr. Wood explained that the general fund balance was at $6.6 million. The state legislature
recently increased the maximum percentage that a city could hold unassigned within that fund
from 25% to 35% of the total budget. Taylorsville’s was at 20.4%. He had no concerns with the
governmental funds and pointed out that none of them exceeded the budget which had been
previously adopted by the council.

For reporting purposes, the storm drain fund had to borrow $831,000 in cash from the general fund
because there had been such significant capital improvements during the year. Quite a bit of cash
was converted to a capital asset which had subsequently depreciated. The unrestricted portion of
the fund was in a deficit, and this was something that the city should keep an eye on since the
number should be positive rather than negative. It wasn’t an issue yet but should be carefully
watched.

The city’s proportionate share of the liability related to Utah Retirement Systems had gone down
significantly from over $700,000 to $116,000 which was a good thing.

Councilmember Burgess asked that for the benefit of the listening public, Mr. Wood go into more
detail regarding the Storm Water fund, which he did.

Councilmember Armstrong interjected that one of the things the Budget/Audit committee looked
at was the fact that some of the city’s infrastructure was 15 years old. The committee members
had discussed the possibility of creating a replacement fund and/or increasing certain fees in order
to address aging infrastructure. Mr. Harrington responded that the Storm Drain Fund was already
set up to handle aging infrastructure, so he did not recommend increasing fees. It would only be in
a negative cash position for this particular year, although he agreed it was something to keep an
eye on. Mr. Taylor noted that the projects the $4.00 storm drain fee was based on had all been
completed. Therefore, the current fee should cover any future infrastructure improvements.

Mr. Wood stated that based on what he looked at, he did not find any material weaknesses or
significant deficiencies that needed to be reported in accordance with government auditing
standards.

Councilmember Barbieri said that the council’s fiscal responsibility was overwhelming, and she
was very grateful for the expertise of the budget/audit committee and the financial staff.

        6.2      Mid-Year Budget Discussion – Scott Harrington

      Scott Harrington explained that he would email the recommended budget adjustments to the
councilmembers after the meeting, but he would now hit the highlights. The administration wished
to add $5.2 million to the FY21-22 budget, much of which was grant money, an increase in
anticipated sales tax revenue, and money from fund balance for the purchase of real property.

On the expense side, he had transferred funds to the capital projects fund to pay for real property
which was purchased. There was also an increase to public safety salaries, keeping them on pace
with the market. There had been some building expenses and an increased events schedule that
would result in increased expenditure as well. Costs for the telephone system had increased, as had
the costs of parks maintenance. Additionally, some police vehicles that were originally intended
for purchase during the 2020-2021 fiscal year ended up not being available until FY21-22 so that
would result in the need for a budget amendment.

A public hearing would be held during the first city council meeting of January. The council could
choose to vote on the budget adjustments that evening or at a later meeting. Whichever the council
was most comfortable with would be fine. He pointed out that the public would need to have the
actual dollar amounts available to them at the time of the January 5th public hearing.

Councilmember Barbieri asked for further information regarding the “buy back” agreement as it
related to police vehicles. Mr. Harrington explained that a vendor had guaranteed the city a price
to buy the leased vehicles back.

Mr. Harrington hoped to meet with each councilmember individually to go over the proposed
budget amendments and answer any questions they had. Mr. Taylor asked that if there was
anything that councilmembers wished to add to the budget, they bring it up during those meetings
so that the item(s) could be presented to other councilmembers during their own meetings.
