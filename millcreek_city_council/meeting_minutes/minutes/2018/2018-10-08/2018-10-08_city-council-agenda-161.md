2. Discussion on Bonds; John Brems, City Attorney
Mayor Silvestrini said this discussion is about bonds that are posted by developers or
contractors who do work in Millcreek. John Brems said a bond means a surety bond, letter of
credit, financial institution bond, cash, assignment of rights, lien, or other equivalent security
required by a municipality to guarantee the proper completion of landscaping or an
infrastructure improvement. He went through each bond found in Millcreek code with a
proposal from staff for each. An excavation bond is when a contractor digs in the street and
code says that no such permit shall be issued unless and until the applicant therefore has
posted with the engineering division a complete bond, the amount of which is to be
determined by the engineering division. Engineering has proposed that projects under
$10,000 would be allowed a surety bond and a bond greater than that would only be allowed
to be cash, letter of credit or an escrow bond. The Council agreed with this standard. Mayor
Silvestrini said a surety is bond is a contract between a contractor and an insurance company
that says if the contractor does not construct, then the company would pay money to the City.
The other bond forms are cash sitting in a bank waiting for the City to call upon it. Mayor
Silvestrini said the City is reluctant to allow surety bonds because there is almost always a
fight with the insurance company if there as been a default and it becomes expensive to fight
for the money. The other bonds discussed were driveway bond, special events bonds,
highway dedication, storm water quality, flood control, zoning, landscaping /irrigation, and
zoning. Mayor Silvestrini said the same standard proposed of a $10,000 threshold should be
applied for all bonds with the types allowed. Mayor Silvestrini told Brems to make the code
consistent with the types of bonds allowed and to update the referenced department names
accordingly.

1.3 Salt Lake Chamber’s Housing Gap Coalition Presentation

Brynn Mortensen said the Salt Lake Chamber Housing Gap Coalition is a capital city by
name but statewide by mission. There are over 8,000 members in the 29 counties. She
said people are having a hard time finding housing. This is the first time in 40 years that
Utah has more households than housing units, a gap of 54,000 units. Utah is 4" in the
nation for housing price index percent change. The value of a $125,000 home in Utah in
1991 increased to $347,000 by 2017, but at the national growth rate that same home
increased to only $184,000 by 2017. The median home price will be more than $700,000
in the next 26 years if current trends continue. She said there are 5 reasons that housing
prices are increasing: housing shortage, construction and labor costs, local zoning
ordinances and nimbyism, land costs and topography of Wasatch Front counties, and
demographic and economic growth. Utah is leading the nation in job growth. The
Housing Gap Coalition is trying to get ahead of the situation. They are running various
programs to teach construction and trades to high schoolers, running a public awareness
campaign for people to plan to address growth to maintain quality of life, and they are
meeting with cities along the Wasatch Front to ask them to sign a support resolution
which states they are committed to doing their part in smart growth.

Mayor Silvestrini said Millcreek has been taking advantage of grants from the Wasatch
Front Regional Council to create a general plan, a town center, and to do things that fit
into the definition of smart growth which means planning for growth and putting density
where it can be tolerated without affecting the great neighborhoods. Mayor Silvestrini
said this resolution talks about what Millcreek is already doing. Millcreek basically has
no impact fees and does not add to the cost of construction. He thinks training skilled
tradesman in the housing industry is excellent. Millcreek is trying to plan nodes of
density where there is infrastructure and transportation. He said Utah’s population is
expected to double in the next 50 years and most of the growth is internal. Mayor
Silvestrini said the State legislature said they would take over local control to address the
situation, but he thinks that is the wrong answer. Millcreek wants to preserve what is
worth preserving but plan for the future.
