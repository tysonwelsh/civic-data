2.      A presentation by the Intermountain Utah Valley Hospital administration (22-073)
        (0:20:54)

Kyle Hansen, Administrator of Utah Valley Hospital, presented. Intermountain Healthcare is
changing their name to Intermountain Health, which is intended to emphasize their overall focus
of prevention and care. They currently do not have a permanent CEO, but they hope to have a
leader named before the end of the year. Their mission is to help people live the healthiest lives
possible and they hope to continue to be a model health system. Intermountain Health’s current
footprint includes much of the intermountain west—Nevada, Utah, Idaho, Montana, Colorado,
Wyoming, Kansas, and parts of Arizona and New Mexico. Mr. Hansen also briefly reviewed the
membership of Intermountain Health’s various boards.

Mr. Hansen reviewed some statistics of the Utah Valley Hospital and their service areas in Utah
County. The hospital is a level 2 trauma center and they have seen a significant uptick of 37%
more trauma cases since 2019. They need to resource this growth properly in order to continue
providing excellent levels of care. He shared their vision for the future and what they hope to
develop and accomplish. They are currently constructing a children’s hospital in Lehi; the
pediatrics floor at Utah Valley Hospital will be moving to Lehi once that facility is operational.
As with many industries, they are facing some workforce challenges including struggling to find
qualified candidates to fill roles at the hospital, as well as to retain those employees, many of
whom have highly specialized skillsets.

Councilor David Shipley asked if any of the staffing relates to anything the City is involved with,
such as housing or living costs. Mr. Hansen said they are trying to change the economic model of
healthcare to one where their organization takes on full financial and clinical risk to be able to
truly serve the community for overall health. Their organization is looking at patient populations
as well to examine the types of social determinants impacting them. Mr. Hansen was not sure of
specific issues impacted by the City but there were many complex factors at play.

Intermountain Health is a not-for-profit operation; they do not have shareholders and are not
accountable to provide specific earnings—what they earn is invested back into their services and
the community to better serve their patients. Mr. Hansen outlined information about their charity
care program—in addition to providing charity care, they also help connect patients with

3.      A presentation regarding conservation easements. (22-079) (0:47:46)

Wendy Fisher, Utah Open Lands Executive Director, presented. Council Chair George Handley
provided some context on his request for this presentation. Ms. Fisher indicated that the scenery
and recreation opportunities in Utah are a huge draw for businesses to locate to the area. Ms.
Fisher outlined some reasons why the City might want to consider conservation easements as a
tool to preserve open space in the community. Utah Open Lands works with various private and
public landowners and community organizations to create conservation easements. Ms. Fisher
highlighted several recent examples in the Utah County area.

Some conservation easements are sought as a tax deduction tool. Other conservation easements
may be enacted to preserve the scenic, historic, and natural resources of a property. Ms. Fisher
discussed some of the market forces impacting and resulting from conservation easements. A
conservation easement generally results in net revenue for the city, as these sites typically do not
require many services. She also explained the importance of determining a vision for the
property itself along with a management plan. She invited questions from the Council.

Mr. Handley had several questions. He asked about the valuation baseline documentation that
Utah Open Lands would offer—Ms. Fisher outlined several ways they could go about doing this.
Certain types of land uses are not compatible with conservation, and in those cases other tools
such as a deed restriction may be more appropriate to accomplish the desired result. Mr. Handley
invited other questions from the Council. He asked if the Council was comfortable with the
Natural Resources Committee exploring this topic further to see whether the City wants to do a
land inventory as part of the canyon and foothills plan. He wanted to seek some guidance from
the Council about whether this was a priority. Councilor David Shipley asked if this would be
done in conjunction with planning efforts on the Provo River, and some of those properties
would be more of a priority to him. He was interested in what the committee would propose. Mr.

4.      A discussion on housing in Provo (22-072) (1:21:16)

Dave Harding, a community member and former Councilor, presented. Council Chair George
Handley indicated that Mr. Harding had been wanting to share some thoughts for quite some
time but due to scheduling constraints, it had not happened sooner. Mr. Harding indicated that
since moving to Provo, he has taken an interest in housing and how it impacts people as well as
the people who move here. Mr. Harding referred to the handouts which were included in the
meeting packet. Mr. Harding reviewed a slideshow from a presentation given in 2017 by Carrie
Walls, the City’s former Zoning Administrator before her retirement, with annotations he had
added and he shared his commentary on these housing topics. Mr. Harding briefly responded to
small clarifying questions from Councilors throughout his presentation. Mr. Harding outlined
several aspects to the paradigm through which he views housing issues. He thought it was
important to simplify Provo’s approach to housing—if it is easier for the public to understand, it
is easier to comply with and enforce. He also thought it was important to encourage attainable
housing for a cross-section of the community as well as encourage homeownership by protecting
ownership opportunities. Mr. Harding elaborated on this by explaining some of the research
shared by Dr. James Wood, Ivory-Boyer Senior Fellow at the Gardner Policy Institute, which
shows that many Americans generate their lifelong wealth through home ownership. Mr.
Harding wanted to make sure that Provo residents across all walks of life had opportunities to
afford homes when they were ready and wanting to do so.

Mr. Harding shared more of his suggested high-level approach. His idea was that certain housing
types should be constructed for certain types of owners—twin homes, townhomes, and condos
should be constructed for owner-occupants. Multifamily units such as duplexes or apartments are
designed for investors. Then the general approach should examine the intended ownership status

5.      A presentation of the Consolidated Annual Performance and Evaluation Report
        (CAPER) for the 21-22 Fiscal Year (22-038) (2:05:14)

Melissa McNalley, Community Grant Coordinator, presented. The Consolidated Annual
Performance and Evaluation Report is an annual reporting requirement that summarizes projects
done during the fiscal year for Community Development Block Grant funds. Ms. McNalley
summarized projects which were funded through CDBG and HOME. She highlighted the
accomplishments of the projects, which resulted in 17 jobs created or retained, three businesses
assisted, four homeowner houses built, nine homeowner houses rehabilitated, and 15 families
which received first-time-home-buyer financial assistance. She also highlighted statistics for the
infrastructure and public services-related projects. Ms. McNalley also highlighted overall
progress towards their five-year plan.

Councilor David Shipley asked if the five-year plan is something they revisit. Ms. McNalley
explained that every year when they complete the annual action plan, they set up goals to help
meet each of the strategic five-year goals to move them further along in accomplishing those.
She clarified that the five-year goals are static, and they try to change their annual targets to
result in the best progress towards the goals. Mr. Shipley acknowledged the amount of work that
goes into this and thanked Ms. McNalley for her work. Councilor Rachel Whipple felt some of
the five-year goals felt very modest. Ms. McNalley was fairly certain that the five-year goals
were set based on past performance in order to be more realistic. Ms. Whipple suggested some
goal categories seemed like low-hanging fruit—Mr. Shipley had mentioned direct financial
assistance and she agreed it would be good to work on making that more accessible. Ms.
Whipple asked how the financial requirements were set; Ms. McNalley indicated that these were
set by HUD. Councilor Bill Fillmore asked what the public facilities infrastructure and public
service activities categories were measuring, and Ms. McNalley said these figures are reporting
the numbers of population served. For an area that benefits, they use the population figure for the
relevant Census tracts. Councilor George Handley suggested that general public information and
education about this would be very helpful. Presentation only.

7.      A discussion regarding the proposed "Percent-for-the-Arts" program from the
        Public Art Committee. (22-061) (2:31:08)

Melia Dayley, Policy Analyst, presented. The Public Art Committee presented in the spring on
this policy draft; there have been some changes since then, which have been delayed due to staff
shortages, but they are bringing a draft to present today. There are eight sections of the policy,
beginning with the purpose and goal of the policy, which is intended to set up a designated
source of funding to facilitate and promote public art in the city. The second section outlines
definitions relevant to the policy. Ms. Dayley reviewed several definitions including “project”
and “art.” The third section outlines the creation of the program, including the collection and use
of appropriations. Of note, Ms. Dayley explained that the 1% to be directed towards public art
would not apply to federal or grant funding. She also noted that the 1% applies to just the
construction budget. The program utilizes an Administrative process, which involves the Arts
Council as a recommending body before appropriations come to the Council.

Councilor David Shipley clarified that the funds for percent-for-the-arts go to a designated
account. Ms. Dayley clarified that the account is a separate account within the Covey Center, as
the Covey Center director will be involved in administering the funds. Ms. Dayley continued
reviewing the provisions for creation of the program. Councilor George Handley asked if there
was a grant program where available funding could be applied for and allocated. Ms. Dayley said
that specific procedures would be left to the Arts Council. For leftover funds, or if a project has
an exemption, then there would be a continuing pool of money for ongoing art project
applications. Mr. Handley also asked about the definition of art—he had a question about
whether decorative arts (functional objects like a lamppost) or other types of architectural
enhancements would be permitted. Ms. Dayley said the definition was broad enough that it
allowed for flexibility in how the definition was applied.

Councilor Bill Fillmore asked who had the ultimate decision authority on the quality of the art.
Ms. Dayley indicated the Arts Council had authority over the art and the City Council has
discretion about appropriating the funds. Mr. Fillmore indicated that art is in the eye of the
beholder. Councilor Rachel Whipple commented on the Arts Council. She also addressed
community values and standards in subsection 5(4)(i); given that the people on the Arts Council
are members of the community, she trusted that they could exercise good judgment. She noted
that as a City Council, they aren’t making value judgments on art; there is some separation
between the political body and the art body for that reason. Mr. Fillmore wanted to know what
safeguards were in place. Ms. Whipple pointed out that the appointment process of the Arts
Council (a mayoral appointment with Council advice and consent) was the first layer of

a built-in funding source for maintenance needs. Ms. Dayley invited any questions or direction

Council meetings. Mr. Handley liked the policy and thought it was ready for a vote.

across the state, they have had to make some adjustments. The State of Utah has a robust
program. They are committed as a committee to review, evaluate, and make changes if needed.
Downtown Provo Inc. has done dozens of murals in the downtown area on a shoestring budget.

also thanked Mr. Jones for his input and contributions. Presentation only. This item will be
