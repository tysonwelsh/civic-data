4.0   Discussion: UTOPIA Fiber – Roger Timmerman

1.1   Roger Timmerman, UTOPIA Fiber, thanked the Council for the opportunity to speak with
      them. He said that he felt that UTOPIA Fiber was an exciting opportunity and that he
      hoped that some useful information was provided to the city to help with some of its future
      planning.

      Mr. Timmerman explained that UTOPIA was an interlocal entity of cities. He said that if
      Draper was interested in a fiber network for its community, UTOPIA was a good partner
      to make that happen. Draper would essentially be partnering with other cities that were
      investing in fiber. He was forthcoming about UTOPIA’s history. From 2004-2008 several
      cities had to backpay debt on projects because they didn’t get enough customers. However,
      in 2009, there was a turning point when the Utah Infrastructure Agency (UIA) was created
      and led to the most successful fiber project in the country. The UIA resulted in $230
      million of fiber projects that have all been paid for completely by subscriber revenues with
      no cost to the city.

      Mr. Timmerman explained that almost all Utah broadband companies use UTOPIA. This
      works similarly to open access roads: municipalities invest in the infrastructure that
      becomes a public resource. UTOPIA builds the fiber network and then lets different
      companies use the fiber network. That is how providers can offer services to different
      markets.

      Utopia charges the end-user $30 a month for the service for the fiber in addition to the
      provider options. In the end, you get a 250 Mbps connection for $65/month, a 1 Gbps for
      $78/month, and a 10 Gbps connection (the fastest in the country) for $200/month.

      From a city perspective, the city should see it as its infrastructure. A company is not
      coming into a city. The city is a facilitator to help build a fiber infrastructure. The city
      gets to use it and can allow it to do all sorts of smart city applications such as smart
      irrigation, the PurpleAir air quality sensors, and wildfire sensors.

      Mr. Timmerman then outlined what a partnership with UIA would look like for a city. He
      said that this model had been working. Since 2009 UIA was 10 for 10. All of their projects
      had been successful in that UIA had taken on the debt and the city had no cost. The catch
      is that the city is a financing partner with UIA with an associated guarantee. UIA takes on
      dept, utility locating, repairs, the backbone of interconnectivity, etc. and the city pledges a
      40% take rate. If there is a shortfall on subscriptions, the city then loans the amount, which
      is paid back when UIA gets enough customers. This means that every city that takes on
      one of these projects is financially responsible for the project they take on. It is a risk
      management measure. He explained that one of the ways to mitigate a city’s risk is that as

they develop more properties they are not taking on rate backing, but those properties
account towards the city’s revenue.

Mr. Timmerman made that claim that the investment, even at $40 million was worth it for
the city because it lowered everyone’s rates through the introduction of new competition,
improved property values, and protected net neutrality.

As part of the process, UIA puts a two-year capitalized interest into financing. This means
that the city’s obligation doesn’t start for two years, allowing UIA to build a network and
ramp-up. Advanced revenues buffer against a shortfall. This buys UIA five years before
they need revenues. He said that with other cities, they have broken even a lot faster than
five years. Typically, UIA gets enough revenues before the obligation even begins and
doesn’t even need to use the buffer. He referenced a $23 million project with Layton that
went well.

UIA looked at the feasibility for Draper to invest in a fiber network. Some of the
advantages for Draper included:

       •   Existing UTOPIA fiber backbone within the city that would allow for faster
           buildout, revenue coverage, and a lower project cost.
       •   Significant business revenue potential to offset risk.
       •   Similar city take-rates all exceed the level Draper would be asked to guarantee.
       •   Draper’s future development lowered the risk to the city over time.

There would be a higher construction cost due to rock. Overall, Mr. Timmerman believed
the project would be very low risk. UTOPIA went ahead and designed the whole network,
even though that planning would typically result from an expensive feasibility study.
UTOPIA designed every connection, box, and neighborhood so that they could come to
Draper with a robust and accurate proposal. The result of this planning was a $39 million
project cost. The monthly guarantee comes out to $195,000. Although this sounds like a
lot of money, because UTOPIA charges $30 a month, Draper would only need 6,500
subscriptions or a 39.2% take rate to cover the obligation. This take rate would be lowered
to 36.2% with businesses and city revenue. There would be a 25-year term to pay back
any debt beginning after the two-year buffer. If a shortfall in the loan occurred, that money
would be paid back to the city when they reached sufficient revenues, or at the end of the
bond period, whichever is sooner. UIA was obligated to pay for expansion, additional
installations, annexations, upgrades, all repair maintenance, with no guarantee from the
city.

Mr. Timmerman outlined the process for moving forward. If the city approved a contract
with UIA, they would guarantee project completion in three years, though most projects
had been completed in less time. He then provided some options for what Draper could do
if they did not feel ready to commit to building a fiber network, but wanted to set
themselves up for the future. He said that the city could invest in some routes to public
facilities and provide Wi-Fi in the parks. He said that a lot of cities have found that they
could take what they were currently paying and move that to UTOPIA who could build

      additional fiber routes that UTOPIA gets paid back from the normal revenue. This
      becomes public infrastructure to offset costs down the road for fiber. Mr. Timmerman also
      noted that there were opportunities to take advantage of other funding if Draper felt like
      taking on a 39% take rate was too much risk. He noted that in looking at other cities
      compared to Draper they were well above that percentage. However, there were ways to
      offset the cost, and the necessary take-rate, with other opportunities. These included the
      $50 million the state recently budgeted for broadband, CARES Act funding and the next
      stimulus act, and the inclusion of conduit/fiber in other city infrastructure projects.

1.2   Councilmember Roberts said that there were a lot of competing broadband technologies.
      He said that Draper would be on the hook for 25 years to cover any shortfall. He asked
      what other technologies Mr. Timmerman says are out there could potentially replace fiber
      optics. He said that one of his concerns was that the technology could become obsolete in
      25 years that the city would be required to continue paying for.

      Mr. Timmerman said that the devices that are currently connected to the fiber network
      would probably only be relevant for the next ten years. The fiber itself, however, was good
      for 50+ years. He said that fiber itself essentially had infinite capacity. They were able to
      offer 10 Gbps service today that was ten times faster than anything else available on the
      market. Faster service was still possible. Other technologies were scrambling to keep up
      where service was lousy. He said that wireless wasn’t able to provide service across the
      entire city. He said that satellite was even worse. He said that he had lots of slides that
      addressed all of the competing technologies and why they were not long-term viable
      competitors to fiber. He said that they love those other technologies because UTOPIA can
      sell to them. He said that nearly all of the wireless providers in Utah use UTOPIA fiber.
      He said that UTOPIA enabled those services. However, in terms of service to homes,
      nothing beats fiber, and UTOPIA’s costs were lower than other providers. He said that
      from a long-term perspective fiber was cheaper because it had a longer lifespan.

1.3   Councilmember F. Lowry wondered why UIA needed to partner with Draper City to come
      and start providing the service to residents and businesses. If projects were as successful
      as they appeared to be, he wanted to know why UIA would need to partner with the city.

      Mr. Timmerman said that the demand was there, they knew they would get the customers
      and knew the infrastructure would pay for itself under the model. He said that the reason
      why it was profitable and worked under the model was that UIA was able to take advantage
      of tax-exempt bonds, long-term financing because they are a public entity. If an
      organization is a public entity, then they rely on public financing. UIA was not motivated
      by profit, but the collective of cities did not have any motivation to take on a project in
      another city.

4.0   Update: Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District – Reed Gibby

4.1   Reed Gibby, representative of Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District, thanked the
      Council for the opportunity to provide an update for the water district. He said that he was
      very impressed by the organization and professionals of the district. He turned the
      presentation over to Bart Forsyth, the district’s general manager.

4.2   Bart Forsyth, Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District General Manager, outlined water-
      related issues that pertained to Draper City. The first item the district wanted to touch on
      was a summary of some recent board decisions and emerging trends. To begin, Mr. Forsyth
      showed the cities and municipalities included in the district. He said that the Jordan Valley
      Water Conservancy mainly provided wholesale drinking water to those in the district.
      They served a population of about 750,000 people. Two reservoirs–Jordanelle and Deer
      Creek–store most of Jordan Valley’s annual water supply. Eighty percent (80%) of
      supplied water is delivered through the Provo River System. The district was governed by
      a board of nine trustees that represented eight geographical divisions within the service
      area. Mr. Gibby represented both Draper and Midvale City. The water conservancy district
      offered three key services: delivering water, managing demand, and developing supply.
      The water conservancy district has determined that to maintain water supply in the future,
      significant changes were required in the landscape through new construction moving
      forward. Additionally, they believed that it was imperative to develop new sources of
      supply as outlined in their 50-year plan.

      Mr. Forsyth outlined some of the parameters of Draper City’s water contract. Currently,
      Draper’s minimum purchase amount was 3,800 acre-feet (AF) plus a 20% allowance
      totaling 760 AF, or a total of 4,560 AF. He noted that Draper had stayed within that
      threshold for some time, but that in 2020 the threshold was exceeded. 2020 was very dry
      and warmer than average, so there were significant water deliveries made not only to
      Draper City but to all agencies within the district. The district had met with Draper’s staff
      and made the suggestion that the city probably needed to think about a purchase contract
      increase to keep the city’s contract current with demands.

      The Jordan Water Conservancy District continues to experience explosive growth and is
      predicting a 70% population increase from the present day to 2065. To address this, the
      district has determined that landscaping needed to be completed in a more water-wise
      fashion. Jordan Valley had already been working with Draper staff on adopting efficiency
      standards to ensure a reliable water supply in the future. Mr. Forsyth also displayed the

                                         Page 10 of 24
      average temperature reading at the Salt Lake International Airport since 1948 to
      demonstrate the steady increase in temperatures. He described it as an alarming trend
      because as temperatures increase, plants require more water. This was especially a concern
      because as the temperature continues to increase, it makes it much harder to achieve water
      conservation goals. A primary area the district is focusing on is the reduction of turfgrass.
      The higher temperatures also exacerbate drought conditions. At this point, Mr. Forsyth
      turned the presentation over the Matt Olsen.

4.3   Matthew Olsen, Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District Assistant General Manager,
      addressed water usage throughout the year. In the graph he displayed, he pointed out the
      steady use of indoor water use throughout the year, which accounted for 40% of total
      annual water use. The other 60% of water use accounted for outdoor water use that peaks
      during June, July, and August. This significant use was why Jordan Valley was focused
      on outdoor efficiency.

      He then talked about why the water district was looking at water development out until
      2065. He said that this 50-year assessment brought the Board of Trustees of Jordan Valley
      to come up with some direction and adoption of policy changes.

      Jordan Valley had invested over $700 million in its water system. One of Jordan Valley’s
      goals was to keep water rate adjustments relatively stable from year to year. One recent
      development was the creation of a water revenue stabilization fund. The district uses
      income from last year’s operations and puts it into a fund to offset future rate adjustments.
      The fund was used heavily in 2020 creating a 0% increase in water rates overall.

      Another major effort that would help lower the cost of water by deferring really expensive
      projects and reducing unnecessary complications is the idea of outdoor water efficiency
      standards. If the district can focus on new construction, where landscaping could be
      installed the right way at the beginning, the district saves about five times the cost of going
      out later and ripping out the inefficient landscaping. There were two ways that the
      standards were being considered. The first way was to require any new annexation that
      came into the district that was outside of the current service boundaries would be subject
      to the outdoor water and efficiency standards and a Block 2, or higher water rate.

      Jordan Valley has also focused on water purchasing contracts. Beginning on January 1,
      2021, any water taken over one hundred twenty percent (120%) of an allotment is subject
      to a higher Block 2 rate. That Block 2 rates will likely be double the current rate. To avoid
      that Block 2 rates, a new contract, or newly amended contract could go through and receive
      Block 1 rates if the entity had adopted the water efficiency standards.

6.1   Todd Draper, planner, explained that part of the impetus driving the proposed change was
      that the city was starting to see negative impacts occurring to site circulation when
      secondary or dual uses repetitively or routinely block access to on-site parking. He said
      that staff also felt that some updates to the terminology used in the ordinance should be
      consistent between sections and updates should eliminate ambiguous or duplicative terms.
      Lastly, there was an outdated reference to the Unified Fire Authority that needed to be
      updated and corrected to reference the Draper City Fire Department.

      The items were taken to the Planning Commission on January 14, 2021, with a
      recommendation for approval with additional modifications to the text on a 5-0 vote. The
      updated ordinance with the proposed changes included had already been provided by the
      City Recorder.

      The main piece that was added appears in section 9-25-03(D) which added parking to be
      provided and to continue to be available. The section of code specifies that “designated
      parking spaces shall be used solely for the parking or storage of one motor vehicle,” and
      that “dual-use of parking spaces for public safety and emergency access aisles, internal
      vehicular circulation aisles, queuing lanes or similar uses is prohibited.”




                                        Page 21 of 24
      In section 9-25-060: Number and Type of Parking Spaces, the primary change to the code
      was to eliminate the term stacking and include the preference of the term queuing. The
      previous ordinance used both terms and implied that they were similar causing ambiguity.
      The team felt that queuing was the correct term.

      In section 9-25-080 in regards to parking area design and construction, the language was
      made to queuing and there was a reference to the appropriate widths as outlined in a table
      to clarify the necessary spaces required. In section K, public safety access was updated
      from the Unified Fire Authority to the Draper City Fire Marshal.

      Mr. Draper then displayed sample motions, including the motion in the middle that would
