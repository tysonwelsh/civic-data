PUBLIC COMMENT PERIOD
Cheniel Hill, founder and senior officer of SAFE (Stand Against Fear and Exploitation),
addressed the council to advocate for stronger accountability for sex offender registry
violations. She highlighted a recent incident involving Wanda Barzee, who violated her
registry conditions by entering public parks, and criticized Utah law for treating such
violations as misdemeanors. Hill introduced the Sex Offender Registry Violation
Accountability Act, which would reclassify these violations as felonies. She requested the
mayor and council members to submit letters of support for the proposed legislation,
emphasizing the need to protect children and enforce real consequences for offenders.
SAFE is building a statewide coalition to support the bill.

Troy Muir, representing Gerald Wright STEM Academy, spoke on behalf of 25 students
who identified an issue with the neglected vacant lot between their school and Foxtail Park.
As part of an Academic Service Learning project, the students proposed transforming the
area—currently overgrown and hazardous—into a butterfly garden or an educational green
space. They documented their findings, conducted surveys, and created a hallway replica
of their vision. Muir presented a booklet with their proposals and requested that the city
council and mayor review the students' ideas for potential action.

Kirk Lowe addressed ongoing concerns about the difficulty in securing tee times at local
golf courses, particularly during peak seasons. He referenced a news report from San Diego
where bots were being used to reserve tee times through the same "ForeUp" system used
locally, with those times resold on secondary markets. To combat this, some courses
implemented a $10 hold fee, refundable upon play or forfeited if unused. Lowe suggested
West Valley consider similar measures. He also raised concerns about league bookings
occupying most weekdays and the possibility of individuals reserving multiple tee times,
further limiting public access. He urged the city to investigate and consider solutions.

Diann Rakadroka, representing the Community Education Partnership (CEP) of West
Valley City, spoke in support of renewing CEP’s contract. As the coordinator at Granger
Elementary, she emphasized the program's critical role in supporting at-risk students and
families, particularly during after-school hours. Rakadroka shared examples of students
facing crises, including gang exposure and traumatic home situations, who rely on the

program for safety, stability, and academic support. She noted the high demand for the
program and its positive impact on students’ academic performance and well-being.
Rakadroka urged the council to continue funding CEP to ensure continued support for
vulnerable children and families.

Kilinn Oldroyd, also representing the Community Education Partnership (CEP) of West
Valley City, urged the council to renew CEP’s grant. With 12 years of experience running
an after-school program at Silver Hills Elementary, she shared the profound impact the
program has had on over 600 students. Oldroyd emphasized the importance of after-school
care during critical hours when children are often unsupervised due to parents working
multiple jobs. She described how students actively seek out and return to the program,
noting its role in preventing youth from engaging in gangs, drugs, and crime. Drawing from
her work at youth services, she reinforced that many troubled youth lack guidance and
support—something CEP helps to provide. Oldroyd ended with a heartfelt plea to continue
the funding, describing how difficult it was to part with students without a clear plan for
the program’s future.

Peggy Sue Coates spoke in support of the Community Education Partnership after-school
program. With over 25 years of experience working with junior high students on the west
side of West Valley, she highlighted the ongoing need for structured, supervised after-
school care. Coates emphasized that many children are left waiting after school due to
parents' work schedules or lack of transportation. She noted that unsupervised time often
leads to vandalism and other trouble. Through her role teaching art in the program, she has
provided a creative and safe outlet for students, including those who lack resources or safe
environments at home. Coates stressed that while these students may not have perfect
grades, they benefit tremendously from the support and security the program offers. She
concluded by underscoring the peace of mind it gives working parents, knowing their
children are safe and engaged after school.

Mike Markham, a veteran, addressed the Council to express his appreciation for the City's
Memorial Day program held on Monday. Markham shared that it was the first such event
he had ever attended, having avoided them in the past due to difficult memories from his
service. He made a special effort to return early from his mountain cabin to attend and
thanked Councilmembers Mr. Pili, Don Christensen, Karen Lang, and Will Whetstone for
their presence at the event. Markham emphasized the importance of showing support to
veterans—not just those who have died or are POWs, but also those who return home and
struggle in silence. He shared personal reflections on his mental health journey, including
the positive impact of finally choosing to take medication, though he expressed regret for
not doing so earlier while his wife was still alive. Markham concluded by urging the
Council to continue standing in support of veterans and thanked them again for their
attendance.

Kason Kendall, a teacher coach at Valley Junior High, spoke in support of continued
funding for after-school programs. He expressed gratitude for the Council’s past support
and highlighted the positive impact these programs have on students. Kendall specifically
referenced his experience with the Money Club, where students learn essential life skills
such as entrepreneurship, distinguishing needs from wants, and the risks of payday loan
companies—especially relevant in the West Valley and Taylorsville communities.
He emphasized that after-school programs offer a safe, stable environment and noted their
broader reach compared to athletics alone. These programs provide diverse opportunities
like art, Legos, and academic help, benefitting students with varied interests. Kendall also
praised a bilingual presenter from Granite Credit Union who was able to engage both
English and Spanish-speaking students simultaneously, which he found especially valuable
given the financial literacy requirements in high school and the lack of exposure many
students have at home. He closed by reiterating his thanks and urging the Council to
continue funding these critical programs.

Ryan Mahoney, a resident of West Valley City in District 1, introduced himself to the
Council, the Mayor, and the City Manager. He announced his intention to run for a City
Council At-Large seat in the near future. Mahoney expressed his desire to serve the
community and to build relationships with the Council as he prepares for his candidacy.

Jeffrey Crosby, who works as an aide in the after school program at Silver Hills Elementary
School, expressed concern about moving students to different buildings for after school
programs. He emphasized that the current buildings feel like a home to the children and
that the familiar staff and environment provide them comfort and security. Crosby shared
that the relationships formed between staff and students are strong, almost like family
bonds, which contribute to the children's well-being. He requested that students be allowed
to remain in their current after school program locations to maintain this sense of stability,
safety, and connection.

Kenzie Simpkins, aide at Silver Hills Elementary School after school program, spoke about
her 10+ years of involvement, starting as a volunteer at age 16. She highlighted the strong
commitment between staff and students, emphasizing how the program positively impacts

     public comments regarding application ZT-1-2025, Filed by West Valley City,
     Requesting a Zone Text Amendment to Section 7-10-103(2) to Address Standards for

       Written documentation previously provided to the City Council included
       information as follows:

               Section 7-10-103(2) currently requires the following:

       (2) Fifty percent of the area of the landscaped buffer shall be planted with
       grass. Artificial turf that meets the standards in Section 7-2-127 may be used
       in lieu of grass. The remaining 50 percent of the area shall include at least 50
       percent live plant material and may include specialty paving, Street furniture,

       percent live plant material coverage, not including tree canopies. Artificial
       turf that meets the standards in Section 7-2-127 may be used in lieu of grass
       and shall not exceed 50% of the area.

       b. Seventy-five percent live plant material coverage, not including tree
       canopies, with a combination of deciduous and evergreen plant material and
       at least two Mineral Ground Cover or Mulch varieties provided throughout
       the area. Twenty-five percent of the plants must be a minimum of 5 gallon.

Caden Sumptin stated that he lives across from Monroe Elementary and expressed
concerns about safety and access to 3100 South due to traffic congestion during
school hours. He shared that it can take up to 30 minutes to exit his driveway
because of heavy traffic and the presence of children crossing the sidewalk. To
address this, he installed a gravel roundabout driveway in his front yard after calling
City Hall and being told gravel would be permitted as landscaping. After
completing the driveway last November and using it throughout the winter, he
recently received a citation stating gravel was not permitted—contradicting the
information he was given. He reached out to Representative Matt McPherson, who
had previously visited during the driveway's construction and supported its purpose
for safety and water conservation. Caden asked the council to allow him to keep
the gravel driveway, citing improved safety for children, reduced risk of accidents,
and less water usage. He mentioned halting further landscaping due to the citation
and reiterated his hope for a resolution allowing the driveway to remain in place.

Written documentation previously provided to the City Council included
information as follows:

   The applicant is proposing to demolish the two existing homes on the property and
   construct a 26-unit, two-story rental townhome development with front-loaded
   garages. The applicant’s original proposal included 49 units which were potentially
   possible through the use of the 2-acre Rocky Mountain Power owned parcel to the
   north for access, parking, and recreation space. However, the applicant was
   unsuccessful in obtaining ownership or a perpetual easement from Rocky Mountain
   Power, so the project was scaled back to the property the applicant has under
   contract. The proposed density of the project (excluding the Rocky Mountain
   Power property) is 10.4 units/acre. All units include 3-bedrooms and two-car
   garages. All garages have driveways that are deep enough to park in. By way of
   background, DAI developed the H2O Lofts project at 2864 S Decker Lake Dr. The
   townhomes proposed on the subject property are similar to those at the H2O Lofts
   project. The following points are provided for reference:

       •   There are eight developed or approved townhome developments that front
           or have access on Redwood Road.
       •   As part of the City’s General Plan update effort, staff discussed this
           property, along with other properties along Redwood Road, with both the
           Planning Commission and City Council. Both the Planning Commission
           and City Council indicated that they were supportive of townhomes at this
           location.
       •   Redwood Road is currently a 7-lane arterial street that is serviced by UTA
           bus route 217.
       •   Average annual daily traffic (AADT) on this segment of Redwood Road
           was 26,000 in 2023. A 7-lane arterial can handle a traffic volume of 40,000
           at a level of service (LOS) C and 46,000 at a LOS D.
