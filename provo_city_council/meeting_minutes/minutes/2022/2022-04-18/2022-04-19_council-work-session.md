        Year Consolidated Plan. (22-038) (0:27:06)

Department of Housing and Urban Development, and how the city receives CDBG and HOME

Services) are representatives on the committee. Presentation only. This item was already
scheduled for the Council Meeting on April 19, 2022.

7.      A presentation regarding the FY2023 Budget- Vehicle Fleet (including vehicle
        replacement) (22-016) (1:06:47)

Warren Merritt, Fleet Manager, presented. Each area under Public Works has special vehicle and
equipment needs. The fleet advisory committee includes individuals from several departments
and makes recommendations based on the needs of the fleet and of individual departments. Mr.
Merritt highlighted best practices for fleet vehicle replacement cycles.

Councilor Rachel Whipple asked about increasing gas prices versus the increased cost for
electric vehicles. Mr. Merritt indicated the cost would be eventually offset by the savings for fuel
costs. Councilor George Handley asked for a more detailed estimate of the costs and savings.
The cost difference seems to be changing rapidly whereas the presentation only reflects standard
prices. Mr. Merritt said the City has two electric vehicles right now as well as 20 hybrid vehicles
in the Police Department right now; Police plans to add 17 more this coming fiscal year. This is a
great option for the Police Department since those vehicles do a lot of idling. In response to a
question from Mr. Handley, Mr. Merritt indicated that the current fleet replacement budget does
not factor in the addition of more police officers—these projections would need to be updated
accordingly if there were more officers in the city. Jimmy McKnight, Public Works Budget
Analyst, clarified that the fleet replacement list includes only replacements to the current fleet.
When a police officer is added in the budget, they work with them to make sure the associated
costs are added to the related part of the budget. The fleet replacement fund includes all vehicle
purchases in the city, then fleet essentially charges the cost of the loan to the department where a
vehicle was purchased. Mr. McKnight addressed forecasting for fuel budgets—their current
forecast results in an overall increase of $640,000 to citywide fuel budgets.

Ms. Whipple asked about the fuel budget versus fleet budget. Mr. McKnight indicated that each
department pays for its own fuel and its own maintenance. She also asked about the vehicle
selection for certain uses and if Fleet has considered any alternatives to vehicles. Mr. Merritt

8.      A presentation regarding the FY2023 Budget- Energy (22-016) (1:54:15)

Travis Ball, Provo Power Director, presented. Provo Power’s goal is to be the safest and most
reliable energy company in the country. Mr. Ball shared highlights of their strategic plan and
reviewed the core values of Provo Power. They appreciate the support that the Council and
Administration have given them—they have enough funds to provide reliable services and have
not had to submit supplemental requests. They also maintain a very proactive capital
improvements plan and schedule—over the last decade, they have invested over $60 million into
capital projects. Mr. Ball highlighted the supply chain issues impacting their operations. He also
highlighted some efforts for conservation and sustainability, including their LEED-certified
building, hybrid vehicles, public electric vehicle charging stations, and community solar project.

Mr. Ball reviewed Provo Power’s resource mix from FY20 compared to FY23. A total of 15% of
energy now comes from solar. Coal has decreased quite a bit and hydroelectric sources have also
decreased due to water shortages throughout the state. Mr. Ball shared details of several of the
department’s conservation programs, including GreenXchange, SharedSolar, and rebates. The
department also powers 5934 city streetlights at no charge to the City’s General Fund (in
comparison, Orem residents have a $3/month charge for streetlights from the power company).
Provo Power also maintains 55,000 trees in the city.

Ned Hill, Energy Board Member, said he has served on many organizations and he did not know
of one better-managed than the Provo Energy Department. He complimented several aspects of
the Provo Power operations, including competitive rates (despite equipment and fuel increases)
and high reliability. The Energy Board went over this presentation in great detail at the last board
meeting and they are recommending a 2% rate increase to keep rates at pace for now. A gentle
rate increase will keep the department’s goals realistic and insight. The Energy Board voted
unanimously to recommend a 2% rate increase for the coming year. Mr. Ball indicated their cost
of service study recommended a very small increase for the next 3 years. A residential rate
comparison with other cities shows Provo as right in the middle (and less than Rocky Mountain
Power). The commercial/industrial rate is close to the bottom of the comparison range.

Kat Linford, Management Analyst, and Scott Bunker, Assistant Energy Director, did a
demonstration on how the Energy budget is set up and allocated. Councilor David Shipley asked

10.     A presentation regarding the FY2023 Budget- Legal (22-016) (2:48:47)

Brian Jones, City Attorney, presented. He highlighted the different divisions and what they do:
      City Legal Services
      Claims, Litigation, Risk Management, and Safety
      Criminal Prosecution
Mr. Jones clarified for the public that the City’s legal department is not a law firm for the city’s
citizens. Their non-personnel operating budget is only 5% of their total budget, with 95% of their
budget going toward personnel. Since 1996, in the last 28 years they have added only 2.5
positions—adding two positions and one paralegal, while reducing one half-time employee.
These increases have been occasioned by additional responsibilities being given to their office
(including Council legal responsibilities, risk management, and safety responsibilities).

Mr. Jones noted that their budget had an increase in 2015, but it was due to reallocation of
certain funds and where they were being paid from; the years after this on the chart split out this
differential to make the comparisons more visible. Due to inflation, they also do not have the
same purchasing power as nearly 30 years ago. The largest expenses were for software (software
for accessing criminal records, police databases, legal research, and case files). They also have a
line item for contracting outside professional services—occasionally they need to hire an
independent attorney to provide advice to a city board and commission due to the conflict of
interest of the City Attorney providing counsel in that capacity. The next largest cost is for
equipment, including computers, supplies, and paper/printing costs (the last of which continue to
diminish each year but are still an expense). The Legal budget is the same as last year; not
including the years they made cuts during the pandemic, this has stayed static for the last 5-6
years. Legal is asking for the same budget this year as pre-pandemic.

Mr. Jones outlined the challenges he foresees for the Legal Department, which fit into two
categories: personnel and equipment. The personnel-related challenges include hiring constraints
and heavy workloads for staff. Their workload is challenging but manageable as it is; adding
additional responsibilities would not be possible. There are also some unknown factors which
will impact their staffing, including a grant-funded criminal position for domestic violence work
(which currently diverts some of the overall caseload to this attorney and allows them to absorb a
higher workload in other areas) and the possible impacts of recent state legislation on their cases.
Mr. Jones outlined the equipment challenges and Legal’s plan for replacing outdated and out-of-
warranty equipment. Moving forward, they will fund computer replacement in portions each year
so that all computers get replaced on a rolling basis.

Councilor Rachel Whipple asked if the Justice Court’s use of video meetings had impacted their
caseloads. Mr. Jones indicated this had more of an impact on the court itself than on their office,
but it definitely created a backlog. They have been working with the Justice Court to identify
other ways to catch up on this backlog of cases. Presentation only.

Priority items in this category included a culinary water treatment facility and sidewalk
replacement. Councilor David Shipley thought a culinary water treatment plant was critical
