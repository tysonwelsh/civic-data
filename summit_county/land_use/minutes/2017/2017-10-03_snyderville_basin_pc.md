Planner Northrup-Robinson said on July 6, 1998, the Board of County Commissioners 
approved The Canyons Specially Planned Area (SPA) and adopted Ordinance 333.  This 
approval contained approximately 63,000 acres and had five different property owners.  
At the time of approval, it was anticipated there would be a major amendment in the 
near future in order to incorporate more land from additional property owners.

G
ENERAL OVERVIEW AND BUILDING HEIGHTS  
Planner Northrup-Robinson said the established areas consisted of the Resort Core, the 
Lower Village, West Willow Draw, and East Willow Draw.  The most crucial component 
of the SPA is the land use and zoning (LUZ) chart.  It allocates the density, heights, uses, 
and maximum square footage for all parcels.  Other elements in the SPA include the 
comprehensive design guidelines, transportation master plan, an employee housing 
plan, tails master plan, natural resources management plan, open space, and a view 
protection plan.  These are all key factors of the DA today.   
 
As part of the development pattern, it was determined that the majority of the density 
and the height would stay within the Resort Core and would trickle out to other areas 
such as West Willow Draw, Frostwood, and the Lower Village area.  She said Provision 
2.3.1 of the DA allows for density to be shifted on commonly owned parcels.  Prior to 
this provision, shifted densities did not require a major amendment.   
 
Planner Northrup-Robinson said the 1999 DA was approved with over 8.2 million 
square feet in density.  That included both hotel accommodations, multi-family, and 
retail commercial.  She said the proposed amendment will reduce the density by 
approximately 129,471 square feet, perhaps more.  The majority of this reduction is 
from commercial density.  There is some reduction in multi-family.  The smallest 
reduction comes from the hotel/lodging component.   
Planner Northrup-Robinson outlined the parcels where density is being removed 
completely.  These are RC-3, RC-4, RC12, and W36; however, significant density is

Administer Barnes said he is an architect by training.  When this process started a little 
over one year ago, height was one of the biggest public concerns.  He said the Planning 
Commission has discussed this in great detail.  A work session specifically about the 
height was held and a site visit was taken.  The Commission toured the area to better 
visualize what was being proposed.   
 
Administrator Barnes said a wide-spread concern has been the developer’s ability to 
manipulate the finished grade.  He said that Staff believes that currently there is no 
clear definition of a story’s height.  Many people have argued that a story’s height is 10 
feet; however, that is only implied in the DA.   
 
Administrator Barnes said the applicant is proposing to change measuring a building’s 
height from stories to an absolute height from sea level.  This would not be opened to 
interpretation.  It would be a more exact way of measuring.  It gives predictability into 
the future.  The question becomes what the allowed height should be.

A slide was shown of the difference between the permitted height under the existing 
DA, the height under the applicant’s proposal, and the difference.  Sometimes, the 
overall height has a decrease.  Sometimes it is an increase.  A map was shown that 
demonstrated these changes.   
 
Administrator Barnes said there are other things to consider when thinking about 
height.  These include height exceptions.  They have been listed in the report.  One of 
the comments received from the public is if an absolute height is established, it should 
be based on the existing number of stories.  There should be no exceptions including 
rooftop viewing decks and terraces.     
 
Administrator Barnes said in his analysis, he tried to determine if what the applicant 
was asking for was different than what has been applied in the existing development.  
He said the applicant was tasked to perform an analysis of the existing buildings.  There 
are members of the public that are concerned about the height of proposed buildings 
near their property.     
 
Administrator Barnes gave an example of problems that his analysis demonstrated.  A 
person living on the ninth level may be concerned that the potential height of a nearby 
7-story building may block their view.  This is because story heights have not been 
defined.  Because the DA refers only to the number of stories, heights can vary 
depending on a number of things such as topography, the finished grade, and the height 
of each story.  He said that variance is allowed by the Design Guidelines and is open to 
interpretation by the CVMA and County Staff. 
 
Administrator Barnes said he reviewed each and every site with an eye towards seeing 
how reasonable the developer’s application was in comparison to what had been 
planned.  He took an objective look at what was being proposed.

He said the developer figured out what the height of a building would be if they were 
building a structure with similar ground and intermediate stories heights, along with 
the roof heights that have been approved on other buildings.  They did a calculation and 
arrived at the altitude heights they are proposing.         
 
Administrator Barnes said the average industry standard of a ground story height is 
between 12’ to 16’.  The developer took the stance that a ground floor of 14’ is 
reasonable.  Administrator Barnes said he believes that is reasonable and is typical of 
what actually exists.  The applicant is proposing to cap intermediate floor levels at 11 
feet.  This also seems reasonable and typical of what has already been approved.   
 
Administrator Barnes said the roof has been a key issue.  The existing guideline 
assumes a 6/12 pitched roof.  Over the typical width of a building, that would be 16 feet 
high.  This may or may not be an occupied story.  Taking everything into consideration, 
the applicant is proposing a maximum height elevation.  In his opinion, the proposal is 
within normal industry standards.   
 
Administrator Barnes said one question he has had is if the applicant would be getting 
an advantage by turning a potentially occupied pitched roof to an occupied flat roof.  
Are they increasing the number of available stories?  He determined that the answer is- 
“perhaps.”  If it is an advantage, it is not extreme.  It would be a similar height.          
 
Administrator Barnes said a possible concern may be with allowing height exceptions.  
Would an elevated penthouse, chimneys, and normal mechanical protrusions create 
something extra?  In his opinion, it does not.  To him, the importance is the quality of 
the end project.   
Administrator Barnes said that every site will go through a design review process.  That

ARKING  
Planner Northrup-Robinson said as the Resort Core develops; the surface parking will 
be eliminated.  Some of the parking will be absorbed into the structures.  Most of the 
parking will be replaced to an area located in the Lower Village.   
 
Planner Northrup-Robinson said the developers are proposing 1,060 parking spaces in 
the Lower Village for day-skier parking.  The existing parking area will be redeveloped.  
Staff is recommending that is developed through a low-impact permit.   
 
Planner Northrup-Robinson said this parking area is a critical component.  It will be 
adjacent to it is the LV-6 parcel, which is anticipated to be employee housing.  A critical 
component is to ensure the safety and walkability to the team building, the cabriolet, 
and the mass transit areas.

5-lane road with a bike lane is being recommended on Canyons Resort Drive to the
