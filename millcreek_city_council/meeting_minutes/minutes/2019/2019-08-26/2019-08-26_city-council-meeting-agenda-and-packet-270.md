1. Overview of H.B. 411 Community Renewable Energy Act; Travis Jones, Regional
Business Manager for Rocky Mountain Power

Millcreek City Council Meeting Minutes 26 August 2019 Page 2 of 10

Kurt Hansen did an overview of the bill. He said the bill was a pathway for municipalities to
adopt a resolution to use 100% renewable energy by 2030. He said other cities had previously
adopted a similar resolution before the bill was passed; Salt Lake City, Park City, Summit
County, Moab, and Cottonwood Heights. He said renewable energy was wind, solar,
geothermal, and hydroelectric. He went through the step by step process outlined in the bill
for a City to participate in the program. The first step is to adopt a resolution by December 31,
2019. Hansen said the resolution would be for the whole community to use renewable energy,
not just city buildings. Mayor Silvestrini said it would mean anyone using metered power in
Millcreek. Hansen said the second step would be for Millcreek to enter into an agreement with
Rocky Mountain Power to pay them for third-party expertise assisting with the program study
and for notices to the customers informing them about the program. Hansen said the third step
is for the Public Service Commission (“Commission”) to adopt administrative rules, which
they have not yet done. Mayor Silvestrini asked about the Commission’s rulemaking process.
Travis Jones, Regional Business Manager for Rocky Mountain Power, said it was still in
progress. Mayor Silvestrini said Millcreek could enter into the resolution and then see what
the cost would be for the agreement with Rocky Mountain Power before moving forward.
Jones said the resolution would be to gage the community’s interest. Hansen said the fourth
step is for Millcreek to make application with Rocky Mountain Power and that application is
submitted to the Commission. Hansen said there would be a required plan for
programs/assistance for low-income residents, and that can be whatever Millcreek wants it to
be. The fifth step is for the Commission to review and approve the application. Within 90
days of the Commission’s approval of the application, the City would adopt an ordinance to
move forward with the 2030 renewable energy initiative as the sixth step. The seventh step is
for Rocky Mountain Power to provide notice to the customers. The eighth step is an
opportunity for the customer to opt out of the program. Council Member Jackson asked about
the program being tied to the address or the property owner should someone move. Jones said
that detail had not been worked out, but he assumed since the limits are geographically based
it would stay with address but there would be an opportunity for new customers to have the
chance to opt out. Council Member Marchant brought up that the bill stated that any new
customers would automatically be opted in but would be given notice that they could opt out.
The ninth step is that the customer may change their mind and leave the program. The tenth
step had requirements for Rocky Mountain Power on customer billing. The eleventh step is
that Rocky Mountain Power could make an annual rate adjustment filing with the
Commission.

Travis Jones went over the Blue Sky Program. Customers support renewable energy at a fixed
price ($1.95 per 100-kilowatt hours (kwh) block per month), 100 kilowatt-hour “blocks” of
100% western region wind (40%) and solar (60%) energy, that helps fund new, community-
based renewable energy projects in their state. Mayor Silvestrini said there are three categories
of renewable energy already and he understood that those customers with net metering from
solar panels were exempt from the program. Jones said there are two rate structures for net
metering, and he will have to see if both rate structures qualify. Council Member Marchant
said the bills says net metering clients would not be part of the program. Jones said net
metering is unique because the energy from resident’s solar panels is metered and goes back
into Rocky Mountain Power. He said subscriber solar is buying blocks of energy offset by a
renewable resource. The Blue Sky Program is a renewable energy credit. Jones said there is a
waiting list for subscriber solar, but the turnaround time is 3 months. Mayor Silvestrini said
the Blue Sky Program is an exchange credit for energy derived elsewhere. Jones said the

renewable energy. Mayor Silvestrini said he met with Bluffdale and they have solar panels on
their city buildings. Jones said there is a grant program through Blue Sky for adding solar
panels on community buildings. He said renewables are a virtual exercise. Council Member
Marchant said Rocky Mountain Power’s website did not describe the grant program and its

Mayor Silvestrini asked if more renewable energy facilities would be built in Utah. Council

capacity out of state. Jones said Rocky Mountain Power tries to keep costs for customers low

renewable energy is diverse. Council Member Marchant said Millcreek would be relying on
the Public Service Commission to see that the study conducted achieves Millcreek’s goal by
2030. Jones said the community needs to decide if there is a benefit. Council Member
Marchant said Millcreek values renewable energy, the question is the accountability behind it

unknowns and the resolution would not be binding.

Council Member Jackson asked if Rocky Mountain Power has been replacing assets with
renewables and asked about their process for attrition. Jones said they are phasing out
traditional ways of creating energy, but they have a diverse portfolio of reliable sources to
meet customer needs. He said in 2018, about 40% of their energy came from coal, 30%
natural gas, 8% hydroelectric, and 17% of the energy was renewable. Council Member Catten
asked if the developed program would affect Rocky Mountain Power’s decision to participate.
Jones said Rocky Mountain Power would purchase whatever is developed with the study.
Council Member Jackson asked if renewable energy was reliable. Jones said power would still
be delivered the same way and having multiple sources makes it more reliable. He said
nothing currently being used for energy would be decommissioned but they want to keep up
with modern technology. Council Member Marchant said Rocky Mountain Power was
transitioning to renewable energy and asked if they had a plan. Jones said there was not a
timeframe for that, but Rocky Mountain Power was committed to accomplishing the goal.
Council Member Jackson asked about other cities having made similar commitments with
renewable energy. Jones said he did not know about cities outside of the state doing
something similar to this program, but Utah cities had not opted into this one yet. Mayor
Silvestrini said the total community usage would be based on Millcreek, Millcreek Canyon,
unincorporated areas. Jones said 3,396 users in those areas were using renewable sources with
19,114,122 kilowatt hours of total usage. He said in 2018, there were 678 solar panel
installations which equated to 9,612,566 kwh. Jones said the percentages were relative to
surrounding cities usage.

Mayor Silvestrini introduced Tyler Poulson as the Sustainability Program Manager for Salt
Lake City and said he assisted in the drafting of H.B. 411. Poulson said for Salt Lake City, the
story of the bill started in 2016 in signing a joint resolution committing to net 100%

renewable electricity by 2032. They realized in order to accomplish this goal, they would need
State level legislative changes and to work through the regulatory process in Utah. In 2016,
the Council also signed a public facing cooperation statement to work together with Rocky
Mountain Power on the legislative and regulatory steps to authorize the transition. He said the
net 100% renewable goal, what the bill authorizes, is to catalyze new renewable projects so
the net annual output of those projects equals the total annual consumption; it does not mean
that 24/7 - 365 power would be renewable energy. They are looking to integrate renewables to
meet the net annual target. He said this is the first time an investor on utility, Rocky Mountain
Power, has done a program like this. Other cities (Burlington, VT, Aspen, CO, Georgetown,
TX) have announced that they have achieved net renewable energy, but he did not know how
it was achieved. The driver in 2016 was to reduce Salt Lake City’s carbon footprint, not the
biggest factor of area air quality. H.B. 411 granted legal authority in Utah to create tailored
renewable portfolios for communities. He said the bill focuses on three key principles; net
100% renewability, not shifting costs to other entities in Utah, and customer opt-out. He said
individual customers have the legal right to opt out and maintain regular service. There are
three key stakeholders; communities, Rocky Mountain Power, and Utah regulatory staff and
Public Service Commission. He said the six key steps of the bill are: the City adopts a
resolution, the City signs an agreement with Rocky Mountain Power, the City pays for the
first round of customer noticing and Rocky Mountain Power pays for the noticing of new
customers, there is regulatory filing, the Public Service Commission issues orders for
projected rates for the program, and the City considers a local ordinance. Communities who
move forward will receive two notices through three billing cycles to opt out and termination
charges will be assessed after that. He said the Public Service Commission’s draft rules will
be filed in early November. Council Member Uipi asked if the rule making process would be
open to the public. Poulson said there would be engagement and Salt Lake City has a FAQ’s
page posted on their website.

Mayor Silvestrini asked Poulson about other interested cities in Utah. Poulson said there are
more than ten communities who have expressed interest. Council Member Marchant said
some cities produce their own power and asked if they would be involved. Poulson said those
cities would not be part of this process. Council Member Marchant brought up nuclear power
sources. Poulson said the community resolutions previously adopted influenced the bill.
Council Member Marchant asked about coal-power electricity usage after 2030. Poulson said
the legislation is not specific to sources being developed in Utah and reliability would not be
impacted. Mayor Silvestrini asked about the differences between this program and Rocky
Mountain Power’s Blue Sky Program. Poulson said the current programs offer the opportunity
for any customer to participate with renewable energy sources and credits and this program
would make using renewable energy the operating standard.

2. Transit Service Discussion; Carlton Christensen, UTA Board Chair
Carlton Christensen, Utah Transit Authority (UTA) Board Chair, provided the Council with a
list of transit lines that run through Millcreek and the average ridership. He said UTA tracks
boardings and departures on every transit option. UTA’s safety message is to avoid
distraction, many fatalities incurred on the system are due to people not paying attention. He
said the Legislature created a modification to the governance of UTA two sessions ago from
sixteen part-time board members to three full-time members known as the Board of Trustees.
He said there is a UTA Local Advisory Council made up of municipal mayors and council
members. That council has input on finances, capital planning, service delivery, transit-

oriented development, and long-range planning. Christensen went over the organizational
structure which has an Executive Director, Chief Officers, and 2,500 employees. The Board of
Trustees tasked the Executive Director to focus on the internal operations of the organization.
He said UTA’s portion of the Salt Lake County 4" Quarter transportation tax would be used
on bus service which will have a phased approach to service implementation. Phase 1 will be
on mobilization from fall of 2019 to August of 2021. The second phase is the implementation
of the new bus services beginning in August 2021. UTA is constructing a new bus facility in
downtown Salt Lake City to replace an old one. UTA has added three electric buses to the
fleet and added more services in Salt Lake City. He said it takes a while to hire and train bus
drivers. This year is the 20" anniversary of the TRAX line in downtown Salt Lake City. He
said the cars, which have a forty-year life, are tired but UTA is doing work on them. UTA is
launching a pilot program to look at micro transit to serve the newer suburban neighborhoods.

Christensen invited the Council to communicate with him any time. Mayor Silvestrini said
staff would work with UTA on capacity issues with new projects. Council Member Marchant
thanked Christensen for taking him on a transit tour in Utah County. He brought up bus rapid
transit (brt) and wondered if it could be a possibility in Millcreek. Christensen said there was
(brt) in Ogden and a mid-valley connection in Murray going to West Valley. He said it has
separated roadway areas, central loading areas, and comes by every six minutes. He said when
school is in session, about 10,000 students ride it a day.

2. Planning Matters
2.1 Discussion and Consideration of Ordinance 19-39, Amending Title 19 of
Millcreek Code with Respect to Noticing Hearing Requirements for Land Use
Hearings
Mayor Silvestrini said the Council does a first reading on planning matters, so the
Council had already heard the matter. Francis Lilly said the changes to the ordinance are
that it gives the Land Use Hearing Officer more structure to evaluate a request on
nonconforming structures and protections for affected neighbors, a reasonable
determination must be made that the property change conforms to the neighborhood and
the Residential Compatibility Overlay Zone, and gave the Hearing Officer power in
imposing limitations on a permit. The other changes are adding hearing and due process
requirements that would affect anything going to the Hearing Officer. Lilly said notices
would be given 14 days prior to the hearing to property owners within 300 feet of the
subject property, notice would be given to the community council chair, and there would
be guidelines for individuals on how to participate in the hearing. He said the hearing
differs from a public hearing such as those held before the public body. The community
councils and Planning Commission supported the amendments.

Mayor Silvestrini asked if there was public comment on the matter. There was none.
Mayor Silvestrini said the ordinance was an elegant solution to a problem the City has
faced.

Council Member Uipi moved to pass item 2.1, Ordinance 19-39 land use hearings. Council
Member Catten seconded. The City Recorder called for the vote. The motion passed
unanimously.
