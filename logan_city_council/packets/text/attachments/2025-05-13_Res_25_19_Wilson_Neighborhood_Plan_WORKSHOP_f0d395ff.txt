WHEREAS, the Municipal Council finds that the physical condition of the Wilson
Neighborhood is of significant importance to the vitality of the City of Logan and Cache Valley;

[Wilson Neighborhood Plan logo]
Executive Summary

As per Logan City's General Plan, the Community
Development Department is creating six Neighborhood
Specific Plans. The neighborhoods were based on the six
elementary school boundaries. The Neighborhood
Specific plans examine, at a grass-roots level, the
challenges, issues, and wishes of each area. The plans
include existing conditions, inventories, demographics,
feedback, and recommendations. The recommendations
should be referenced when adopting future codes and
zoning that shape growth, neighborhood character, open
space, transportation, and overall quality of life.

The Wilson Neighborhood is one of Logan City's oldest,
and most diverse. Originally called "The Island" and
shaped by natural river drainages departing Logan
Canyon, agrarian patterns emerged here in the late
1800's more organically as compared to the rigid square-
block patterns found in other core neighborhoods.
Wilson is rich in culture and uniqueness, and heavily
influenced by early Scandinavian immigrants. As the
city continued to grow in population, Wilson
experienced challenges similar to other core
neighborhoods. Rental housing pressures associated
with a growing Utah State University, along with post
World War II migration trends to suburban areas,
impacted neighborhood character. The departure to the
suburbs meant longer travel distances, ushering in a new
car-oriented development pattern resulting in wider
faster streets designed to move cars quickly. The
contrasting home designs and block patterns of Cliffside
compared to the Island is a visual reflection of this
change in lifestyle.

[map/plat page 6 — "Logan Island Survey Plat circa 1900"; legible title-block text: "MAP OF SURVEYS ON LOGAN ISLAND INCLUDING PLATS B.C. FARMLAND & PLAT D. LOGAN CITY."; map labels: LOGAN ISLAND PLAT B]

Steep topography, numerous waterway conveyance, and beautiful natural open spaces contrast with
diverse buildings creating the uniqueness and wonder of the Wilson Neighborhood. Containing
Logan's oldest park and some of the best outdoor recreation opportunities in the city, careful and
strategic growth management will be one of the biggest challenges moving forward. Innovation,
collaboration, and proficiency will be needed to provide future generations with affordable housing
at all of life's different stages. Effective and equitable transportation infrastructure, reliable city
services, neighborhood safety, and economic growth in commercial areas are clear priorities from
the group. Preservation of cherished open space and recreation access are important assets, making
Wilson attractive to current and future residents. Better branding of the "unique character" of
Wilson will help to attract new generations and new investment. Part of this branding effort will
include the resident-preferred, sub-neighborhood boundaries of Cliffside, Island, Sumac, and
Canyon Road. Pride of property ownership will increase with these branding efforts. Balancing
rental housing units with new owner-occupied units will be important for stability and overall
neighborhood aesthetics. Core area residents have indicated a desire for more alternative
transportation improvements and street safety renovations to help minimize street dangers and
improve their physical exercise and well-being. Recreation opportunities will need to expand to
better accommodate population growth. With skilled comprehensive leadership, these issues can
be resolved, and the future will remain bright for the neighborhood.

History

Native American tribes, including Shoshoni and
Blackfoot, lived in Cache Valley for generations prior to
the arrival of European settlers in 1855. Called different
names over the years, the "Island" was originally
developed as small farms, predominately by
Scandinavian immigrants attracted to this area because
of its proximity to water and fertile soils. The Logan
River branched downstream from the mouth of the
canyon forming an island between the main and north
branch, also known as the "The Little Logan River".  The
history of the Island, and often referred to as the
"Seventh Ward" by the settlers, is as rich as the fertile
soils, natural riparian habitat, and wild cutthroat trout.
Being the more naturalistic area of Logan City and
having a history of farming, the Island doesn't strictly
follow the traditional 10-acre square block Plat of Zion
pattern like other core neighborhoods do. Over the years,
and as times continue to change, small family farms have
been replaced with various housing infill projects and
small business ventures.   Numerous duplexes and
triplexes are randomly scattered throughout due to R1,
R2, and R3 zoning district allowances of the 1950's-
1970's. And later, the neighborhood boundaries
expanded into the Sumac and Cliffside areas as the city
and valley expanded outwardly. Aside from the small
stretch of southeast Main Street, the Wilson
Neighborhood has always been predominately
residential in nature.

Demographics & Data

The demographic data from Social Explorer – American
Community Survey (ACS) 2021 shows the Wilson
Neighborhood population at approximately 7,621.
Wilson makes up approximately 15% of the City's total
population of 52,420.  The age-group breakdown in the
Wilson Neighborhood is similar to the overall city with
18 to 24-year-olds being in the majority. Wilson's
median age of 28 years old is slightly higher than the
citywide median of 24. Race breakdowns in Wilson also
closely match citywide breakdowns, with approximately
90% of the neighborhood being "white alone"
(85% citywide).

With 2,627 total households, the Wilson Neighborhood
makes up 15% of citywide households (17,152). Married
households are the most common at nearly 62% and
household size at 2.9 people per dwelling unit, aligns
with the rest of the city.

Education levels in Wilson are very similar to citywide
figures. Completed "some college" is the largest group
in education level breakdowns. Bachelor's degree
holders and high school graduates are second and third
respectively, with Master's degrees following.
Household median income is higher in Wilson at
$66,046, compared to the citywide median of $46,761.
Utah State University full-time students typically skew
income data citywide.

Existing Conditions

The Wilson Neighborhood is a mix of natural areas,
multi-family and single-family homes of all ages, small
businesses, public spaces, and a stretch of south Main
Street. Never fully achieving the square block patterns
seen in other core neighborhoods, Wilson has a wide
range of block sizes, shapes, and levels of connectivity.
Along the western edge and near downtown, a diverse
mix of land use and structure types are found.
Operational canals, rivers, and streams form a network
of water ways crossing and serving all parts of Cache
Valley and eventually reaching the Great Salt Lake.

The Wilson Neighborhood has 1,568 total acres of land
within its boundary. A total of 2,586 residential
structures, 42 commercial structures, 2 public schools,
and 13 public facilities are all located inside the
neighborhood. There are 14 public parks, 2 open space
preserves, over 6 miles of trails, 4 canals, and the Logan
and Little Logan River. There are 6 religious structures
in Wilson.

Wilson has approximately 31 miles of streets in different
shapes and sizes. Considering both sides of the street,
there are approximately 19 miles of missing sidewalk
sections forming 126 gaps in the pedestrian network.
There are 11 bridges spanning the Logan River (2
pedestrian only), 7 traffic signals, two pedestrian tunnels
and three public art installations in Merlin Olsen Central
Park. Wilson's eastern edge is the gateway to Logan
Canyon and points beyond.

Zoning & Land Use

The Wilson Neighborhood currently has 10 zoning
districts. NR-6 and NR-4 zones make up the largest land
area at 930 acres. 83% of the properties in Wilson
currently conform to zoning land use and structure type.
For example, a triplex located in a single-family zone is
considered a nonconforming density and structure type.
Commercial zoned properties consist of about 3% of the
overall neighborhood area, with the remaining smaller
percentage of zones being Mixed Use, Town Center,
Neighborhood Center, and Mixed Residential. Wilson
has large portions of REC (green) and PUB (blue) zones,
especially along the eastern mountain interface.

The Future Land Use Plan (FLUP) designations are
currently 97% accurate with current zoning designations.
A few properties, like Herm's Inn, are not accurate with
FLUP designations. Approximately 24-acres on the
FLUP, located primarily along the 100 East corridor, are
prescribed to change from NR-6 to COM/TC sometime
in the future. Future zoning or FLUP amendments should
consider conformity to existing structures, densities and
land uses to achieve better overall conformance. Infill
and redevelopment opportunities are mostly located
along Main Street, 100 East, and vacant middle-of-the-
block areas. PUB and REC zoned areas need to be
adjusted to better reflect the FLUP and current land uses.
In general, higher-density redevelopments  near
downtown puts less stress on infrastructure and reduces
suburban sprawl. Downtown housing will likely be
smaller unit sizes, but the trade-offs can be high-quality
design, more walkability and social opportunities.

Housing

The Wilson Neighborhood is an eclectic mix of housing
types and sizes, built in different eras throughout the last
125 years. With 2,586 total residential structures and a
population of approximately 7,621, the average
household size sits just under three people per dwelling
unit. With 930 acres currently zoned for residential, that
is an overall housing density of 2.78 units per acre.

[chart: "How can housing improve in the neighborhood?" CHOOSE ALL THAT APPLY — 213 RESPONSES; bars: 94, 30, 57, 25, 100, 28; legend: More affordable for first-time buyers; No enough housing types/options; High quality materials and better designs; More rental options; More home ownership opportunities; Other]

Currently, the biggest challenge with housing is
affordability and obtainability for potential home
buyers. A significant spike in home prices over the
past several years has worsened this situation. With
population growth and the increased demand for
housing, current supply inventories fall short.
Furthering the problem, employee wage increases
are not keeping pace with home price increases.

Another issue for homebuyers is that older generation homeowners are hesitant to sell their home
and downsize because of the financial impacts in this market. Material supply chain issues, national
buying competition, a lack of rental tenant and buyer's advocacy organizations gives the upper
hand to sellers. There are also market pressures to convert owner occupied housing into short-term
rentals, taking housing supply off the market. Younger generations, typically first-time
homebuyers, are currently struggling to afford housing. Rents have also skyrocketed with this
historic spike, adding to the affordability problem. Possible mitigation could include increased
occupancy in existing legal units, allowing for more accessory dwelling units, upzoning, and
incentivizing builders to build larger quantities of homes. Better organizing and harnessing buying
power for prospective home buyers and renters could be explored. Continuing to innovative and
effectively integrate new technologies into home-building will help the situation. The
neighborhood survey results from 2023 revealed there is some willingness to explore higher-
density redevelopment, especially in downtown areas. This complex housing affordability issue
will require numerous efforts and solutions from all sectors to improve.

Transportation

Transportation is important for commerce and
residential mobility. Presently, most people choose the
automobile as their primary mode of transportation.
Infrastructure and culture have revolved around the
automobile for decades. The recently adopted citywide
Transportation Master Plan (TMP) identifies corridors
and strategies to better facilitate automobile traffic and
its anticipated growth. The automobile is the most
popular but also the most expensive for both residents
and government building and maintaining
infrastructure. Automobiles are the most dangerous
mode with crashes killing hundreds of Utahns every
year. They're environmentally unfriendly and directly
linked to poor public health but they're very
convenient and have become second nature for most
residents. At current levels of automobile dependence,
major transportation modal changes will be slow to
realize. Except for the steep areas of Cliffside, most of
Wilson is well-suited for less impactful alternative
modes of transportation. Short travel lengths and trail
networks make alternatives like biking, walking, and
transit more viable. Logan City adopted a Bicycle and
Pedestrian Masterplan and if fully implemented,
alternative transportation will become an easier choice
for residents. Automobile and alternative modes
compete for space within the street right-of-way,
meaning the more car-friendly the street is, the less
pedestrian-friendly it becomes and visa-versa.

[map/plat page 12 — "Master Transportation Plan Future Street Classifications"; labels: E 700, N 300 E, N 400 E, N 500 E, E 100 N, Logan, E 300 N, Canyon Rd, Sumac Dr, Bay Hurdle Park, E 100 N, E 100 S, E 200 S, River Heights Blvd, 1000 E]

When streets are too car-centric they better enable suburban sprawl and discourage livability in
core neighborhoods. In other words, wide, fast streets through core neighborhoods only benefit
suburban sprawl and their mobility. The Wilson Neighborhood survey results show that
neighborhood residents do not want wider, faster streets near their homes and schools. They desire
more pedestrian friendly street designs. Perspectives on this are typically selfish, and the main
reason automobiles are so impactful to our cities, with most people wishing to drive their own car
quickly across the city and through other neighborhoods, yet they don't want others driving fast
through their neighborhood because it's loud, unsafe, and generally disruptive.

The TMP plan factors trip generation data and travel demand modeling to recommend future street
widening and classification changes for acceptable automobile levels of service. Recommendations
are based on population growth and identify streets in Wilson for widened and higher classification.
East Center Street, Canyon Road, and 400 East increase from Neighborhood to Collector Street
classification. Currently at approximately 60 feet wide, Collector Streets are prescribed at 80 feet
wide in the TMP. Street widening may require home demolition and neighborhood change. Moving
forward, a better balance of essential commerce and reasonable residential mobility with core
neighborhood respect and safety will need to be struck. If not, core neighborhoods will become less
desirable and continue to experience a disproportionate amount of negative transportation impacts.

Parks & Recreation

From shady picnic areas to the exciting terrain of bike
trails, the Logan Parks and Recreation Department has
something for everyone. The purpose of the Parks and
Recreation Department is to serve the leisure and
recreational needs of the neighborhood.

Wilson has 14 parks and dedicated open spaces inside
the overall boundary. Five of the fourteen have
bathroom facilities, and four allow for dogs. Seven
parks have associated paved parking areas.  Hyrum
Gibbons Mount Logan, and Denzil Stewart Nature
Parks both have interior pathways for exercise and
leisure. Six of the formal parks have picnic pavilions,
playgrounds, and sports courts. All parks have
informal grass areas suitable for informal field sports
and gatherings.

The Canyon Road Trail connects the neighborhood to
the mouth of Logan Canyon and Cache National
Forest. Merlin Olsen Central Park is considered the
heart of the neighborhood and trail connection to
Downtown. All of Logan City Parks and Trails close
from 10:00 PM to 5:00 AM. Slacklines and hammocks
are not permitted in City Parks and dog owners must
clean up after their dogs.

Public Facilities

Wilson contains numerous public facilities and public
infrastructure. Dry Canyon is a large and potentially
damaging watershed. At the mouth of Dry Canyon a
flood mitigation reservoir is located with overflow
piping to the Logan River. The Mount Logan Park
flood mitigation detention pond is also used as soccer
fields. The Crockett Well House along Canyon Road
was recently completed and brought into compliance
with safety standards and specifications.

Over the next several years, the Public Works
Department is planning several new public facility and
infrastructure projects in Wilson. A large new water
storage tank is planned near the USU Ropes Course
requiring new water lines under Canyon Road and up
the Dugway. The two bridges spanning Logan River
at Center Street and 100 North are planned to be
replaced in 2027-2029. A trail underpass is planned
along the north side of Logan River at 600 South Main
Street. Various sidewalk projects are planned, mainly
in the Island area, helping to fill missing gaps along
older streets.

Public art installations and mural paintings will
continue as per Logan City's Public Art Program with
a second mural recently completed in the west tunnel
at Merlin Olsen Park.

Neighborhood Survey

In the spring of 2023, the residents of Wilson were given
the opportunity to take a 32-question neighborhood
survey. The survey, offered in paper and digital format,
focused on important neighborhood topics. 229 residents
completed the survey resulting in a better understanding
of neighborhood concerns.  229 responses out of nearly
7,000 neighborhood residents, is a small sample size, but
gives City Staff and Elected Officials a glimpse
neighborhood desire. In general, the results show a
preference to associate with their sub-neighborhood
names, like Island and Cliffside, rather than the overall
Wilson Neighborhood name. People are generally
satisfied with city services and utilities. The two most
important issues are housing and transportation. They
want affordable housing and efficient transportation but
not at the cost of compromising neighborhood character
and safety. Summarized survey results are listed below
and in the appendix.

Recommendations

    1. Future Logan City documents and planning efforts should recognize sub-neighborhood names Island, Cliffside, Canyon Road, & Sumac.
    2. Zoning Map amendments should consider changes inside and near downtown to better facilitate new affordable and obtainable infill housing.
    3. When developed, the large vacant areas south of Cliffside should include multiple street connections to for better traffic distribution and safety response.
    4. Future zoning along the edge of downtown should encourage higher density development, as that is the preferred location indicated by residents.
    5. Zoning Map amendments should change Public Zoning designations to Recreation for the large eastern areas of natural open space.
    6. Local programs should be established to help incentivize and retain more owner-occupied homes in the Island area.
    7. Local housing programs should be established to better inform and collectively harness buying power for potential home buyers.
    8. More efficient methods of construction and alternative building materials should be applied in appropriate locations for more affordable housing units.
    9. Standard home occupancy limits should increase and be based on structure and property capacities to open more housing opportunities and supply.
    10. New accessory dwelling units in owner-occupied homes should be further encouraged and implemented to open more housing supply.
    11. Larger vacant and infill parcels should utilize the PD-Overlay zone to develop affordable single-family homes in innovative ways.
    12. Neighborhood street design should prioritize local safety and neighborhood character over suburban commuter vehicle speed and volume.
    13. 200 East should bridge the Logan River and connect south to 100 East and future street widening should only be done on critical transportation corridors.
    14. Transit service should increase frequency and hours of operation to attract new and more ridership.
    15. Pedestrian and bicycle infrastructure should be expanded because it's a healthier and less impactful mode of transportation to the neighborhood.
    16. New development should connect to existing street grid patterns to minimize dead-ends and ensure equitable traffic distribution.
    17. City emergency services and utilities should continue to provide essential service while planning for future growth areas in the neighborhood.
    18. Canyon Road Trail and the Boulevard Trail should officially connect to each other and narrow sections should be widen to 8-foot multi-use standards.
    19. The Bonneville Shoreline Trail should naturally follow hillside contours versus the straight deer fence and ultimately connect Dry and Logan Canyon.
    20. A better trail connection between Denzel Stewart and Canyon Road Memorial Park will help the overall trial network and link two popular areas.
    21. Trail fundraisers and adopt-a-trail efforts could create new revenue for improvements and new trail naming of generic names, like Canyon Road Trail.
    22. Neighborhood street intersections should continue to be enhanced with clear markings, lighting, and signage for safety and clarity.
    23. Multi-use trails inside Merlin Olsen Park and Pioneer Park should be widened to meet standard 8-foot widths for two-way traffic.
    24. New concrete trail surfaces should saw-cut joints and avoid rounded control joints for smoother and more enjoyable use by wheeled users.
    25. Streets with no sidewalk on either side or near school zones should be prioritized for future sidewalk installation projects.
    26. Natural rivers and canals should be highly valued by residents and local leaders for their beauty, neighborhood uniqueness, and wildlife habitat.
    27. Large public infrastructure projects should involve neighborhood residents for better collaboration, analyzation and alternatives when necessary.
    28. The FLUP should be updated to accurately account for Herm's Inn, Rudy's Greenhouse and other similar stable commercial uses.
