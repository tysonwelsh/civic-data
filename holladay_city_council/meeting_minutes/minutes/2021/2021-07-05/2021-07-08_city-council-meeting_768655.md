IV.     Public Hearing on Proposed Amendments to the City’s Fee Schedule.
V.      Public Hearing on Proposed Amendments to Noticing Requirements.
The above two agenda items were discussed together. Planning Manager, Jonathan Teerlink, reported
that the intent is to become more compliant with the State statute with regard to newspaper circulation.
For legislative items, notice is required to be placed in daily circulation for newspapers. The
requirement is that notice be posted in three public noticing locations including the City’s website
and the State’s noticing website. The City also took the opportunity to clarify and simplify the
noticing sections of the ordinance. Various noticing requirements are spread out in terms of types of
applications, subdivisions, site plans, etc. Those references have been placed in a matrix that will be
placed at the end of the application. It will allow applicants, developers, and citizens to review the
chart and see specifically what the noticing requirements are rather than having to sift through the
ordinance.

The Fee Schedule is an effort on behalf of the City administration to create a Consolidated Fee
Schedule that is not codified by ordinance but rather by resolution so that the fees can be changed as
needed. It is necessary to remove all references to fees and fines in all portions of the ordinance and
place them in a Consolidated Fee Schedule that can be more easily referenced.

City Attorney, Todd Godfrey, explained that fees are set for numerous things and many involve
making an educated estimate with respect to the internal cost to the City to take a particular action.
There are times when that fee may not perfectly match a specific circumstance but when set by
ordinance, City administration cannot adjust it. If set by resolution, the administration has more

       a.       Water Company Presentations.

Mayor Dahle reported that representatives were present from all three water agencies. He commented
on a recent article that appeared in The Journal about water and how it is delivered to citizens. He
was pleased to have all three agencies present. Jeff Snelling, Laura Briefer, and Stephanie Duer were
present from Salt Lake City Public Utilities, Alan Packard and Shane Swensen were present from
Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District (“JVWCD”), and Darren Shepherd was present from
Holiday Water Company.

Mr. Swensen introduced himself as the Engineering Department Manager for JVWCD. Alan Packard
serves as the Assistant General Manager and Chief Engineer. Mr. Swensen reported that JVWCD
provides water to most municipalities in the Salt Lake Valley outside of Salt Lake City and Sandy.
They also have a few retail areas including one in the City of Holladay. A map was displayed
identifying the area they provide water to. Their primary purpose is to deliver water to residents
primarily as a wholesale provider to municipalities or water conservancy districts. There are also
small areas within Salt Lake County that were not being served by a city that they have provided
water since the 1950s. They try to manage demand so that they have enough water for future
demands. That is accomplished by promoting the use of water more efficiently or through
conservation.

Mr. Swensen reported that even with the conservation that is being promoted, their current supplies
will not meet the buildout demand. As a result, they are looking to develop future supplies. They are
currently experiencing extreme drought conditions and JVWCD develops resources to help them get
through short-term droughts. They are comfortable that this year there is enough water supply to get
through the year. They, however, fully support the governor’s executive order urging residents to
reduce their water usage, primarily with respect to irrigation. Residents were also asked to consider
conditions when they water, water at night if possible, and not water if it is rainy or windy since
irrigation is not effective during those times. Homeowners should also repair leaks or indoor issues
with the water system and ensure that fixtures are efficient. Mr. Swensen reported that JVWCD is
also asking that residents delay the installation of new landscaping, which requires significantly more
water.

Mr. Packard reported that in addition to the response actions for this year, they are promoting long-
term changes in how water is used. JVWCD uses a long-term planning horizon that is 50 years into
the future and project population growth. They concluded that past landscaping practices are
unsustainable going forward. They are also striving to have diverse water supplies. They are looking
to further diversify to ensure that a single drought event does not cripple the water supply. They are
actively putting measures in place to mitigate the current situation, prepare for future droughts, and
become more drought resilient.

JVWCD is seeking to make conservation a priority and create a durable reduction in water use. They
are in the process of preparing a Drought Contingency Plan, which they received grant funding for
from the United States Bureau of Reclamation in early 2020. To qualify for the grant, the Bureau of
Reclamation wants it to be a very public process. The elements of the plan include (1) identifying
mitigation measures, (2) being better prepared for the future, (3) establishing formal procedures to
monitor conditions, (4) establishing different target reduction levels depending on severity, and (4)
identifying specific response actions based on the level of drought.

Mr. Packard displayed data on temperature trends from the 1940s to today at the Salt Lake City
Airport. Average temperatures in June, July, and August showed an increase in temperatures, which
makes it more difficult to preserve water. In JVWCD’s service areas, approximately 60% of water is
used outdoors. They are seeking to achieve conservation through education campaigns, incentives,
and structural regulations. Examples were given of incentives programs that are currently in place.
They are available to everyone within the JVWCD service area including retail areas of the City.

It was reported that JVWCD is a wholesaler so they have resources that municipalities do not.
Generally, however, the Central Utah Water Conservancy District works with municipalities outside
of the Jordan Valley District to implement programs within their service areas. Conservation efforts
were described.

Mr. Packard next addressed regulatory/structural changes that have been made. In late 2019, JVWCD
adopted water efficiency standards/limitations on the amount of turf that can be installed with new
construction. The standards originally included a requirement that new construction install certified
indoor fixtures. It was noted that the State of Utah has adopted the International Plumbing Code
statewide. State law prohibits a municipality from mandating anything that is more stringent than the
International Plumbing Code. The language in their efficiency standards was changed accordingly.

Mr. Packard explained that their standards are applicable only to new construction. They provided
incentives for their member cities to adopt and include them in their ordinances. For residential
development, the water efficiency standards specify no grass in park strips. In front and side yards
of new construction, no more than 35% turf is allowed. Other water-wise landscaping must be used.
For new commercial properties, 20% turf is the maximum unless it is a school. Turf is no longer
allowed in park strips although there are opportunities for exemptions. Mr. Packard reported that
three of their member cities have adopted the water efficiency standards and they have implemented
them for their own retail system.

Laura Briefer from Salt Lake City Public Utilities addressed the status of their supply this year and
the short and long-term planning taking place. She reported that Salt Lake City Public Utilities is a
municipal department of Salt Lake City. They provide water to all of Salt Lake City and a portion of
the City of Holladay, Millcreek, Cottonwood Heights, South Salt Lake, Murray, and Midvale. Years
ago, Salt Lake City was asked by Salt Lake County to extend service to promote economic
development in the valley. Over time, various annexations and incorporations took place on top of
the existing water system. They continued to consider the entire system as part of the Salt Lake City
system, which is very interconnected. Their sources of water come from Deer Creek Reservoir in
terms of storage with direct rights in Big and Little Cottonwood Canyons, Millcreek, Parley’s Creek,
and City Creek. They also operate several water treatment plants and a vast distribution system that
covers about 140 square miles. They are one of the oldest retail water suppliers west of the

Mississippi and at one time had water pipes dating back to the last 1800s. They have a very strong
capital improvement program to assess, replace, and rehabilitate aging infrastructure.

Ms. Briefer reported on the status of water supplies and stated that the surface water sources that they
rely upon are very low. They range from the low 30s to 50 percent of average yield. They have
monitored those conditions since February 2021. In March they recognized that they were going to
have a very low yield year on their surface water and activated Stage 1 of their Water Storage
Contingency Plan. Stage 1 puts the public on alert that some of their supplies will be well below
average and they requested voluntary conservation action. As they continued to observe stream
systems getting lower, they implemented Stage 2 of the Water Storage Contingency Plan, which
includes voluntary participation from residents and businesses and mandates certain restrictions.
Ms. Briefer was pleased to report that actions taken by their government facilities and voluntary
actions have made a significant impact. She thanked the City of Holladay for its efforts.

Ms. Briefer commented that one of the positive things about Salt Lake City’s water system is that
they have a diversity of resources to rely on. That is not an accident. She explained that in the 1930s
when the region experienced an intense drought, a lot of public investment was made into water
storage to satisfy demand. They participate in the Deer Creek Reservoir/Provo River system through
their membership in the Metropolitan Water District of Salt Lake and Sandy, which was created to
help Salt Lake City diversify its system as the community grew. Ms. Briefer reported that a portion
of their water system also relies on groundwater supplies. They are careful with how they use the
stored and groundwater and they optimize their surface water sources to the extent possible and draw
on storage sources during drought years. She saw no issues with meeting demand but stated that
Stage 2 is justified to ensure the reliability of all water sources.

Ms. Briefer commented on the short-term drought response, conservation, and long-term planning
taking place. She explained that their Water Shortage Contingency Plan involves short-term
planning. They have also updated their 40-Year Water Supply and Demand Plan to take into
consideration issues such as changing land use, demographics, population growth, and supply. They
also factor in climate change. The plan was updated in 2019 and shortly thereafter they developed a
2020 Water Conservation Plan that takes a long-term look at how much conservation is needed to
ensure that supply and demand by 2060 match. There are additional strategies in the current 2020
Conservation Plan as well as incentive plans, education, and policy work.

Salt Lake City Public Utilities Water Conservation Manager, Stephanie Duer, shared short and long-
term conservation strategies. She reported that they have spent a significant amount of time working
with their partner cities, the County, and State facilities to strategize approaches to reducing water
use in the short and long term. They have devised strategies and her office is helping to provide
analytical data and other planning processes. Their messaging was described and is aimed at their
institutional and residential customers. They also have a program for commercial customers.
Ms. Duer reported that their strategies and partnerships are working well. After a brief uptick in water
use earlier in the season, increased messaging from JVWCD, the State, and other communities, they
are now using water below their last three-year average. Later this year, Salt Lake City will be
offering rebates for high-efficiency spray nozzles for rain-free sensor shutoffs. It is the only model
on the market with a three-day shutoff. Turf alternatives were also considered.

Mr. Duer stated that in their service area, outdoor water use is close to 30%. They have 90,000
connections of which 12 to 14 percent are commercial customers who use about 50% of their water.
For that reason, it is important for any program implemented to include them. They are implementing
the American Waterboard Association (“AWA”) and an audit of their internal infrastructure system.

Darren Shepherd from Holiday Water Company reported that they are very small and would love to
have a team to help with conservation. He identified Holiday Water Company’s service area on a
map displayed. Their borders follow Upper Canal and move along the east side of the border. He
explained that around 1920, Holiday Water made an arrangement with Salt Lake City allowing them
to take many of the water rights in exchange for 1.2 million gallons per day and maintenance of the
main lines that come through the City of Holladay. In exchange, they were able to use the excess
water. Spring Creek is another water source used in the City of Holladay. Mr. Shepherd stated that
during the winter months all of their water supply comes from Spring and Cottonwood Creeks. In
the summer months that is not enough to serve the residents in their system so they utilize four wells
that meet the remaining need water and pull from the aquifer.

Mr. Shepherd reported that during the summer, the largest users are the church, the school districts,
and the City. The rest involves primarily residential consumers. They do not have any incentives for
conservation but they work hard to inform their customers that it is a precious resource. They also
refer to the Governor’s directive to conserve. Mr. Shepherd pointed out that Holiday Water Company
has access to an abundant amount of water and they have seen no indication of water problems. They
have had no growth. If someone sells a one or two-acre parcel, the same amount of water that was
used to keep the property green equates to what a resident will use. He pointed out that their
drawdowns on the well are not extreme and very little was seen with the aquifer. Mr. Shepherd stated
that both agencies have great programs with others offered by the State.

Council Member Gibbons asked if there were any open ditches that are serviced. It was reported that
there still are some open ditches from Cottonwood Heights to the north. Salt Lake City, by agreement,
serves to the gates of the ditches and individual canal companies serve each of the individual
shareholders on the canal. Spring Creek processes about 2.5 million gallons per day. The rest of the
stream continues the flow down between Valley View and Kentucky Avenues. In the winter months,
it runs into the drain and onto the Great Salt Lake. In the summer months, they open the gates and
there are about 50 residents that have water rights to use that water. The open ditches run down
Arbor, Viewmont, Bonaire, and Fairview Avenues. The water eventually flows into the Salt Lake
Jordan Canal. They also have shares in the Upper Canal. Holiday Water Company administers those
irrigation rights. Mr. Shepherd explained that without the ditches there would be serious drainage
issues. With regard to encroachment and risk, Ms. Briefer stated that Salt Lake City shares the same
concern. They have worked with residents to keep them out of easements so that they can maintain
the ditches. While they deliver water, they also capture storm drainage during different times of the
year.

       d.      Calendar.
               • Council Meeting – August 5, 17, and 19.
The possibility of rescheduling the August 19 City Council Meeting to August 26 was discussed.
Mayor Dahle stated that the intent was to vote on the Truth in Taxation issue on August 19 if the
Council is unable to vote on August 17.

Mr. Godfrey stated that the deadline is August 30. He reported that the Truth in Taxation Process
contemplates that the Council will vote the night of the hearing but it is not legally required. There
is nothing preventing the Council from continuing the public hearing. The Truth in Taxation statute
does, however, require the Council to allow time for any interested person to speak that night. The
law requires the Council to stay and listen for as long as citizens want to speak. The Council could,
however, continue the public hearing if they so desire. Mr. Godfrey stated that the Council is
permitted to change the number based on the input they receive.

Council Member Gibbon supported noticing the hearing for a possible vote that night but leave it
open to the possibility of continuing the hearing and vote to a later date. The decision was made to
reschedule the August 19 City Council Meeting to August 26.
