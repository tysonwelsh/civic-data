2.1 Parry Farms Recovery Update (Staff Presenter, Shane Paddock). .

Public Works Director, Shane Paddock reported on the Parry Farms Retention Pond Recovery and
presented a photo from 2021 showing what the park used to look like. The goal was to get it back
to where it was before. He showed a rendering of the Wood Hollow drainage, which runs through
a culvert. The developer was required to oversize the storm drain system at Parry Farms
Development to handle this extra flow of stormwater that comes from the mountains to the west.
At the time, the pond at Perry Farms was enlarged to handle the extra flow of water. Prior to the
flooding, there was drainage on the south side of Redwood Road near the power substation that
they were keeping an eye on. Within two days, water started to flow swiftly.
Mr. Paddock reported that Salt Lake County plans to install some type of storm retention upstream.
Bluffdale was not the only municipality impacted by the runoff this year. Jordan Valley Water

1.1 Legislative Update on Land Use. (Staff Presenter, Grant Crowell).
Community and Economic Development Director, Grant Crowell, was in the process of reading
through the Code from the Legislature regarding land use. He belongs to the Utah Chapter of the
American Planning Association (“APA”) and has worked with them, the Utah League of Cities
and Towns (“ULCT”), and the Local Governments Trust with each offering a different perspective.

The APA reported that there were 35 bills with 19 that passed. The Land Use and Development
Management Act (“LUDMA”), the ULCT, and the Wasatch Front Regional Council (““WFRC”)
all provided summaries. Some of the major themes were housing and the market. The Legislature
was trying to assist the City in facilitating housing. Some states are requiring duplexes or
fourplexes anywhere a single-family home is allowed and the ULCT is asking for an opportunity
to work on the issue.
The most significant change to housing was to change the reporting date from October to August.
At least one of the three menu items they report on requires work with the City Council on the
analysis of a corridor. Mr. Crowell had attended at least two trainings on the matter in the last
couple of months, which was rigorous. He stated that it will only get more rigorous. He reported
that 90 cities were deemed to be non-compliant last year. Reviews were being conducted on behalf
of the State by the same organizations, such as the Department of Workforce Services who take
their job very seriously. The Legislature added a daily fine that will accrue at $250 per day for
non-compliance. Under some circumstances, it may be possible to appeal but fines will still apply.
Annually, the City needs to report on the effectiveness of its efforts, the outcomes, the barriers,
what was a hindrance, and market conditions. If the City is successful in implementing the
Detached Accessory Dwelling Unit (“D-ADU”) Ordinance, they will have one year to report on it
but they will be able to count it. In the next few years, they will have to come up with another
menu. The Council should review the menu options and prepare a five-year plan. Housing issues
were to be discussed in greater detail over the summer.
Mr. Crowell reported that clarity was added to how the Water Use and Preservation Element is
prepared. Staff and the City’s consultants will likely need to do more work on that and have until
2025 to complete it. When preparing General Plan updates, they are required to provide them to
the WFRC and the Mountainland Association of Governments (“MAG”).
Mr. Crowell reported that the subdivision approval process underwent recent changes. It is an
entirely new section that was added to LUDMA. He explained that the elected officials from any
city or county cannot approve plats. This is common, especially in rural areas, and was changed
only in the last five or six years. As a result, terminology changes will be necessary. Currently,
the land use authority for preliminary plats is the Planning Commission and is still allowed by
State law. The Zoning Administrator is the authority for final plats but they have to create a new
term. Concept plans are no longer allowed to be required. This has been the case as a courtesy or
by request so Bluffdale City is ahead of the curve.
Mr. Crowell reported that the development community and the Legislature wanted to place
substantial limits on the times the City is allowed to turn around reviews and the number of times
a matter can be reviewed. After speaking with attorneys from the State Ombudsman’s Office and
the ULCT, Mr. Crowell thought that the question on the maximum of four review cycles means
preliminary or final. He stated that this can be a resource issue. If the City does not have the
resources to do the 15 days, they fail. Sometimes they have a backlog of 10 or 12 applications. In
practice, it is imperative that they not allow anything to be officially submitted if it is incomplete.

1.2. Discussion on Ordinance No. 2023-08 — TABLED FROM APRIL 26, 2023 -

Amending Bluffdale City Land Use Ordinances Regarding Accessory Dwelling

Definitions in BCC 11.20, City of Bluffdale, Applicant. (Staff Presenter, Grant

Crowell).
Mayor Hall reported that public hearings have already been held on the issue of land use ordinances
regarding ADUs but many residents would like to comment. Staff has received phone calls and
emails. Residents were present tonight who wanted to speak during the regular meeting.
Procedural issues were discussed.
Mr. Crowell was tasked with looking at whether the City could be more clear on the definition of
what they are counting when they use terms like “maximum square footage” or “total square
footage”. The Salt Lake County Assessor’s Office provides a good calculation for floors.
Mr. Crowell explained that there are habitable and conditioned spaces. It was his opinion that
habitable space makes the most sense. It can be used to determine the maximum size of an ADU.
If it is 50% with a primary dwelling that is 4,000 square feet, the habitable space of the ADU can
be 2,000 square feet in size.
Habitable spaces are used for living, sleeping, eating, or cooking. Bathrooms, toilet rooms, closets,
halls, storage, utility spaces, and similar areas are not considered habitable spaces. Mr. Crowell
observed that the size and proportion ranges and trying to create a rule is difficult. The goal is to
remain at 50%. Mr. Crowell was concerned that someone will have a 2,000-square-foot ADU and
use the rest of the 4,000 building as storage.
Council Member Hales commented on habitable space and stated that if someone builds an
accessory dwelling they are not counting bedroom closets, bathrooms, or the garage. As a result,
they could have a home that counts as 3,000 square feet with another 2,000 square feet in closets
and bathrooms. Mr. Crowell confirmed that was the case and stated that people sometimes put
second stories on their accessory buildings but are not allowed to live in them. They are referred
to as unfinished storage spaces or utilities, which are allowed. He explained that what governs the
size of a structure is the total lot coverage and setbacks. Ultimately, the City will not know the
effectiveness or unintended scenarios until something is on the books.
Council Member Hales pointed out that people will use whatever they can to maximize their
buildings. It is not an unintended consequence but an expected consequence. Mr. Crowell expects

Council Member Kallas referenced the 50% language and questioned whether they are better off
removing sub-bullet B to clarify that a subordinate ADU must be 50% of the square footage of all

allowed to construct a 10,000-square-foot ADU with a 20,000-square-foot house under
construction. He considered the scenario of an existing 60’ x 80’ garage with a second story. The

7.2 Discussion and Vote on Resolution No. 2023-22 — Fireworks Restrictions.
(Staff Presenter, Matt Evans).

Fire Chief, Matt Evans, reported that the proposed resolution establishes the fire restrictions for
this year. Every year the map needs to be provided to the County by the end of May. It was the
same map that was used last year and covers the area from Porter Rockwell Boulevard east to
Camp Williams Road. This season was expected to be dry.
Council Member Kallas asked if at some point Bringhurst Station will be safe to discharge
fireworks. Chief Evans suggested they consider it in the future but leave things as they are this
year. Council Member Hales did not want to prevent the 300 homes in the area from being able
to discharge fireworks. Council Member Hales agreed and asked that the map be adjusted to
include Bringhurst Station. Council Member Aston did not believe this was a decision the City
Council should be making. She suggested they defer to the firefighters. Council Member Hales
stated that in fact, the Council makes the decision as they are being asked to approve the resolution.
Council Member Aston asked Council Member Hales if feels he has the knowledge and ability to
determine where it is safe for fireworks to be discharged. She was not comfortable with that and
stated that the map was approved last year.
Chief Evans preferred to stick with the current map. He did not object to looking at Bringhurst
Station next year. Council Member Hales pointed out that three is more grass in other areas that
have been approved. Council Member Gaston asked if they could pass the map and readjust it
later. Chief Evans stated that that could be done if needed, however, the map needs to be provided
to Salt Lake County by the end of the month. Council Member Gaston recalled a dry year when
they had to expand fireworks bans. He suggested getting it submitted and making adjustments
later.

Restrictions. The motion was seconded by Council Member Aston. Vote on motion: Council
Member Crockett-Aye; Council Member Hales-Nay; Council Member Gaston-Aye; Council
Member Aston-Aye; Council Member Kallas-Aye. The motion passed 4-to-1.
