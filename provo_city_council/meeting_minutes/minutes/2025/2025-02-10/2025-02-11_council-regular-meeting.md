1         A Quarterly Report for the 1st Quarter of Fiscal 2025 (25-007) 0:09:09

John Borget, Director of Administrative Services, presented. He said the unaudited report provides an
overview of revenues and expenditures, highlighting trends compared to both the budget and the prior
year. Overall, general fund revenues are slightly below budget but remain above last year’s levels. Sales
tax revenue has been running behind projections due to fluctuations with the State Tax Commission, but
the situation is being closely monitored. Other revenue sources, including transfers, are performing well.
The Recreation, Arts, and Parks (RAP) tax, which follows sales tax trends, is also slightly below
expectations. Several departments are seeing strong performance. Development Services had a strong
first quarter due to large projects, though growth is beginning to slow. Parks and Recreation is on
budget, while the Justice Court has rebounded significantly post-COVID and continues to trend
positively. The library’s revenue is primarily dependent on the November property tax distribution,
making early-quarter assessments less reflective of overall trends. Among the City's enterprise funds,
the golf course, water, wastewater, and energy departments are meeting or exceeding budget
expectations. The airport is performing exceptionally well, with revenues significantly above budget and
the prior year due to increased flights and activity. Sanitation and storm drain revenues are close to
budget, with storm drain slightly behind. The transportation utility is tracking right at budget.
Expenditures for the quarter are at 24%, which is in line with expectations. The only budget
appropriation during this period was for tax increment financing related to Day’s Market. Borget
concluded by noting that financial trends will continue to be monitored closely and invited any
questions from the Council.

     Motion:      An implied motion to approve Ordinance 2025-15, as currently constituted, has been

Hannah Salzl, City Planner, presented. She provided an overview of a proposal to update code to
encourage amenities like EV spaces and pet-friendly open spaces in multi-family developments. The
changes clarify existing requirements without altering square footage, except for two small
amendments. The goal is to set a common minimum standard for amenities, giving developers flexibility
to cater to their market. EV spaces may count toward amenity square footage if not designated as
parking. Staff reviewed recent developments, finding that the new requirements would not be
burdensome. Developers showed general support, with some appreciating the clarity and others seeking
more flexibility.

Councilor Hoban inquired about the reasoning behind valuing playgrounds fewer points than open
spaces for pets, questioning if the assessment was based on the assumption that developments would
be more likely to attract pet owners than families with children. He also suggested that a playground
could serve both purposes and asked for clarification on the rationale behind the scoring.

Ms. Salzl explained that the scoring is not meant to prioritize pets over children but rather reflects the
market demand. Playgrounds are highly desirable in multifamily developments, so the scoring was
designed to encourage amenities that developers are more likely to consider, based on market trends.

Chair Garrett opened the item for public comment.

James Robertson, of Provo, requested a brief, simplified summary of the topic at hand.

Angie Carter, of Provo, expressed appreciation for Councilor Hoban's question and voiced a similar
concern. She felt that the current rating system, which values pets higher than playgrounds, sends the
message that pets are more important than children. She suggested that if the city aims to be family-
friendly, the ratings for pets and playgrounds should be equal and encouraged the council to make an
amendment to reflect this.

Chair Garrett closed public comment.

Councilor Whipple explained that developers are required to provide amenity space for large projects,
and this list of amenities, with assigned weights, is designed to guide them. It allows flexibility for
developers to choose which amenities to include but encourages alignment with council priorities. She
highlighted the importance of offering charging spaces for electric vehicles, as many potential tenants
with EVs are currently limited in housing options. The city aims to incentivize developers to cater to this
growing demand. The amenities must add up to 60 points and meet certain square footage
requirements to ensure substantial offerings.

Chair Garrett called for a vote.

     Vote:        The motion was approved 7:0 with Councilors Bogdin, Christensen, Garrett, Handley,
                  Hoban, MacKay, and Whipple in favor.

Mr. Larson explained that proactive maintenance is cheaper than reactive because it allows for planning,

conditions like winter. Additionally, reactive maintenance can cause further damage, such as flooding,

Councilor Bogdin asked for clarification on why irrigation has been shut off in certain parts of the city.

Mr. Haight clarified that while the city has shares in irrigation companies, it does not directly manage
them. Irrigation systems are run independently, and individual users must contact their irrigation
company to determine why they lost or do not have water rights. He emphasized that the city is not
responsible for cutting off rights; it is up to the irrigation companies, who may sell or lease rights. Mr.
Haight explained that when only a few users are on a line, the burden of maintaining the entire system
falls on those users, which makes it financially unsustainable. In the past, when a farmer owned the
land, they were responsible for maintaining the ditches, making it cost-effective. However, when just
one or two users are left to cover the maintenance costs from the water source to their properties, the
liability becomes overwhelming. He noted that this issue is not unique to Provo but has been seen
historically across Utah counties, including Salt Lake, Utah, and Davis, where irrigation systems were lost
due to similar challenges.

Councilor Bogdin asked about backflow regulators that were mentioned by a resident.

Mr. Haight explained that the city is required by state law to have backflow preventers on every meter.
The city council passed an ordinance to ensure that new developments install them. They are starting
with businesses and will gradually address residential properties, focusing on remodels or cases where
contamination is identified. While the implementation is not aggressive, the city must comply with this
mandate. If a resident remodels or has issues with contamination, the city will work with them to ensure
compliance, but the backflow preventers are a legal requirement.
