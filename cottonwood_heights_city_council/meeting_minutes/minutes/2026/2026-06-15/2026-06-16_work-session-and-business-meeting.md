        3.1     Public Utilities Advisory Board. Ted Boyer.

Mayor Bennion stated that Ted Boyer had served on the Salt Lake City Public Utilities Advisory
Committee for 11 years and would be reporting on what he had learned in that time. Mr. Boyer
stated that Mayor Bennion had asked him to report on the committee’s composition and duties and
provide an overview of water rates.

The Public Utilities Advisory Committee has no decision-making power with the exception of
specific easement matters. Volunteer members serve four-year terms and can be reappointed for
one additional term, with a provision allowing members to continue serving until a replacement is
found. Members represent various demographics including residential, commercial, government,
schools, etc., and some like himself represent water customers who live in Millcreek, Holladay,
and Cottonwood Heights. They provide advice to Salt Lake City Public Utilities Director, Laura
Briefer, Mayor Mendenhall, and the Salt Lake City Council on rates and capital expenditures. Salt
Lake City Public Utilities provides water, sewer, stormwater, and street lighting for Salt Lake City
residents, as well as culinary water for Cottonwood Heights, Millcreek, and Holladay. Ms. Briefer
and many long-term agency employees have considerable experience, including certifications in
specialty areas.

During his career, Mr. Boyer had been in charge of three State agencies, including the Utah
Division of Real Estate and Utah Department of Commerce. He retired as chairman of the Public
Service Commission, which financially regulates monopoly utilities like Rocky Mountain Power
and Enbridge Gas, and was familiar with rate making and design.

Mr. Boyer reported that it was a time of great challenge for Salt Lake City and all cities. Principal
challenges are growth, inflation, and aging infrastructure. Much of the city’s infrastructure is over
50 years old and far beyond its useful life expectancy, and large capital expenditures were required
to bring it up to current standards. A small amount of 100-year-old clay pipe was still present in
the system. The water reclamation plant could no longer meet volume demands due to growth,
particularly in the northwest quadrant, and had struggled to meet federal water quality regulations
for lead, zinc, and other materials. They determined that the correct approach was to demolish the
plant and build a new one at a cost of hundreds of millions of dollars. The new plant was almost
completed. However, the Big Cottonwood Canyon and Salt Lake City metropolitan plants were
also 50 years old. An interconnection line was being installed on Nutree Drive to connect the Big
and Little Cottonwood lines in the event of catastrophic failure or plant shutdown.

Council Member Birrell stated that her understanding was that the project was to bring a line down
from the Big Cottonwood Canyon plan to the water treatment plant. Mr. Boyer clarified that they

Mr. Boyer reported that rate revenue was insufficient to cover service costs and establish a reserve
fund. Salt Lake City historically did not have a reserve fund to cover replacement costs, which
had created a situation where they had old equipment that needed to be replaced but no funds to
do so. The agency had saved approximately $150 million by repurposing materials from the old
reclamation plant and done a good job of budgeting over the past five to 10 years. Rates had
increased as anticipated. The 2025 budget was $764 million; 2026 was $421 million, and 2027
was anticipated to decrease to $343 million. However, that did not factor in replacement costs for
the Big and Little Cottonwood Canyon plants. Another challenge was that rates are set on a cost-
of-service model, which charges customers the actual cost of providing services. Salt Lake City
was looking at different alternatives to cover the shortfall including Impact Fees, property taxes,
and rate or fee increases.

Mr. Boyer discussed the qualifications necessary to serve as his replacement on the Public Utilities
Advisory Committee. They must be a resident of Cottonwood Heights, Millcreek, or Holladay.
Familiar with utilities, ratemaking and design would be helpful but was not necessary. They
should have problem-solving skills and be able to work well with other members who have
differing priorities. They must attend monthly committee meetings and occasional other meetings.
It was a non-compensated, volunteer position.

Residents of Cottonwood Heights pay 35% more for culinary water than Salt Lake City residents.
When he was first appointed to the committee, he asked for the rationale behind the surcharge.
They explained that it was due to legacy costs for water rights, infrastructure, etc. After he
continued to press the subject, the agency hired Raftelis to review the surcharge for appropriateness
in 2018. They determined that many agencies charge a surcharge to customers who live outside
their boundaries due to legacy and other costs. For example, Salt Lake City owns all culinary
water facilities in Cottonwood Heights, so they bear the legal responsibility to repair or
replacement them, ensure compliance and adequacy of service, etc. They also have the obligation
to repay debt and bear all insurance risks. It is more cost-effective for Cottonwood Heights to
purchase water than to create its own infrastructure and buy new water rights. Salt Lake City
followed American Water Works Association revenue requirements in setting the surcharge, and
he had concluded that it was reasonable.

was timely, as Mr. Gerber, Mr. Shipp, and Mayor Bennion, along with leadership from Millcreek
and Holladay, recently met with Salt Lake City Public Utilities to discuss project governance and
planning.

Council Member Hyland asked why Cottonwood Heights does not have voting rights with the

        4.3     Short-Term Rental Discussion – Council Member Suzanne Hyland.

Council Member Hyland reported that the short-term rental (“STR”) program was ready, but there
had been some discussion regarding updating policies prior to launch.

STRs had been a topic of discussion since late 2024. In a June 2025 Work Session, the Council
considered only allowing owner-occupied rentals in residential zones, but no policy decisions were
made. Some residents would like them banned, but other communities had tried to do so and
failed.

STRs could be limited in the following ways:

    •   Specific zones
            o Brighton has definable zones; STRs are allowed in the condominiums around
                Solitude, but not allowed in specific neighborhoods.
    •   Owner-occupancy
    •   City or neighborhood caps

A Short-Term Rental Committee was assembled to consider best practices, and information shared
at the 2025 town hall could be revisited in the upcoming town hall. The Council had discussed
civil enforcement rather than criminal and decided to move forward with software implementation
to enforce existing policy and create a map of STR hotspots to help inform new policies.

Mr. Spung reported that Granicus had pulled data from the various listing platforms and
consolidated it into a dashboard. He then reviewed a map created by the City’s Geographic
Information Systems (“GIS”) specialist that included both Granicus data and City licensing
records. Green dots indicated licensed STRs and red triangles were unlicensed STRs. Yellow
triangles indicated properties listed on STR websites but that require a 30-day minimum stay,
which may be a way to circumvent restrictions. STRs were present throughout Cottonwood
Heights, with 108 licensed and approximately 301 unlicensed rentals.

Now that the data was available, Mr. Spung recommended that the City Council discuss whether
