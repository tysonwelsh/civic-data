 2. DISCUSSION TOPICS
       a. Presentation of the Impact Fee Analysis and Study for Parks, Police, and Fire
          Budget & Management Analyst Becky Condie introduced Fred Philpot, Vice
          President/COO of LRB Public Finance Advisors. Council Member Whitelock asked if Mr.
          Philpot had experience working with the Utah State Legislature. Council Member Green
          asked how much the City paid for LRB’s services. Administrative Services Director Danyce
          Steck said the City paid approximately $72,000 for the study, and emphasized that
          updates every year or two would be much less expensive. Chair McConnehey asked if it
          was common to not have a non-residential impact fee for parks.

          Mr. Philpot said LRB worked with the Utah League of Cities and Towns (ULCT) and
          attended Legislature meetings but was not involved in actively lobbying. He outlined the
          need for, and process involved, in an impact fee analysis and study. Mr. Philpot explained
          that frequency of updates depended on many variables, including assumptions in the
          study, changes in development and growth, and inflation.

          Council Member Green commented that the document included with the agenda packet
          was stamped DRAFT, and Mr. Philpot confirmed changes could be made until the study
          was formally adopted by ordinance. Mr. Philpot said the City’s impact fees were last
          updated in 2017, and LRB was engaged to complete an update to the City’s Impact Fee
          Schedule. He outlined the process for crafting a working Impact Fee Facilities Plan (IFFP)
          and Impact Fee Analysis (IFA): determine demand; provide inventory of existing
          facilities; establish existing and future level of service; identify existing and future capital

          improvements; and conduct a proportionate share analysis.

          Mr. Philpot explained that impact fees for parks and public safety were based on existing
          and future population and calls for service. He explained the collection of data and
          answered questions from the Council. Chair McConnehey expressed interest in how the
          average time per call changed over time, and said he hoped some of the nuance in the
          measurements could be captured. Mr. Philpot suggested regular updates would help
          capture change in need over time. Council Member Green expressed interest in looking at
          the complexity of public safety calls and suggested a yearly update of the demand analysis
          to determine how to better fund fire and police. Mr. Philpot commented that there was a
          delicate balance between specificity and ambiguity in those types of reports, and
          mentioned it was not unheard of for the legality of impact fees to be challenged. He said
          LRB tried to establish methodology that complied with the statute while protecting the
          city with regards to future challenges.

          Mr. Philpot presented data for parks and public safety regarding level of service, and
          existing facilities. Responding to a question from Chair McConnehey, Mr. Philpot
          explained that within Utah it was not standard practice to assess a park impact fee to non-
          residential development. He said he had considered what type of study could evaluate the
          impacts of non-residential development on parks and be used to help support calculation
          of a fee on non-residential, recognizing that it was not a standard across the state and may
          be challenged. Chair McConnehey suggested the City approach the ULCT regarding the
          possibility of park impact fees for non-residential development.

          Council Member Green liked the idea of exploring a trail impact fee. He would prefer a
          transportation impact fee but believed it would not be approved. He believed the City
          would need to find the right balance with a non-residential park impact fee so that
          commercial development would not be chased away. He referred to development
          agreements and public infrastructure districts (PIDs), and asked what other mechanisms
          were available for collecting impact-related revenue. Mr. Philpot listed special
          improvement districts, community reinvestment areas (tax increment investing), funding
          from other government industries, and general fund and utility revenues.

          Mr. Philpot presented data regarding the future needs of parks and public safety based
          on level of service and answered questions from the Council. He explained that impact
          fees needed to be proportionate to demand and should not be used to correct an
          imbalance. Mr. Philpot presented a proportionate share analysis, with a proposed
          increase in single family and multifamily Park Impact Fees, a proposed increase in Fire
          Impact Fees, and a proposed increase in Police Impact Fees.

          Council Member Whitelock expected that residents of multifamily development would
          use parks more than residents of single-family development, and asked why the proposed
          impact fee for multifamily was lower than single family. Mr. Philpot responded that,
          according to data, multifamily units had a lower average household size than single family
          units on a per unit basis. Responding to a question from Chair McConnehey, Mr. Philpot
          explained that the impact fee study was an impact fee eligible expense. Council Member
