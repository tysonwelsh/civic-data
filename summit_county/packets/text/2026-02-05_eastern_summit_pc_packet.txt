RECOMMENDATION: Staff recommends that the Eastern Summit County Planning Commission 
(ESCPC) review the proposed Plat Amendment, conduct a public hearing, and vote to approve 
the Plat Amendment pursuant to the findings of fact, conclusions of law and conditions of 
approval found in this Staff Report. Staff have reviewed the application for compliance with all 
standards in the Eastern Summit County Development Code and have found that it meets the 
requirements for approval.  
 
Project Description 
Project Name:   Promontory Sage Hills Estates Lot 20 Amended Plat 
Applicant(s):   Matthew Idema, Promontory Land Development Director 
Property Owner(s):  Gregg and Lauren Bennett 
Location:   6813 White Dove Way 
Zone District:   Promontory Specially Planned Area 
Parcel Number and Size: PSH-20, 0.59 acres 
Type of Process:   Administrative 
Final Land Use Authority: Eastern Summit County Planning Commission  
 
Proposal 
The applicant, on behalf of the property owners, is requesting to amend the location of the 
drainage easement on Lot 20 of Promontory Sage Hills Estates. The original drainage easement 
encroaches on the Development Envelope. The applicant seeks to rectify this by removing the 
easement from the Development Envelope portion of the lot and relocating it along the 
western edge of the Development Envelope boundary as depicted in Exhibit A. The requested 
amendment would allow the owner to site their house near the rear of the Development 
Envelope and resolves the conflict between the location of the drainage easement and the 
location of the Development Envelope.   
 
 
3
                                             2 
 
Vicinity Map 
 
 
Background 
Promontory Sage Hills Estates Subdivision was recorded 6.10.2022 (Exhibit B). It consists of 42 
lots, four tracts, and two parcels. The lots contain Development Activity Envelopes. The 
drainage easement, if not moved, would prevent the landowner from siting their home in their 
desired location within the Development Envelope. 
 
Removing the drainage easement requires a Plat Amendment because the easement was 
depicted on the plat. The Planning Commission is the Land Use Authority for subdivision plat 
amendments which alter utility easements and all other amendments that do not impact a 
road. 
 
Analysis and Findings 
Section 11-4-5(E.10) of the Eastern Summit County Development Code sets the following 
grounds for vacating or amending a subdivision plat not involving a private or public road:  
 
1. If the Land Use Authority is satisfied that the public interest will not be materially 
injured by the proposed vacation, alteration or amendment, and there is good cause for 
4
                                             3 
 
the vacation, alteration or amendment, the Land Use Authority, may vacate, alter or 
amend the final subdivision plat, or any portion of the final subdivision plat.  
  
Analysis: The requested amendment allows the landowners to construct their home 
within the Development Envelope. The good cause is that the Development Envelope 
will no longer be encroached upon by the drainage easement.  Staff finds no evidence 
that the public or private interest would be materially injured by the proposed 
amendment of the drainage easement. COMPLIES 
 
2. No plat amendment shall be approved which results in an increase in density. 
 
 Analysis: Density is defined in the Code as, “The number of dwelling units per acre with 
respect to residential land uses.” The applicant is requesting to amend the location of a 
drainage easement on an existing residential lot. The amendment would not result in an 
additional dwelling unit. COMPLIES 
 
Public Notice, Meetings and Comments  
Public notice was published in The Park Record and individual notices were mailed to property 
owners within 1,000’ from the boundaries of Lot 20, Promontory Sage Hills Estates. As of the 
date of this report, no public comment has been received. 
 
Findings of Fact: 
1. Promontory Sage Hills Estates Subdivision was recorded July 10, 2022 as part of the 
overall Promontory Specially Planned Area. The subdivision consists of 56.60 +/- acres 
divided into 42 lots, four tracts and two parcels.  
2. Lot 20 of Promontory Sage Hills Estates Subdivision, with parcel number PSH-20, is 
located at 6813 White Dove Way. 
3. Lot 20 consists of 0.59 acres. 
4. Each lot of Promontory Sage Hills Estates Subdivision contains a Development Activity 
Envelope. 
5. On December 18, 2025 the applicant, Matthew Idema, on behalf of the property 
owners, Gregg and Lauren Bennett, submitted a complete plat amendment application 
to amend the drainage easement on Lot 20. 
6. The property owners propose to move the drainage easement to the west of the 
Development Activity Envelope to enable them to site their house at the rear of the 
Development Envelope.  
7. Service Providers including the County Recorder’s Office, County Engineer’s Office and 
Snyderville Basin Water Reclamation District (SBWRD) reviewed the proposed plat 
amendment and gave comments. 
8. Prior to recording the plat, the applicant will update the plat to comply with the 
comments of the Service Providers.  
9. Density is defined in the Code as, “The number of dwelling units per acre with respect to 
residential land uses.”  
10. The amendment would not result in an additional residential unit. No new lots, tracts, or 
parcels are created through the plat amendment.

                                             4 
 
10.  Plat note #23 of Promontory Sage Hills Estates Subdivision gives Promontory 
Investments, LLC the authority to record additional easements. It reads, “Declarant may 
grant easements for utilities whether or not the easements are intended to serve 
Promontory.” 
 
Conclusions of Law:  
1. Public interest will not be materially injured by the proposed amendment and there is 
good cause for the amendment.  
2. The amendment would not result in an additional residential unit. 
 
Conditions of Approval: 
1. All service provider requirements shall be satisfied. 
2. Prior to issuance of a building permit for any structure on Lot 20 of Promontory Sage 
Hills Estates Subdivision, the amended plat shall be recorded in the office of the Summit 
County Recorder.

Chapter 1: Introduction 
What is a General Plan  
 
A General Plan is a long-range policy document that guides the physical development of a 
community. In Utah, general plans are required by the Land Use, Development and Management 
Act (LUDMA) and must address the present and future needs of the County related to growth and 
development in unincorporated areas.  
 
The purpose of the general plan is to make land use planning clear, efficient and predictable by 
identifying the types and locations of development that are appropriate.  
 
The Eastern Summit County General Plan (General Plan) is an advisory, non-binding document 
used by County decision makers to guide land use decisions, coordinate orderly development, and 
advance the County’s vision and goals.  
ﬁ 
Planning Authority  
 
This General Plan is a legally adopted policy document prepared in accordance with Utah 
State law. Utah Code 17 -79-403 identifies the required elements of a county general plan, 
while LUDMA allows flexibility in how these elements are organized and permits  the inclusion 
of additional topics of local importance.  
 
The General Plan is intended to be a living document. As conditions change due to 
development patterns, new information, or evolving community needs, amendments may be 
necessary to keep the Plan current and effective. Amendments may be initiated by the Cou nty 
or requested by the public.  
 
In addition to policies, the General Plan includes implementation strategies and 
recommendations to support Plan goals. The Implementation section of the document is 
designed to be reviewed and updated periodically to track progress and identify new or rev ised 
actions as needed. 
ﬁ 
 
How to Use the Plan 
 
Following the Introduction, this Plan is organized into Chapters (2 -9) based on speciﬁc elements 
(with starred elements required by LUDMA). These elements include Land Use, Housing, 
Transportation and Connectivity, Economic Development, Natural and Cultural Resources, Open 
Space, Community Health and Recreation, Sustainable Development, Water Use, and Public 
Facilities and Services. Each Chapter describes existing conditions, summarizes community 
engagement, and establishes goals, objectives, policies, and strategies.  
 
Goals deﬁne the community’s long-term vision. Objectives identify measurable steps toward these 
goals. Policies guide decision-making, and strategies outline speciﬁc actions. Together they provide 
a framework for implementing the plan.

The General Plan includes a Future Land Use Map illustrating preferred development patterns. This 
map is conceptual and ﬂexible, is not zoning map, and may evolve over time as conditions and 
community priorities change. The map reﬂects the unique character and needs of each area while 
remaining consistent with the overall goals, objectives, policies, and strategies of the plan. 
 
The ﬁnal chapter, implementation, identiﬁes speciﬁc actions, responsibilities, timelines, and 
funding considerations necessary to carry out the Plan and achieve its goals.  
  
Summit County Context  
 
Eastern Summit County has a long history shaped by its land, water, and natural resources. For 
thousands of years, the area was used by the Shoshone and Ute Tribes, who relied on its rivers, 
valleys and mountain landscapes for seasonal travel, hunting, gat hering, and trade. Their 
stewardship established a deep connection between people and the land that continues to inﬂuence 
how the region is valued today.  
 
European American settlement in the mid -1800 introduced permanent communities centered on 
agriculture and resource use. Towns such as Henefer, Coalville, and Kamas developed as farming, 
ranching, and timber production hubs. Reliable water sources supported irrigation and livestock, 
establishing a rural working landscape that remains a deﬁning feature of Summit County.  
 
Mining and logging later expanded the local economy and shaped transportation networks and 
settlement patterns. Coal production in Coalville and mining  activity tied to Park City created 
employment and regional connections, while logging in the Kamas Valley supplied timber to 
surrounding communities. These industries brought growth but also reinforced the County’s 
dependence on its natural resources.  
 
By the mid -20th century, extractive industries declined, and Eastern Summit County began to 
transition toward recreation, tourism, and residential development. Changes in forest management, 
improved transportation, and the rise of outdoor recreation shifted land use prio rities toward 
conservation, access, and quality of life. The area became valued not only for production, but also 
for its scenery, open space, and recreational opportunities.  
 
Today, Eastern Summit County reﬂects a blend of its agricultural roots and its role as a recreational 
and residential landscape. Working farms and ranches remain alongside growing interest in tourism, 
outdoor recreation, and rural living. This mix shapes current planning challenges, including 
protecting community character, managing growth, preserving open space and water resources, and 
balancing private property rights with long-term environmental stewardship.  
 
Current planning conditions include increased interest in new forms of development and 
governance, as well as proposals that could signiﬁcantly inﬂuence land use patterns, service 
delivery, and community identity. These emerging issues highlight the impo rtance of clear policy 
direction and proactive planning.

Chapter 2: Land Use (LU) 
Purpose 
The Land Use Element establishes the framework for how land is used, developed, and conserved in 
Eastern Summit County. It guides zoning, development decisions, and infrastructure planning in a 
manner that balances growth, community needs, and the protection of rural character , open spaces, 
and natural resources. The policies in this chapter provide direction for where development should 
occur, the form it should take, and how land use decisions support the long-term vision of Eastern 
Summit County. 
  
The broader regional context, existing conditions, and community engagement ﬁndings that inform 
the Land Use Element are described in Chapter 1. This Chapter builds upon that foundation by 
translating community values and planning priorities into land use goals and policies.  
 
Community engagement consistently emphasized the importance of protecting open space and 
rural character, improving housing options, managing traffic, and supporting vibrant, well designed 
commercial areas. 
 
Existing Conditions  
 
Eastern Summit County’s land use currently includes a mix of residential, agricultural, recreational, 
and conservation areas. Zoning also allows for limited commercial uses along the highway corridor 
from Henefer to Woodland. Residential densities range from approximately one unit per ﬁve acres to 
one unit per eighty acres, with higher densities concentrated in the established unincorporated 
communities of Woodland, Marion, Oakley, Peoa, Wanship, Hoytsville, Upton and Echo. 
 
The Easten Summit County Development Code states that the intent of this zoning framework is to: 
 
“…ensure that the rural, agricultural and small-town character of the eastern portion of the 
County shall remain, even in the presence of growth and change. The County intends to 
assure the managed, proper, and sensitive development of land to protect and enhance 
these desired qualities and the lifestyle that exists.” 
 
In practice, the current zoning pattern supports a concentration of development within e x i sting 
community areas while maintaining large areas of agricultural and rural land at low densities. This 
approach reﬂects long-standing efforts to preserve rural character, protect agricultural lands, and

Explanation of why we have identified certain areas for future land uses  
The future land use plan for Eastern Summit County reflects a balance between the need for 
development and the desire to protect the area’s unique natural environment. Key features of the 
future land use plans include: 
1. Rural Residential Areas:  Expansion of rural residential neighborhoods with a focus on large 
lot sizes to maintain the area's low-density character. These areas will allow for single-family 
homes and small -scale agricultural uses. Prioritize clustered housing to preserve open 
space or existing agricultural uses. 
2. Growth Areas:  Designated areas for higher -density development, including mixed -use  
zones, to promote compact, walkable communities with opportunities for both residential 
and commercial development. These growth areas will be located near existing 
infrastructure, transportation corridors, and employment centers. 
3. Agricultural Protection Zones:  Establishment of zones to protect the County's agricultural 
lands from urban sprawl. These areas will focus on preserving the viability of farming and 
ranching operations, as well as the open space values they provide. 
4. Opens Space and Conservation Areas:  Designation of high-priority conservation areas that 
will be protected for ecological value, including riparian zones, wetlands, wildlife corridors, 
and watershed protection areas. 
5. Recreation and Tourism Areas:  Continued development of tourism-related infrastructure, 
including trailheads, campgrounds, and outdoor recreation areas. These areas will be zoned 
to ensure that tourism-related development does not negatively impact natural resources. 
6. Mixed -Use Development:  Encouragement of mixed -use developments, particularly in 
areas surrounding existing towns and communities. These areas will include opportunities 
for commercial, residential, and recreational spaces in a well -planned, integrated 
environment. Support affor dable housing initiatives to meet the needs of residents and 
workers.  
7. Industrial : This designation is for areas where manufacturing, warehousing, and light 
industrial activities can occur. It should be located in areas that minimize conflicts with 
residential and recreational uses and are well-served by transportation infrastructure. 
8. Infrastructure and Transportation:  Expansion of infrastructure (water, sewer, roads, and 
transit) to support future development in urban growth and mixed-use areas. Transportation 
planning will emphasize connectivity and sustainability, focusing on multi -modal transit 
options, including public transit, bike paths, and pedestrian-friendly developments.

Housing Goals, Policies, & Strategies 
Rather than being monolithic, moderate -income housing is a multifaceted challenge for Summit 
County. It is tied closely to a variety of challenges including extremely high median housing costs; 
traffic congestion and legacy transportation issues; availabili ty of  suffi ci ent local laborers; busi ness 
challenges due to lack of access to labor; an increasingly non- diverse community; a shrinking 
number of full -time residents with long -term community and generational ties; increasing 
environmental degradation due to increasing development pressures; concerns about sustainability 
of air, land, and water natural resources; and the critical need to manage and limit rapid growth in a 
County that seeks to preserve open spaces, agricultural and cultural heritage (collectively, “Critical 
Concerns”). Accordingly, creating standards and strategies concerning moderate-income housing in 
this Chapter would be incomplete, ineffective, and potentially harmful to the vision and goals of this 
General Plan as well as the County Council’s vision, values, and strategic goals without carefully 
evaluating the impacts of any such standards and strategies on the Critical Concerns to determine if 
they will aggravate or mitigate and provide solutions to those Critical Concerns. All such standards 
and strategies and the Critical Concerns should continually be evaluated given the dynamic nature 
of growth in and on the borders of the County. The Summit County Manager, relevant staff as 
determined by the County Manager, and the County Council should regularly assess the County’s 
moderate-income housing needs and seek to articulate where such housing is most appropriate and 
for whom the need is greatest at any given moment always taking into careful consideration the 
Critical Concerns (“Moderate-Income Housing Assessment”). A priority of any such assessment 
should seek to identify if housing challenges are creating signiﬁcant labor shortages in critical areas 
such a law enforcement, emergency medical services, education, doctors and nurses and othe r 
health care professionals, and local government workforce. In addition, the Moderate -Income 
Housing Assessment should examine the needs of long-term resident seniors seeking to age in place 
in the County as well as the County’s tourism industry’s workfor ce. With respect to tourism 
workforce, any strategies developed in this Chapter 7 should seek to require major employers to bear 
some of the burden of providing employees with housing opportunities as well as reasonable wages 
that consider the high housing costs i n resort counti es much li ke the di fference i n wages pai d i n rural 
Utah compared to San Francisco, California. 
 
39
External inﬂuences also need to be part of the Moderate -Income Housing Assessment, such as 
whether nightly rentals are removing long-term housing from the available housing capacity with a 
result of hollowing out formerly well-established neighborhoods, particularly outside of resort cores. 
Such assessment should consider any moderate-income housing permitted, platted, commenced 
and/or built since the prior Moderate-Income Housing Assessment. Careful consideration of where 
to locate housing is a fundamental question the Moderate-Income Housing Assessment needs to 
examine in each instance with a focus on the Critical Concerns. For example, building or incentivizing 
affordable housing in the Snyderville Basin for County workforce that work primarily in County offices 
in Coalville requires commuting, which the moderate-income housing plan should seek to reduce. 
In addition, approving a large ratio of market rate housing to obtain a smaller ratio of affordable 
housing exacerbates the dearth of affordable housing  given the additional services and workers 
required to service the market rate housing, and should be avoided. The critical questions of each 
Moderate-Income Housing Assessment should include for whom, ideal location(s), transit and 
transportation access, trails access, and access to services to reduce commuting and car trips, as 
well an assessment of the state of each of the Critical Concerns.  Those preparing the Moderate -
Income Housing Assessment need to include speciﬁc metrics that are transparent, effecti v e, and 
easy to understand that can be used to provide positive or negative impacts that may be used by the 
County to adjust the goals and strategies contained in this Chapter 7. 
 
With respect to all analyses and changes to land use regulations contained in this Chapter 7, the 
County should carefully consider the positive and negative impacts on each of the Critical Concerns 
to make sure any such changes are likely to reduce deﬁcits identiﬁed in the Moderate -Income 
Housing Assessment and do not result in unintended consequence s that negatively impact the 
Critical Concerns. 
 
Moderate income housing is deﬁned as: “Housing occupied or reserved for occupancy by 
households with a gross household income equal to or less than 80% of the median gross income 
for households of the same size in the county in which the housing is located. ” However, given that 
the median gross income for the County is substantially higher than the national average , 
consideration should be given to requiring moderate -income housing that is targeted to lower 
median gross income depending upon the intended solutions sought as identiﬁed in the Moderate-
Income Housing Assessment. GOAL: Provide moderate-income housing opportunities that address

housing deﬁcit due to the additional impacts created by the market rate housing and coincident 
infrastructure, services, and other needs that generate requirements for additional workforce. The 
primary goal should be meaningful reductions in existing deﬁcits identiﬁed in the Moderate-Income 
Housing Assessment.

Chapter 6: Natural and Cultural Resources 
The Natural and Cultural Resources Element provides guidance for preserving, protecting, and 
enhancing and the natural environment and a sense of community through new development. The 
Element aims to highly prioritize the conservation of open space by incentivizing compact, 
sustainable development in appropriate areas. The Element also aims to preserve the rural character 
of the area while more actively supporting the less-celebrated identities in Eastern Summit County.  
This Element is separate from the Resource Management Plan, required for compliance with House 
Bill (HB) 219, Resource Management Planning by Local Governments, amended Section 17 -27a -
401, and HB160. Summit County’s Resource Management Plan, and element of this General Plan, 
can be found here. The Resource Management Plan identiﬁes resource management strategies for 
public lands held by the Bureau of Land Management (BLM) and the US Forest Service within Summit 
County.  
The following strategies in the remainder of this chapter act as guiding tools to protect natural and 
cultural resources as they relate to land use and development, outside of those federally managed, 
public lands. 
Context 
Eastern Summit County provides a gateway to many protected parks and natural areas, including 
Rockport State Park, Echo State Park, the Hi stori c Uni on Paci ﬁc Rai l Trai l State Park, and notably the 
Uinta-Wasatch-Cache National Forest. The protected lands combined with large swaths of 
agricultural open space have allowed Eastern Summit County to retain a rural feel as the community 
has changed. Because agricultural land uses have preserved views, wetlands, and migration 
corridors, much of the area with higher development pressures are not preserved as open space.  
Community members highly associate their quality of life with the abundance of open space and 
quality of the natural environment. But interestingly, Eastern Summit County communities grew 
because of natural resource extraction. The industry of each community and railroad history is still 
directly tied with the culture of the area, although often, indirectly shown. Few signiﬁcant, historic 
buildings remain.  
Similarly, despite the historic presence of Native peoples in Summit County, few sites, public 
education and art exhibits, or cultural events exist?. More can be done to incorporate and celebrate 
history of past and present cultures. Recent population growth has come with changing 
demographics. Although still predominantly white, Eastern Summit County has a growing Latino 
population, that is often largely underrepresented in community events and resources.

Chapter 7: Community Health and Recreation 
The Community Health and Recreation Element considers how our built environments affect our 
physical, mental, and social health. The Element strives to inﬂuence built environments in ways that 
foster good health outcomes.  
This Element is supplemented by the Summit County Rail Trail Corridor Plan, which speciﬁcally 
considers recreation along the Historic Union Paciﬁc Rail Trail. The Rail Trail Corridor Plan is a 
separate Element of this General Plan.  
Context 
Community health in Eastern Summit County is shaped by the close-knit communities but limited 
convenient access to health infrastructure. Access to healthcare services, while improving, remains 
a challenge. The population is increasingly focusing on wellness and outd oor activities, however , 
mental health services, substance abuse treatment, and care for the aging population could be 
improved. Compounding vulnerabilities create disparate differences in health outcomes for some 
populations.  
Recreation has historically been directed toward the National Forests and State Parks in the area, 
but there is a growing desire from the community for more comprehensive, local recreation 
opportunities. Although North Summit Recreation was recently created, they are limited in capacity 
and are primarily focused on recreation programming and community sports. South Summit 
residents have not voted in sufficient numbers for a recreation district. Instead, non-proﬁts like the 
South Summit Trails Foundation have been slowly working to build and maintain trails in the area 
while the cities of Oakley, Kamas, and Francis have some facilities like city parks with various courts 
and playground equipment.

comparison to places in the rest of the state, Summit County struggles in a few key areas. Summit 
County is less healthy or signiﬁcantly less healthy than the rest of the state in the following 
categories: low-income renter and homeowner sever cost burden, park access, traffic volume, high 
school enrollment, Theil Index of Racial Segregation, motor vehicle crashes, median rent, teen

I. Introduction  
In recent years, drought, climate change, and rapid population growth have placed increasing 
strain on Utah’s limited water resources. On April 21, 2022, Governor Spencer J. Cox issued an 
Executive Order declaring a state of emergency due to drought conditions in Utah. That summer 
the Great Salt Lake also reached its lowest levels ever recorded1. In response to the declaration, 
the governor’s office released an action plan for water, which included better integrating water 
and land use planning at the local level 2. As a result of this increasing pressure on the state’s 
water resources, in 2022 the Utah state legislature passed SB 110: Water as Part of a General 
Plan. SB 110 requires municipalities and counties to develop general plans that consider the 
effect of de velopment on water demand and water infrastructure, methods of reducing water 
demand and per capita water use for existing and future development, and potential changes to 
local government operations to reduce water waste.3 This section of the Eastern Summit County 
General Plan meets the intent of both SB 110 and other statutory general plan requirements, 
including agricultural protection (UT Code § 17-27a-403).4   
In Summit County, climate change is expected to result in more frequent and severe droughts, 
thus increasing water scarcity in the County. According to a Woodwell Climate Research Center 
Climate Risk Assessment Report for Summit County, in the past two decades (2000–2020), central 
Summit County experienced severe drought less than 20% of the time. By mid -century (2040–
2060), severe drought stress will increase to less than 30% of the time.5 As of August 2025, 100% 
of Summit County was experiencing drought conditions with 65% experiencing severe or extreme 
drought, according to the U.S. Drought Monitor.6   
Summit County also continues to grow  albeit more slowly than much of the State. By 2050, the 
population is projected to reach 55,373, a 19.5% increase from the County’s 2025 population of 
approximately 44,556. A growth rate of approximately 0.78% per year. 7 According to the

Water Rights in Summit County 
Water rights are essential for the Summit County government to ensure legal access to water 
for municipal, agricultural, recreational, and environmental uses. A comprehensive water 
rights plan assists the County in the management of existing resources efficiently, avoids 
forfeiture due to nonuse, and adapts to changing land use and infrastructure needs. A 
proactive plan also supports long-term sustainability, compliance with state regulations, and 
equitable distribution of water across County assets. 
Understanding and managing water rights in Utah is incredibly complex. Water rights are 
governed by Utah Code Title 73, which declares all water in the state to be public property 
(§73-1-1) and establishes beneficial use as the basis, measure, and limit of all rights to use 
water (§73-1-3). Rights may be lost through abandonment or nonuse over a seven-year 
period unless a nonuse application is filed (§73-1-4). Water rights are acquired and modified 
through formal applications to appropriate, change, or exchange water, and must be actively 
maintained through filings and compliance with deadlines. 
It is also important to distinguish water rights from water shares. A water right is a legal 
entitlement to divert and use water from a specific source, while a water share represents 
ownership in a water company or irrigation entity and entitles the holder to a portion of the 
company’s water, subject to its rules and infrastructure. Water shares do not automatically 
confer a standalone water right and often require separate change applications to be used 
outside their original context (§73-1-11). While a comprehensive water rights analysis is not a 
component of this Water Plan, Summit County is actively working toward that goal for all 
County-owned water rights.

Wastewater Treatment in Eastern Summit County 
Eastern Summit County municipalities, including Kamas, Francis, Oakley, Coalville, and Henefer, 
collect and treat wastewater at water reclamation facilities before placing it back in local 
streams and rivers. Properties that are not connected to municipal wastewater lines use 
private, onsite wastewater treatment systems (or septic systems). Summit County’s 
Environmental Health Department maintains a septic program to ensure the safe design and 
permitting of septic systems.28 
Centralized wastewater treatment is often considered beneficial for residents and businesses 
given the consistency of service and the lower maintenance costs. It also supports Summit 
County’s goal to protect and restore local stream flows. Within city limits, new development is 
required to tie into public sewer connections.

VI. Water Efficient New Development  
Summit County is committed to ensuring water resiliency, water quality, and the efficient use of 
water in new development. Eastern Summit County’s current development code is intended to 
protect the rural nature of the region and to promote and encourage t he preservation of 
agricultural lands. Only low densities are permitted in agricultural areas and the base density in 
residential areas is one unit per 2.5-acres at its highest. The Village Overlay Zone allows for higher 
density and compactness in unincorp orated communities which results in lower water demand 
per unit. 
Clustered development is also encouraged to protect wetlands, riparian areas, steep slopes, 
ridgelines, and other environmentally sensitive areas wherever practicable. In some zones, an 
incentive density is provided for developments with units that are clustered on the development 
site plan and preserve a percentage of the parcel as meaningful open space. Cluster development 
reduces water demand per unit and prevents sprawling developments, thus tempering 
unmitigated growth.  
The development code ensures watershed protection by prohibiting any development within 
1,500ft above and 100ft below each spring used for culinary use or public water supply where 
the development could possibly pollute the water source. No structure can be located within 40ft 
of a wetland or within 100ft of a year-round naturally occurring stream, a reservoir, lake or pond. 
 
Stormwater Management in Summit County  
In addition to this General Plan, the County has a Stormwater Management Plan reviewed 
and updated annually, as needed,that outlines the County’s stormwater system and goal to 
reduce the amount of pollutants entering streams, lakes and rivers as a result of runoff from 
residential, commercial, industrial and community areas.33 The Summit County Stormwater 
Division works diligently to protect waterways by promoting practices that reduce 
stormwater runoff and pollutants entering waterways. This work includes both regulatory 
and permitting aspects as well as community education, such as fairs,events and tabling

Call out box - Instream Flow Dedication in Utah  
 
Much in the way that some of our water resources are dedicated to agricultural or municipal 
uses, in Utah, water rights can be dedicated to instream flows in order to keep sufficient 
water flowing in the river channel throughout the season to support wildlife, the aquatic 
environment, and water levels in the Great Salt Lake. In 2022, the Utah Legislature passed HB 
33 – Instream Flow Amendments which significantly expanded the scope of Utah’s instream 
flow laws. Under the current law the Division of Wildlife Resources, Division of State Parks, 
and the Division of Forestry, Fire, and State Lands as well as any person, subject to the 
approval of a cooperating Division, can apply for instream flow change applications. HB 33 
allows any division or person to file a change application seeking to use a water right for “an 
instream flow within a specified section of a natural or altered stream” or “on sovereign 
lands” (including the Great Salt Lake). The State Engineer’s Office provides pre-consultations 
for any proposed change application. And environmental organizations like Trout Unlimited, 
The Nature Conservancy, and Western Resource Advocates may also be a resource to water 
rights holders that are interested in dedicating their rights as instream flows.
