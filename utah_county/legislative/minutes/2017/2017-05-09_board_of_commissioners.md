6.      APPROVE THE UTAH COUNTY OFFICE OF HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT DRESS
        CODE POLICY
(Track A, 15:25)
Commissioner Graves thanked Utah County Human Resources Director Cammie Taylor for holding discussions
with her staff regarding the proposed Dress Code Policy, as the Board had previously recommended. When
Commissioner Ivie asked whether the new policy would encourage teamwork, Ms. Taylor noted it will help
professionalism, but communication cannot be determined solely by what people wear. Commissioner Lee
expressed concern that some employees are under the impression this is a county-wide dress code, and Ms. Taylor
clarified it is solely for the Human Resource Management Office.

Commissioner Lee aired some grievances regarding the proposed dress code. He pointed out the sentence including
“cleanliness and good hygiene is expected on a daily basis,” stating it seems subjective and can be weaponized to
target certain employees. Cammie Taylor explained the policy is there as a guideline to facilitate conversation, not a
disciplinary tool; perceived violations would be resolved by private conversation. Ms. Taylor mentioned scent
sensitivity and Commissioner Ivie summarized potential health concerns. The group discussed tattoos, and
Commissioner Lee shared he has a personal pet peeve regarding professional appearance being waived on Fridays.
Ms. Taylor responded ‘casual Fridays’ are an industry standard in many office environments across the country.
Commissioners Graves and Ivie stated they are comfortable with approving the policy since the entire department
was involved in the decision.

                                          PUBLIC COMMENTS
(Track A, 30:05)
Linda Hopkinson came forward to comment on a concerning issue: wrongful convictions and wrongful
incarcerations. She read a prepared statement (a copy of which can be found attached to these minutes) about Gary
Dotson, who in 1989 was exonerated and released from prison after DNA evidence proved his innocence. Ms.
Hopkinson explained there are serious psychological consequences of wrongful prosecutions, but generally little or
no reimbursement for the innocent parties whose lives are altered. Ms. Hopkinson and her associates a proposing a
resolution to create the Utah County Restorative Justice Commission to address this issue. She presented a ten-
minute video with excerpts from an episode of ‘Dateline’ which covered the story of Roger and Pam Mortensen,
suspects in the 2009 murder of retired Brigham Young University professor Kay Mortensen. The video included
interviews with Roger and Pam Mortensen, and the prosecuting Utah County Deputy Attorney Tim Taylor. Mr. and
Ms. Mortensen were arrested and spent several months in jail before another witness came forward with information
that led to their release. While Mr. Taylor apologized for the mistake, a lawsuit filed by Mr. and Ms. Mortenson
was dropped because the U.S. Supreme Court has determined grand jury witnesses and prosecutors are immune to
civil litigation.

(Track A, 44:55)
Linda Hopkinson mentioned the television show ‘Dateline’ is returning to Utah County to report on Conrad Truman,
another individual exonerated by a ‘Not Guilty’ jury verdict in the last few months after spending four years in jail.
The prosecutor in the Roger and Pam Mortensen, and Conrad Truman cases, is the same person.

(Track A, 46:30)
Pleasant Grove City Councilmember Ben Stanley talked about the importance of consequences for misconduct. He
expressed governments and entities must be held to the same standards, and take accountability in the same way as
individuals. Councilmember Stanley stated reform is needed, as these high-profile errors are tarnishing the
reputation of the Utah County Attorney’s Office. He encouraged the commissioners to approve oversight and
support the Utah County Restorative Justice Commission.

(Track A, 46:30)
Utah County resident Douglas Tullis came forward to state his son, and the son of Linda Hopkinson, used to play on
the same basketball team. Mr. Tullis explained that while his son went on to obtain a degree in Chemistry from the
University of Utah and is now employed locally as a pharmacist, Ms. Hopkinson’s son is still trying to emotionally
recover from the same issues faced by Roger and Pam Mortensen because of a wrongful conviction. Mr. Tullis
stated implementing a Restorative Justice Commission is a small step, but it is a very needed step for people who do
not have a voice in this community.

(Track A, 51:40)
Katy Bischel read a story on behalf of Elizabeth Craig (a copy of which can be found attached to these minutes).
Elizabeth Johnson Craig was Miss Utah in the Miss America Scholarship Pageant in 1991. She then married, had
children, and dedicated her time to volunteering and motivational speaking. In 2009 she became involved with a
charity representative who sold high-end products online and took the profits from those sales to help impoverished
children. This was a potential violation of company policy, which led to Ms. Craig’s arrest. Ms. Craig’s letter
stated Utah County Attorney Jeff Buhman levied outrageous charges against her, treating her as though part of an
organized crime family and causing her to lose public speaking engagements, jobs, and friends. All of the charges
were dismissed in December 2010. While Ms. Craig is no longer facing the unfair possibility of life in prison, she
had spent $78,000 in attorney fees to defend herself and now spends her days cleaning restrooms rather than flying
around the country changing lives with her speeches. Ms. Craig does not want any other innocent people to suffer in
the same way.

(Track A, 56:25)
Orem resident Dave Duncan encouraged the commissioners to approve the resolution for a Utah County Restorative
Justice Commission. Because of governmental immunity, there is little recourse for people whose lives are so
dramatically altered by prosecutor mistakes. Mr. Duncan noted that some may argue accountability can be
demanded through the election process, but that only occurs every four years and it is only for one attorney – it does
not address the root of the problem.

(Track A, 57:50)
Former Provo City Councilmember Steve Turley was accused of ten felonies by the Utah County Attorney’s Office.
Mr. Turley explained he resigned his position as an elected official and spent a lot of money as a result of these
charges. He recently bumped into an acquaintance that asked Mr. Turley whether he had already “served time” for
the alleged crimes, which were all dismissed at a preliminary hearing. Mr. Turley asked the commissioners to
consider the impact on their lives if they had been in the same situation. He has spent four and a half years dealing
with the aftermath, and it is far from over. Mr. Turley noted the only chance of justice is through litigation, and he
despises litigation. A Restorative Justice Committee could held educate the commissioners, as the entrusted
representatives of the people, to make the right decisions.
