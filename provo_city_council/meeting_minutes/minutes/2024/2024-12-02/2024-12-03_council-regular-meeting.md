2.         An ordinance amending the zone map classification of real property, generally located at
           577 East 400 South, from the one-family residential (R1.6) zone to the low density
           residential (LDR) zone. Maeser Neighborhood. (PLRZ20240234) 0:37:14

     Motion:       An implied motion to approve an Ordinance, as currently constituted, has been made
                   by council rule.

Mary Barnes, City Planner, presented. She provided a brief overview of a rezone request previously
discussed in the work session. The property, located at 577 East 400 South in the Maeser neighborhood,
is proposed to be rezoned from R1-6 to Low-Density Residential (LDR). The request aligns with the
General Plan and the Maeser Neighborhood Plan. The Planning Commission recommended approval on
November 13. The owner intends to build two attached single-family homes, or town homes, in the
backyard of an existing single-family home. Similar residential developments exist in the area, including
multi-family housing nearby. The concept plan proposes access from the rear alleyway, but the final site
configuration would be determined in the project plan if approved. Staff recommend approval, citing
consistency with the General Plan and the goal of increasing housing diversity in the neighborhood.

Councilor Whipple inquired whether the proposed units would be available for rent or sold for owner-
occupied housing. She asked if the property owner was present to clarify his intentions regarding future
residents.

Daniel Franco, property owner, stated that he does not currently plan to sell the units but intends to
keep them within his family for his children. While he acknowledged the possibility of selling in the
future, the current zoning and lot size prevent him from subdividing the property for individual sales. He
also noted that he does not currently live at the property and is unsure whether this project will
incentivize him to move there and occupy the home.

Councilor Whipple asked Mr. Franco if his goal was to create a financial investment for his family rather
than specifically providing a place for his children to live.

Mr. Franco confirmed that it could be either, acknowledging the financial stability aspect. He also
confirmed that he initially considered subdividing the property but was unable to due to zoning and lot
size restrictions.

Councilor Whipple asked Ms. Barnes whether the inability to subdivide the property was due to a lack of
street access. She referenced three neighboring parcels to the east, noting that their combined size
matches Mr. Franco’s lot and sought clarification on the subdivision limitations.

4.         An ordinance amending Provo City Code regarding special use permits for accessory
           dwelling units. Citywide application. (PLOTA20240275) (24-016) 1:19:09

     Motion:       An implied motion to approve Ordinance 2024-61, as currently constituted, has been
                   made by council rule.

Melia Dayley, the Council Policy Analyst, presented an amendment to Section 14.3040 concerning
special use permits, specifically the pathways for ADUs. The proposed change aims to eliminate the
individual property Administrative Approval pathway, which allowed property owners to apply for a
special use permit for an ADU that would only be valid for them. This permit is not tied to the land and
would be revoked if the owner sells or moves. The proposal retains the multiple properties pathway,
which still necessitates obtaining signatures from neighbors and requires approval from the council.

Chair MacKay opened public comment.

Cam Valgardson, a resident of Salem and general manager of Irontown Modular, spoke about his
experience with producing ADUs in Utah. Originally from Provo, he shared that his company, which
builds factory-built homes primarily for out-of-state markets, is now focusing on ADUs due to the
housing crisis. Valgardson mentioned that they are starting a project in Provo and faced several
challenges when determining the feasibility of building an ADU for a client in the Edgemont area. He
highlighted barriers such as the plotting of the property, which designated common space in yards and
restricted private areas to just the house. Additionally, he pointed out the high costs associated with
connecting to sewer and water mains, with a potential $20,000 to $30,000 difference for front yard
connections versus going through the sidewalk, curb, gutter, and street. Lastly, he noted the setback
requirements of 10 feet on a quarter-acre lot, which would place the ADU in the middle of the backyard,
complicating the project further.

David Magleby, of Provo, addressed the council regarding the housing preferences of local citizens,
emphasizing that while there are varying opinions on the issue of ADUs, many Provo residents were not
present to voice their views. He referenced a survey conducted by Y2 Analytics, which indicated that a
significant majority of residents (39%) preferred single-family homes with large yards, compared to only
22% who identified ADUs as the most needed housing option. In fact, ADUs ranked fifth in terms of
housing needs, following single-family homes, student housing, townhomes with individual yards, and
apartments. He encouraged the council to review the survey data, which also highlighted varying
housing preferences across different council districts, noting that no district prioritized ADUs above
other housing types. He urged the council to respect long-standing zoning laws, which are intended to
protect property rights and manage community expectations regarding issues such as commercial
development and property use.

Carlos Sissori, of Provo, expressed his concerns about the removal of the option to apply for ADUs
without a zoning change. He argued that the requirement to gather signatures from neighbors, which
must include a unanimous approval, is overly restrictive for homeowners who wish to accommodate
family members, such as aging parents, while still allowing them some independence. He emphasized
that he supports reasonable restrictions but believes that current regulations limit individual freedoms
