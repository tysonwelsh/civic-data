II.    Sustainability Update – Samantha DeSeelhorst, Sustainability Analyst.
       a.     HB 411 Update.
Samantha DeSeelhors, Sustainability Analyst is a shared employee with the cities of Cottonwood
Heights and Millcreek. Christopher Thomas was also present representing Salt Lake City. Ms.
DeSeelhorst discussed the Community Renewable Energy Program, which was also referred to as
House Bill 411. It created a legal pathway for communities to achieve net-100% renewable energy
by partnering with Rocky Mountain Power. Ms. DeSeelhorst explained that net-100% renewable
energy means that participating communities will still be connected to the standard electricity grid.
However, annual consumption would be offset through the production of new renewable resources.

Ms. DeSeelhorst reported that the first step was to pass a resolution by the end of 2019 that aspires
for net-100% renewable energy by 2030. Holladay and 20 other Utah communities completed that
step, which made them eligible to continue to participate. Ms. DeSeelhorst discussed steps that would
need to be taken in the future that includes the Governance Agreement, which outlined protocols for
voting, cost-sharing, and other administrative elements of the program. The agreement must be
signed by all participating communities by July 31, 2021. Staff representatives from each of the
communities had been collaborating on a draft of the Governance Agreement and legal counsel is
now reviewing the draft.

Several costs were associated with the Community Renewable Energy Program. The draft agreement
proposed two methods including Non-Noticing Costs and Noticing Cost. Non-Noticing Costs were
costs associated with the program, which included a community expert, Rocky Mountain Power filing
fee, and an Office of Consumer Services and/or Department of Public Utility expert. The draft
proposed that the fees be split among the participating communities, based 50% on the electric load
and 50% on the population. The Noticing Cost was the cost to send the two required opt-out notices

Ms. DeSeelhorst further described the costs related to the program. She reported that the total

Ms. DeSeelhorst noted that if needed, the total cost could be split over two fiscal years.

Council Member Quinn wondered if there would be ongoing costs to the City. Ms. DeSeelhorst
explained that once the program is up and running, the costs will shift to community members who
are paying the rates. Council Member Quinn asked about future costs to community members.
Ms. DeSeelhorst noted that a maximum rate increase cap could be suggested during the technical
agreement process. She did not have exact numbers but similar programs had seen very small rate
increases of approximately 2%. Ms. DeSeelhorst offered to keep City Council updated as she found
out more specific information.

         b.     Sustainability Action Plan Update.
Ms. DeSeelhorst updated the City Council on the Interlocal Sustainability Action Plan. She reported
that after Holladay, Millcreek and Cottonwood Heights signed an Interlocal Agreement to collaborate
on sustainability projects, staff felt it was important to put together a plan that outlined priorities. This
would allow all efforts to be coordinated. Staff and community partners from each city created a draft
of the plan which would be brought to the City Council for review and feedback in the future.

        c.      Emissions Baselining Study.
Ms. DeSeelhorst reported that funding was received through the Utah Clean Air Partnership
(“UCAIR”). The UCAIR funding would be used to conduct emissions baselining for city operations
in Holladay, Millcreek, and Cottonwood Heights. There would be collaborations with industry
experts, such as Utah Clean Energy, Utah Clean Cities, and Salt Lake Community College Energy
Institute. The study would look at the emission impact of city buildings, city fleets, and city staff
commutes. Emissions would be quantified in terms of carbon dioxide, nitrogen oxides, and
particulate matter. Information from the study would be used to better understand where
sustainability efforts are most needed.

Initial steps for the project would involve working with students from the Salt Lake Community
College Energy Institute. Students would utilize utility data and building systems information to
analyze the emission impact of each City Hall. Staff would be coordinating building tours for a small
group of students to see the facilities firsthand.

III.    Holladay@20 Process Update – Holly Smith.
Ms. Smith reviewed the proposed timeline with the Council. In January 2021 the project began. The
month of February would include share and listen public events, where staff would talk to residents
about the work done over the last two years. Ms. Smith noted that the share and listen events would
have a town hall format. March would include a review of information and feedback from residents.

during share and listen events in April. Ms. Smith commented that those events could be conducted
virtually if needed. The goal was to have a revised proposal in May so that City Manager, Gina
Chamness could prepare a draft budget for the next fiscal year. There would be budget hearings in

town hall meetings. Districts 1 and 2 could have one meeting, with Districts 3 and 4 another. District
5 could hold the third and final meeting. Council Member Petersen wondered if the Town Hall

two general Town Hall meetings rather than dividing the meetings by district. Council Member
Petersen commented that there could be one meeting during the day and one at night. Ms. Smith
explained that each meeting would last for approximately one hour. There would be an introduction,
a short presentation to relay some of the data and findings, and then a question-and-answer session.
The meetings would take place virtually and residents would be invited to share questions and
concerns. Ms. Smith shared a proposed date of Thursday, February 11, 2021, at 12:00 p.m. and 6:00

The project website was overviewed. Ms. Smith reported that the project website was launched
approximately two years ago when the Citizen Advisory Group began its process. The website
featured an introduction as well as links to videos. The website also included outcomes from the
Citizen Advisory Group work such as surveys, a brochure, Property Tax Facts Sheet, and the Citizen
Advisory Group Recommendation Report. There was also a section on the website dedicated to the
stormwater utility fee and the Holladay@20 process. Ms. Smith reported that a folder would be sent
to the Council Members with additional resources. Staff was willing to meet with the Council
Members one-on-one to discuss the information further.

The Citizen Advisory Group felt it was important to create a document that outlined some of the
potential projects that could be funded with a property tax increase. Ms. Smith believed that the
document could be produced within the next few months. She reported that a Frequently Asked

constituents. Council Member Petersen suggested that a list of links be created so that information

   • Emergency Medical Care;

   • Public Health and Safety Threat Management;

   •   Public Health Compliance Action;
          o Personnel costs, public engagement, community concerts, and equipment costs;
   •   Economic Recovery Support Costs;
          o PPE for businesses and Salt Lake Community Action Program.

Ms. Chamness reported that the Salt Lake Community Action Program related to rental assistance. It
was for individuals between 60% and 80% of the Area Median Income (“AMI”). She noted that the
County has a program for individuals earning less than 60% of the AMI but the City of Holladay
tends to have a slightly higher AMI. As a result, the program was targeted toward individuals who
were not directly benefiting from another program.

       b.     Review of Business Survey.
Ms. Smith reported that a second survey was conducted with the business community to better
understand the impacts of the global pandemic. The survey ran from December 18, 2020, to
January 8, 2021, and there were 95 responses. Some of the key findings were:

   •   More businesses applied and received financial assistance through one or more federal, state,
       or county resources;
   •   Many businesses continued to experience reduced operating hours, number of employees, and
       staff hours;
   •   Operating revenues were less when comparing November 2019 and November 2020; and
   •   Approximately half of the respondents continued to have staff that was furloughed or laid off.

Ms. Smith reported that both the initial survey and second survey asked for feedback regarding how
the City could assist businesses. The top answers related to payroll and rent in both July 2020 and
January 2021. She noted that the second survey added additional categories for potential assistance
including developing online sales or websites and enhancing marketing and business promotion. She
also reported that many customers prefer the new offerings, such as touchless payment and contactless
grocery pickup. Many of the solutions offered as a result of the pandemic could continue to be
desirable long-term.

Ms. Chamness reported that all of the CARES Act funds had been spent and any additional spending
would come from the General Fund. She wondered if the Council Members were interested in
programs that focused on marketing and promotion or online support. Ms. Chamness also noted that
it may be beneficial to focus on additional rental assistance programs for those that fell between 60%
and 80% AMI.

Council Member Fotheringham supported direct assistance to residents. However, he expressed
concerns about potential website development programs. He wondered how the program would
work. Ms. Chamness explained that staff discussed a grant program for websites. They would discuss
what the program could look like and bring options to the City Council for further discussion.

and was not a rezone. He discussed the proposed building height and felt that 65 feet was too high.

Council Member Gibbons suggested that there could be different height standards to the north and

Mayor Dahle agreed with the suggestion but wanted to make sure that the heights were appropriate.

approved for the south side, the height allowance for a single-family home would be the same height
as a commercial building in the gateway. Mr. Allred believed that a 40-foot maximum height would
be restrictive. Mayor Dahle wondered whether 45 feet would be more appropriate.

Council Member Gibbons wondered if the Council was in support of a 58-foot maximum height to
the north and a 45-foot maximum height to the south. The Council Members expressed their support.
Council Member Fotheringham asked if vertical expansion would create property tax revenue growth.
Ms. Chamness did not believe so but noted that the topic of verticality raised some interesting
questions.

Council Member Petersen asked about the elevator shaft. She expressed concerns that a 14-foot
elevator shaft would be visible depending on the placement. Council Member Petersen suggested
that the elevator shaft be placed in the middle of the rooftop or be 20 feet away from each wall.
Mr. Allred pointed out that there was wording that the elevator shaft be architecturally compatible.
Council Member Petersen felt the language should be more specific.

Another proposed change related to the section, Setback from Canals/Laterals. Council Member
Gibbons noted that the current language would prevent buildings from being too close to the canal.
It would also create the opportunity for green space, such as a walking path or bicycle path along the
west side of the canal.

Council Member Gibbons pointed out that there were residential areas adjacent to the proposed zone
that were not on the other side of the canal. He discussed side rear setbacks and suggested that the
language state: “Property(ies) in the Holladay Crossroads Zone that directly abuts residentially zoned
property(ies) shall be 30 feet from the residential property line.” Mr. Allred believed the suggestion
made by Council Member Gibbons was reasonable.

Council Member Fotheringham commented on the aesthetics of the area. He noted that it may be
more aesthetically pleasing if there were similar building heights throughout the zone. There was
discussion regarding uniformity. Mr. Allred commented that the greatest height allowance in the
Village Zone was in the core and the height tapers down as it moves toward residential. Council
Member Gibbons believed something similar was being proposed for the Holladay Crossroads Zone.
The taller buildings would be on the corners and the building heights would taper down.

Council Member Gibbons wondered if it would be possible to create a street-level map. He also
shared additional suggestions from the redline document, which included:
