40      Sanitation Self-Provide
41
42   Before addressing sanitation and self-hauling analysis, Mayor Staggs proposed ending curbside
43   recycling and noted the contract with Waste Management requires a six-month notice, meaning
44   the earliest cancellation would be in January. To help frame the discussion, he shared a short
45   video that a resident had sent, offering perspective on the topic.
46
47   Mayor Staggs suggested shifting to a regional recycling model that emphasizes aluminum and
48   paper rather than plastics. Data from Waste Management showed that only about 8% of the
49   City's waste is recycling material, and around 20% of that is contaminated and ends up in the
50   landfill—leaving only roughly 6% actually being recycled. He supports cutting curbside recycling
51   while maintaining budget neutrality and avoiding a rate increase that would be necessary to
     Page | 2 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     May 6, 2025



 1   continue the program.
 2
 3   Councilmember Buroker expressed agreement that it’s difficult to justify continued plastic
 4   recycling and emphasized the importance of a strong communication plan before a final
 5   decision is made. She stressed that eliminating curbside recycling is a politically sensitive issue,
 6   and residents need clear, thorough education to understand the reasoning behind the change.
 7
 8   Councilmember McDougal voiced strong support for eliminating curbside recycling, citing
 9   financial unsustainability and environmental inefficiency. He emphasized that continuing to fund
10   the program from the general fund diverts money from other priorities, and increasing fees for a
11   service that yields little real-world benefit is unjustifiable. He stressed that the landfill captures
12   methane to generate power, framing it as a responsible alternative.
13
14   Councilmember McCay offered a contrasting view, expressing support for keeping the curbside
15   recycling program. She advocated for an opt-in or opt-out model after the current contract
16   expires in 2028, allowing more committed residents to continue recycling while reducing
17   contamination. She noted that 72% of residents had previously expressed support for recycling
18   and cautioned that eliminating it might not actually save residents money if they end up needing
19   additional trash bins.
20
21   Councilmember Haymond emphasized that no matter what decision is made regarding
22   recycling, clear and extensive communication with the public is essential. He acknowledged that
23   for many residents, recycling has become a deeply ingrained belief system, shaped by decades
24   of messaging that frames it as critical to saving the planet. Because of this, any change could
25   feel deeply personal or even offensive to some. He reiterated that the success of any policy
26   change depends on how well it is communicated, calling for collaboration with the City’s
27   Communication Department to ensure residents are fully informed about the realities and
28   financial implications of maintaining the curbside recycling service.
29
30   Councilmember Pierucci voiced concern that under the current proposal, residents would
31   effectively lose a service (recycling pickup) but continue to pay the same rate. He characterized
32   this as a “hidden fee increase” and recommended exploring the financial impact of reducing the
33   monthly fee by a few dollars if recycling is eliminated, suggesting that the public might be more
34   receptive to the change if it’s paired with a cost reduction.
35
36   Kevin Hicks, City Manager, responded to the suggestion of lowering the monthly fee once
37   recycling is discontinued. He clarified that the Mayor’s proposed budget already avoids a fee
38   increase by subsidizing the sanitation fund with approximately $300,000 from the General Fund.
39   Because the City must give six months' notice before halting recycling, both trash and recycling
40   services would continue during that time, which limits the ability to immediately reduce rates.
41   He noted that if the Council chose to lower the monthly fee after recycling ends, it would likely
42   only last for about six months before needing to raise it again due to inflation and other cost
43   pressures beginning the following July.
44
45   Councilmember Pierucci asked how long eliminating curbside recycling would delay the need
46   for another sanitation fee increase. Mr. Hicks responded that, while they hadn’t done a detailed
47   financial projection, their rough estimate was that it would postpone further increases by about
48   two years. After that, additional increases would likely be needed due to inflation and rising
49   contract costs. Mr. Hicks also clarified that the Mayor's current budget proposal includes a
50   $2.50 monthly fee increase, with smaller cost-of-living adjustments expected in subsequent
51   years. Removing recycling would avoid that immediate increase and allow the City to delay
     Page | 3 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     May 6, 2025



 1   future ones temporarily.
 2
 3   Mr. Hicks explained that the Mayor’s budget includes a regional recycling option, which would
 4   place recycling containers at each of the City’s 33 parks. The cost estimate for this program is
 5   $50,000 for half a year, based on feedback from a hauler, though the actual cost would need to
 6   be confirmed through a bidding process.
 7
 8   Mr. Hicks reported that diversion rate for recycling in early 2025 has increased to 10%, up from
 9   8% in 2024, indicating that more residents are participating in recycling, even with the audits in
10   place. The City’s glass recycling program, which incurs no additional cost for the City, is based
11   on two pickups at designated locations. However, expanding this service would likely result in
12   extra charges.
13
14   Per Councilmember Buroker’s request, staff agreed to prepare a detailed communication plan
15   regarding recycling by the next Council meeting.
16
17   Mr. Hicks explained that, at a high level, he had compared the City’s sanitation costs to a
18   neighboring city that handles its own waste hauling and found their projected expenses to be
19   very similar—slightly lower, in fact. However, the analysis showed that the City still could not
20   afford to self-haul under current revenue levels and would need to raise fees for residents. The
21   pro forma included roughly $5.5 million in startup costs for trucks and facility upgrades, such as
22   a truck bay and wash station. He confirmed the $5.5 million startup cost includes financing
23   estimates for trucks and facility improvements, with vehicle debt structured based on the useful
24   life of each truck (3–7 years) and the bond for building improvements estimated at 20 years with
25   a $240,000 annual payment. The pro forma did not include rate increases in FY26 or FY27,
26   focusing instead on expense projections. He also noted that while no trucks would be replaced
27   in year one, best practices would suggest replacing one truck annually starting in year three, at
28   a cost of around $385,000–$400,000 each. A decision is needed within the next six months to
29   start procurement, as truck lead times are close to two years.

21            Ordinance No. 25-14 – Amending Riverton City Code Section 18.135.200,
22            Temporary Commercial Uses, Addressing Temporary Commercial Uses on
23            Residential Property, Amendments Proposed by Riverton City
24
25   Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, explained that this amendment would allow
26   temporary commercial uses on vacant, undeveloped residential properties that have direct
27   access to major roadways, as defined in City code (e.g., Redwood Road, 12600 South). Based
28   on prior Council feedback, the updated draft restricts access for such uses to major roads only,
29   unless existing conditions prohibit it, in which case limited exceptions apply. The amendment
30   also includes references to the City’s temporary commercial lighting standards. The Planning
31   Commission recommended approval, and staff likewise recommended adoption with the
32   incorporated changes.
33
34   Councilmember Haymond asked whether the proposed ordinance included protections for
35   nearby primary residences and whether temporary commercial uses must be fully self-
36   contained. Mr. Lethbridge confirmed that yes, parking and access must be fully contained on the
37   temporary use property, with no spillover onto side streets. Councilmember Haymond also
38   raised concerns about fireworks stands and large-scale storage of explosives. Mr. Lethbridge
39   responded that while the City code does not specifically regulate fireworks storage, any
40   fireworks operation is subject to permitting and inspection by Unified Fire Authority (UFA), which
41   enforces applicable fire safety codes.
42
43   Mayor Staggs called for public comments;
44
45   Jay Salter spoke in opposition to a potential firework stand on vacant lots located off Christian
46   Way near Redwood Road, citing several concerns. He noted the area’s existing traffic
47   challenges, including limited egress options that would likely become further congested with the
48   addition of temporary commercial activity. He described the general state of the property—citing
49   makeshift fencing and poor upkeep—as a burden on surrounding neighbors, and asked the
50   Council to carefully consider whether allowing a commercial use at this location would be
51   appropriate or beneficial to the community.

11            Ordinance No. 25-15 – DAI Development Agreement, Amending Zoning from R-3
12            to C-R Uses limited to Storage Units, and Preliminary Site Plan Approval for
13            Approx. 5.63 acres Located at 12270 South 4000 West. Applicant-Krisel Travis
14            and Joe Salisbury representing DAI Utah
15
16   Mr. Lethbridge explained that the proposed development, located off 4000 West and north of
17   12600 South, is a storage facility with outdoor vehicle storage, replacing an undeveloped,
18   platted residential subdivision from 2006. The property, which has remained dormant due to
19   legal challenges and the recession, is now being considered for development. The applicant's
20   proposal includes two existing homes that will remain and one lot to be retained for future
21   development. The zoning would be changed to commercial regional (CR) but limited to the
22   specified storage facility uses. Architectural standards and building height limitations are set
23   within the development agreement, and improvements such as curb, gutter, and sidewalk would
24   be made along the frontage.
25
26   Mr. Lethbridge said that a traffic study confirmed that the proposed development would not
27   generate more traffic than a residential development, and no changes to the road's right of way
28   are required. The applicant's proposal includes fencing and architectural elements that aim to
29   blend with the surrounding area, including residential properties. The Planning Commission has
30   reviewed the project in multiple meetings, ensuring it aligns with the community's needs while
31   providing commercial benefits. The development agreement includes several conditions to
32   ensure the project adheres to the outlined standards and fits appropriately within the
33   neighborhood.
34
35   Mr. Lethbridge noted that the proposed development includes several exceptions to City code,
36   including the removal of a required landscape buffer along the northeast property line, due to
37   the mature landscaping at the adjacent LDS church and the applicant’s commitment to an eight-
38   foot fence. The setback for two buildings on 4000 West is reduced from 40 feet to 36 feet, with
39   support from the property owner and the Planning Commission, as the buildings will remain
40   single-story. Additionally, a unique signage tower is proposed, replacing the standard pole sign,
41   as the current sign code doesn’t account for this design. These exceptions were reviewed and
42   approved by the Planning Commission, which recommended the development with a condition
43   to clarify building heights, now addressed in the agreement.
44
45   Councilmember McDougal expressed support for the development, appreciating the design and
46   acknowledging the circumstances that led to this solution rather than the originally planned
47   third-acre lots from 2008. He noted that the two-story buildings would not exceed the height of
48   two-story homes and praised the aesthetic value of the proposed signage. However, he raised
49   concerns about the need for a zoning change in the development agreement, fearing that, in 15
50   years, the zoning could revert and allow for other uses. He recommended removing the zoning
51   change and approving the project solely for self-storage to ensure it remains dedicated to that
