1.   Discussion, Landscaping Requirements/Waterwise Landscaping

     Mr. Montgomery stated while Utah has experienced drought cycles in the past, the time between these
     cycles has reduced over time, and due to this, the amount of water available in the reservoirs is becoming
     so greatly diminished that there is concern. Efforts need to be made to improve water usage, particularly
     due to the increasing population of the area. While there are many water users, the ability to reduce
     water usage for landscaping is both the most visible and most controllable. He reviewed water usage
     data, stating while residential use appears to be the highest, due to the number of residents, the amount
     per user is the least used. He stated the mindset of the population needs to change in order to create
     better water usage, that each person needs to accept responsibility for the future. He stated while Ogden
     City has abundant water shares, has created new storage tanks and replaced older pipes and meters,
     much water still is lost due to the age of water lines because of leakage. While many water providers
     have restricted usage for outdoor landscaping, efforts also should be made to limit the amount of turf
     grass in favor of more waterwise plantings. In addition, recent legislation by the State limits city abilities
     to require sod as landscaping, requires a water plan element to be added to the General Plan by 2025
     hoping to reach a goal of 175 gallons of water per capita by 2065. He reviewed existing ordinance
     regulations for Ogden City and stated landscaping is defined as living plant material, but also includes
     patio areas, water features, natural areas and allows up to 10% to be landscape rock, mulch, or artificial
     turf. Parkstrip areas require trees, which may be combined with grass, ground cover, rock, or low impact
     landscaping.

     Because of the limited amount of space in a parkstrip, these areas often contribute to overwatering as
     much of the water goes either on the sidewalk or the street. While grass is cheaper to install and easier
     to maintain, other treatments are better for these small areas. These areas also often are difficult to
     maintain as they have hard surface on both sides which create a heat island. Weber Basin Water has
     initiated a program to encourage water reduction with an incentive to eliminate the turf park strip, and
     the City Council has committed to also create an additional incentive. These are both dependent on
     ordinances being adopted to clarify regulations for waterwise landscaping. In considering such
     amendments, Staff has identified some points of discussion for the Commission to consider as follows:
          • Remove turf grass as an allowed material in the park strip or allow only when the width of the

         Discussion centered on different types of grasses and the ability some may have to spread across
         the sidewalk while other varieties of grasses remain in one clump. Commission discussed the role
         of park strips in separating pedestrian and vehicular traffic as well as providing space for snow to
         be piled. It also is a place where utilities can be accommodated on private property. Commission
         felt it is important to spell out what a park strip should look like and that citizens become educated
         in alternative watering methods. It was noted there are many areas where secondary water is not
         available and citizens are required to water with a hose, which is a concern if a drip system is to be
         required. There are currently no regulations which require single-family homes to have an
         underground sprinkling system or a drip irrigation system.

         Mr. Montgomery discussed the merits of landscaping, stating it provides oxygen exchange, collects
         air particulates and control dust while also reducing heat. Rocks can reduce maintenance for a time
         period, but weeds still grow amongst them, and their removal is difficult, and often increase the
         requirement for maintenance over time. The mix of rock and trees works only because the tree
         provides shade and reduces heat of the ground surface, but care needs to be taken to assure trees
         continue to grow as they do need water.

         He discussed the proposed incentives, stating citizens could obtain up to $4.25 per square foot of
         sod removed to be replaced with waterwise materials, but certain standards are included in the
         program. These including attending education classes, photo documenting before and after
         installation, providing adequate watering, etc.

         Mr. Montgomery then asked whether turf grass should be limited in all situations, whether it should
         be required to be reduced when building additions occur. He showed photos of both residential and
         commercial/industrial uses where front turf was limited, but other landscaping methods used.
         While most homeowners want a play area for children, it could be limited to the rear yard. He
         suggested turf might be limited to 35% of the yard area, that control valves be required to check for
         leakage, and citizens be better educated in terms of maintenance of these areas. He also suggested
         other ordinances be revised to eliminate bonus density points for waterwise landscaping and types
         of open spaces for multi-family identify dimensional requirements.

2.   Discussion, East Central Community Plan Update

     Mr. Brierley stated he has been working with a steering committee relating to the East Central
     Community Plan, followed by a recent open house where citizens of the entire community were invited.
     He also conducted some drop and stop visits with commuters along 25th Street and had posed survey
     questions. The focus of this plan update was threefold – to accommodate the BRT line, to include the
     standards identified in the Nine-Rails Creative District Plan, and to determine whether accessory
     dwelling units are an appropriate use in the single-family zoned areas within the community.

     The BRT line will benefit the community by providing cleaner air, lowering congestion, accommodate
     higher density housing, better health, providing options for commercial activity, provide travel choices
     and transit ridership. Comments he received relating to mass transit included providing secure bicycle
     storage, wayfinding signs and accommodating wheelchairs with sidewalk space. Respondents also


                                                      [2]
   favor small cottage lots within walking distance of transit stops as well as live/work units and office
   uses. They also supported street vendors at 25th & Monroe and seating areas along the sidewalks.

   The Nine-Rails Plan is an effort to establish an epicenter for art and culture where the creative
   community lives, cerates, shares and teaches for the benefit of Ogden artists, citizens and visitors.
   The plan identifies an area of intense creative district, which is mostly within the CBD zone, but also
   seeks to allow alternative land uses, provide artistic crosswalks, traffic calming, and allowing artisans
   to create, live and sell items within their neighborhood. Staff is suggesting the area identified as the
   creative district be rezoned to R-9 and be allowed to accommodate uses such as art studios, live-work
   units, photo studio, indoor studio for teaching of fine arts, art fabrication, restaurants without drive-thru
   windows, and theaters. The area also could accommodate small cottage single-family lots with a
   1,500 square foot lot to accommodate a home between 600-1200 square feet and a single parking
   space with reduced setbacks. Public art could also be required as an incentive requirement as new
   development occurs. Citizens surveyed felt public art should relate a story of the particular community
   or history of the area.

   Citizens also favored allowing accessory dwelling units to the single-family zoned areas as household
   types has changed over time. He showed various buildings where such a use could be easily
   accommodated, either in the main structure or in another building. He stated current regulations in the
   East Central community limit the height and roof pitch of accessory buildings, and many are now
   unable to accommodate such a unit above garage space due to these regulations. He is suggesting
   the height be limited to 25’, and that the roof pitch be reduced to a percentage which would allow more
   garages to become accessory dwelling units.

   Commission supported these findings and felt the proposed plan can be prepared for the next regular
   meeting.

There being no additional business before the Commission, the meeting was adjourned at 8:20pm.
