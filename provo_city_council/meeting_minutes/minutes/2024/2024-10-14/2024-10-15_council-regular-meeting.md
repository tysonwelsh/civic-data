Public Comment 0:16:47

Vice-Chair Whipple read the public comment preamble and opened the public comment period.

Andrew Thompson, of Provo, addressed the City Council to reiterate his previous request for an ADA
liaison, emphasizing the critical need for dedicated support in addressing accessibility issues within
Provo. He proposed that City Council members and administration staff engage in "empathy training" by
experiencing the city from a wheelchair, which he believes would enhance their understanding of the
challenges faced by individuals with disabilities. Thompson stressed the importance of having a
dedicated ADA coordinator to tackle various accessibility concerns and suggested that neighboring cities
could benefit from similar efforts. He recommended looking into the effective accessibility standards of
Lexington, Massachusetts, as a model for improvement. Additionally, he expressed concern over the
city’s allocation of $73,000 for certain art installations, questioning whether such funds might be better
utilized to enhance accessibility in Provo. Overall, his comments called for proactive measures to
improve accessibility and encouraged city officials to better understand the experiences of individuals
with disabilities.

Alyssa Hughes, a veterinarian from Summit County, spoke in support of the Best Friends initiative to
implement a Trap-Neuter-Return (TNR) program for free-roaming cats in Provo. She highlighted the
importance of TNR as a humane alternative to euthanasia, which many mistakenly believe resolves
issues with stray cat populations. Hughes explained that removing and euthanizing cats can destabilize
colonies, leading to an increase in cat reproduction, fighting, and the spread of diseases. She said in
contrast, the TNR program involves trapping, vaccinating, sterilizing, and then returning the cats to their

Carla Tanner, of Provo, addressed the City Council to advocate for the Best Friends community cat
programming. She highlighted that Utah County currently has the highest euthanasia rate in the state,
largely due to outdated and inhumane practices of trapping, impounding, and euthanizing healthy
outdoor cats. Tanner emphasized that the Trap-Neuter-Vaccinate-Return (TNVR) method is a proven,
safe, and cost-effective way to gradually reduce the population of free-roaming cats over time. She
referenced Governor Cox’s recent proclamation declaring 2024 as the year for no-kill shelters in Utah
and urged the council to implement a community cat program in Provo, setting a positive example for
the rest of the county. On a personal note, Tanner shared her experience of rescuing two outdoor cats,
which she successfully socialized and brought into her home, emphasizing the importance of giving such
animals a chance at life. Ms. Tanner concluded by encouraging the council to sincerely consider the offer
and to trial the program in Provo for three years.

Cecelia Burnside, of Park City and feline veterinarian, spoke in support of the Trap-Neuter-Return (TNR)
program, drawing from her experience with a successful program in Park City. She noted that TNR is a
well-established practice in veterinary medicine and is taught in many vet schools, helping to effectively
decrease cat populations in colonies. She highlighted the emotional toll on shelter staff and animal
control officers who must euthanize healthy cats, emphasizing the importance of compassion in
addressing the issue of free-roaming cats. She argued that community cats should not be viewed as
nuisances but as part of the community that deserves care and support. By implementing TNR, she
believes the mental burden on shelter workers would be alleviated, as it promotes a more humane
approach to managing cat populations. She also emphasized the role of colony caregivers who care for
these cats, noting that vaccinated and sterilized cats will help stabilize populations over time, ultimately
leading to smaller, healthier colonies. Burnside concluded by stating that taking responsibility for these
animals is essential and that communities must take action to care for them appropriately.

Autumn Wagner, of Salt Lake City and representing Best Friends, shared her insights on the benefits of
implementing the Trap-Neuter-Return (TNR) program in shelters. She described her experiences working
with shelter staff and animal control officers who have transitioned from the difficult days of
euthanizing cats to being part of a more humane solution. Many of these workers expressed relief and
gratitude for no longer having to resort to euthanasia. Ms. Wagner highlighted several advantages of the
TNR program, including the ability to bypass the standard five-day holding period for cats, allowing them
to be fixed and returned to their outdoor homes where they are often cared for by community
members. This approach not only stabilizes cat populations but also allows shelter staff to focus on
adoptable animals, including dogs, enhancing overall care and attention in the shelter environment. She
noted that the return-to-owner rate for cats is significantly lower than that of dogs, often in the single
digits, which underscores the need for a different perspective on community cats. Additionally, Wagner
mentioned that TNR can lead to unintentional benefits, such as helping lost indoor/outdoor cats find
their way home. She concluded by emphasizing the positive impact that TNR has on both the
community and the animals involved.

1.         An ordinance amending the zone map classification of real property, generally located at
           1992 North Geneva Road, from the R1.20 (One Family Residential) zone to the LDR (Low
           Density Residential) zone. Lakeview North Neighborhood. (PLRZ20240252) 0:33:22

     Motion:       An implied motion to approve Ordinance 2024-52, as currently constituted, has been
                   made by council rule.

Aaron Ardmore, Planning Supervisor, provided an update regarding city-owned property at 2000 North
and Geneva Road, which has been vacant since the home on the site was demolished in 2016. The city
acquired the property in 2015 and is now leveraging it to address goals outlined in the General Plan,
specifically focusing on workforce and attainable housing. He stated the city has collaborated with Provo
Housing to create a land trust, which would retain ownership of the land while constructing six
townhomes under the Low-Density Residential (LDR) zoning. These homes would then be sold to city
employees or other qualified candidates who meet the necessary income criteria. Ardmore emphasized
that this initiative is part of a broader effort to tackle the ongoing affordability crisis, which has made it
difficult for many city employees to reside in Provo. He expressed optimism about this project and its
potential to pave the way for similar developments in the future.

Councilor Hoban asked how a developer is selected for this. He also mentioned some questions that
were brought up during the work session including how it will be determined who gets to purchase
these homes.

Mr. Ardmore explained that once the property is zoned, Provo Housing will issue a Request for
Proposals (RFP) to select a contractor. He noted that the homes will likely be distributed through a
lottery system but deferred to the Housing Authority for confirmation. He highlighted the strong
demand for these homes, as inquiries about their availability have already begun.

Councilor Bogdin asked why the item was not presented in a neighborhood meeting prior to council.

Mr. Ardmore explained that due to scheduling conflicts, the timing for a neighborhood meeting did not
align. However, since a neighborhood meeting is not required for this matter, it was forwarded directly
from the Planning Commission to the City Council. He added that it is on the next neighborhood meeting
agenda.

Councilor Bogdin asked if there is concern regarding the traffic impact that these homes will have. She
asked Mr. Ardmore to explain where the access points would be.

Mr. Ardmore explained that access to the property will be located on the east side, feeding onto 2000
North. The six single-family attached homes will generate minimal daily traffic, which will not
significantly impact the area. The east-side access was designed to provide ingress and egress to the
garages at the rear of the townhomes, ensuring appropriate vehicle stacking.

Vice-Chair Whipple opened the public comment period.

Don Barr, a longtime Provo resident living near the proposed development, shared his perspective on
the project. He recalled the property's history and the families who previously lived there. While
expressing gratitude for being informed about the development, he noted his preference for four homes
on the site instead of six, citing concerns about scale and traffic. Barr acknowledged that six homes
might be feasible from a logistical standpoint but felt it might overcrowd the area. He emphasized the
increasing traffic on 2000 North as his primary concern and expressed interest in participating in a
neighborhood meeting about the project.

Vonda Barr, a longtime resident living west of the proposed development on Geneva Road, shared her
concerns about safety in the area. Reflecting on the increased activity and traffic over the past 50 years,
she noted that Geneva Road has become consistently busy, and traffic is expected to worsen with the
new intersection and the development of Airport Road. Her primary concern is the safety of future
residents, particularly families with children, as well as those traveling in the area. She expressed doubts
about whether the location is a safe place to live.

With no other public comments, Vice-Chair Whipple closed public comment and invited a council
discussion.

Councilor Bogdin discussed concerns about fencing and safety for the proposed development. She
referenced comments from the Planning Commission, specifically Barbara De Soto's suggestion to fence
off the front part of the property for safety.

Mr. Ardmore confirmed that these details, including fencing and guest parking signage, will be
addressed during the Project Plan application phase.

3.         A resolution approving a wetlands construction and maintenance agreement with GT
           medical (NOORDA Osteopathic School of Medicine). (24-083) 0:46:25

Keith Morey, Economic & Redevelopment Director, presented. He provided an update regarding an
agreement with the NOORDA Medical School related to wetland mitigation near the golf course. He
explained that during the school’s development process, another potential buildable property was
identified, prompting a need to revisit the existing agreement. The updated arrangement outlines how
the medical school can use between two and five acres of city-owned property for wetland mitigation,
with a fee of approximately $136,000 per acre to compensate for the impact. The city will retain
ownership of the property. Mr. Morey emphasized the mutual benefits of the partnership, noting that
the revenue generated could fund improvements to the golf course, including enhancements to the
driving range and the addition of new entertainment options. He described the arrangement as
straightforward and a win-win opportunity for both the city and the medical school.

Board Member Bogdin asked if the driving range will be open as part of the triple play or if it will be a
separate fee.

Mr. Morey explained that the driving range is a revenue generator for the golf course and not a free-use
facility. He mentioned that the planned enhancements could include interactive games and activities
similar to those at Topgolf, though on a smaller scale. These additions could create a more engaging
experience, appealing to a broader audience beyond just golfers, and generating additional revenue for
the course.

Brett Watson, Golf Manager, clarified that there are no plans to raise fees for the driving range, and it
will remain included in the triple play package. He explained that the goal is to improve the existing
driving range by enhancing the hitting area and making the landing area more appealing. These
upgrades aim to strengthen the driving range, which is currently considered the weakest part of the
facility.

Chair Whipple asked if we had already received the Army Corps of Engineers approval for the proposed

Mr. Morey explained that the medical school must still go through approval processes, including
submitting plans to the Army Corps of Engineers. However, they could not proceed fully until the city

determined once the Army Corps of Engineers approves the mitigation plan.

           affordable housing projects (24-081) 0:54:05

Mr. Morey clarified that the appropriation of these funds is a formal step. While the intended use of the
funds has already been discussed, this action is necessary to officially allocate them. The funds were

within their project area as required. Instead of building the affordable housing, they opted to pay the
city the equivalent amount. These funds have been held in the general fund, but now need to be
