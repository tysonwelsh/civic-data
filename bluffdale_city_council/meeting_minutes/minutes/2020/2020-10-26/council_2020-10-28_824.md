Mr. Deeter gave a broad overview of what impact fees are, what has changed in transportation
since the fees were adopted in February, and showed a comparison of the impact fees for three
selected samples consisting of a single-family dwelling unit, 10,000 square-foot retail, and a
55,000 square-foot warehouse. The intent was to see how it compares to the impact fee adopted
in February, what it will be now, and how that compares to neighboring cities.

Administrative Services Director, Bruce Kartchner noted that the Council previously discussed the
impact fees on an individual fee level, but there has not been a presentation that detailed those as
far as the gross impact fees for these different types of projects. For example, the Transportation
Impact Fee was particularly salient compared to other base impact fees. The presentation showed
the comparison in calculations as to what a project would look like and the charges and expected
equivalent charges in other cities.

Mr. Deeter reported that one of the services provided by the City is infrastructure, which includes
roads, parks and trails, water, and police and fire services. Monies are required to provide these
tangible assets as goods and services to the community. Infrastructure is paid through these fees.
The State Legislature followed the direction of the Utah Supreme Court that held that it is feasible
and fair that a citizen is charged for their proportionate share of current infrastructure, or that will
have to be paid in the future. The impact fee is a method for determining the financial impact of
adding to the infrastructure. Fees can be based on current capacity in the system as long as it is
proportional and offers the same level of service provided to all constituents. Impact fees are not
required, but it is a fair and equitable way to fund new growth.

In response to an inquiry, Mr. Deeter suggested that 90% of Utah’s population reside in a city
where there is an impact fee. Additionally, he estimated that it is more common than not to charge
the maximum impact fee. There is a balance that must be struck between city departments that
want to collect the maximum fee and departments that view impact fees as a deterrent to
development. Many cities request the maximum impact fee and then reduce it to show their
constituents that they are erring on the side of caution and trying to be reasonable and not
overcharging for the infrastructure.

It was noted that the biggest issue that the City has with developers is their examples of cities that
do not charge the maximum impact fee. Mr. Deeter pointed out that a significant portion of a
city’s tax revenue comes from sales tax, so if a city cannot attract a retail establishment it directly
impacts tax revenue.

Mr. Deeter noted that every infrastructure system is different and while it is informative as to what
other cities are charging, it should only be one information point. Each city must determine how
they are to pay for their systems, taking into account the costs that they have, regardless of what
neighboring cities are doing.

Mr. Hales discussed the modification made to Transportation Impact Fees and described the
change in methodology. Mr. Kartchner explained that they consistently and more frequently
evaluating impact fees and trying to refine how the calculations are done particularly now since

Mr. Fazio reported that the above item involves the Impact Fee Facilities Plan (“IFFP”) that was
tabled one month earlier. Staff was ready to continue the discussion. Cody Deeter from EFG
Consulting presented the proposed changes and explained that an impact fee collects funds as a
condition of development to offset the proportionate cost to infrastructure. Those funds must be
set aside in a specific account and cannot be used for any other purpose. The impact fees are
reported to the state in a report each year. Mayor Timothy noted that impact fees are collected at
the time a building permit is issued and is a one-time cost.

Mr. Deeter reported that a city is not required to have an impact fee but it is allowed in the State
Statute as part of the Impact Fee Act. A request was made to amend the current IFFP based on
additional information. For water, the construction of a storage tank on the east side was
postponed. That storage was still expected to be constructed in the future but does not fit into the
six to 10-year planning window. For transportation, the cost of the 14600 South Project was
reduced because the City received funds from other sources to reach completion. For Public
Safety, a ladder truck was replaced with a fire engine. Mr. Deeter presented charts and graphs with
impact fee comparisons from neighboring cities. He reported that there is a 90-day implementation
window. If the IFFP amendments are adopted, it would take 90 days for the impact fees to be
utilized.

Mayor Timothy opened the public hearing. There were no public comments. The public hearing
was closed.

Dave Kallas moved to approve Ordinance 2020-23, adopting an amended Impact Fee Facility
Plan and Impact Fee Analysis. Wendy Aston seconded the motion. Vote on motion: Wendy
Aston-Aye; Jeff Gaston-Aye; Traci Crockett-Aye; Dave Kallas-Aye; Mark Hales-Aye. The
