Councilmember Harmon offered to read a Proclamation Declaring August as
"Neighborhood Nights" Month and August 5, 2025 as "National Night Out 2025" in West
Valley City.

Eric Bunderson, City Attorney, presented a consent agreement for the Council's consideration
and vote, noting that the matter was time-sensitive due to the timing of the transaction. He
provided background information, stating that in 2009, the City received a 40% minority
interest in the Grizzlies Hockey Club, LLC, in exchange for forgiving debt owed by the team.
Since that time, the Grizzlies had restructured their organization into two LLCs—one holding
the franchise (in which the City retained its interest) and another holding various assets. The
Grizzlies intend to sell the team and have identified a purchaser. As part of the ownership
structure, the City’s consent is required to allow the majority owner to proceed with the sale,
which includes both franchise and asset transfers—some of which the City was not directly
involved in. Eric explained that the resolution before the Council, acting as the Board of West
Valley City, would authorize the majority owner to take all necessary actions to complete the
sale. He shared a statement from the Grizzlies indicating their excitement about the acquisition
by Pro Hockey Partners, LLC, led by Bob Orabel, a seasoned executive in professional hockey.
However, the final location of the team had not been disclosed, and legal counsel requested it
remain confidential until the sale was finalized. He noted that while the Council technically
had a choice, withholding approval would result in the City owning a portion of a team no
longer based in West Valley City. He emphasized the pragmatic nature of the decision, as the
City would receive a portion of the sale proceeds—40%—reflecting its original investment in
2009.

Councilmember Christensen asked what the difference was in space between the

rather than spread across multiple levels, would improve operational efficiency. He

Councilmember Harmon recalled that a rezone had been done in the area about a
year ago to make the zoning more uniform and asked for a reminder or clarification
on that action. Steve explained that prior to the rezone, approximately two-thirds of
the Lake Park area had been zoned for manufacturing. This zoning dated back to
the 1990s when Zion Securities was planning an office park and determined that
such uses were allowed under the manufacturing zone, so they retained it. Although
the area was governed by private CC&Rs (Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions)
that limited what could be built—outside of the City’s regulatory control—they still
influenced development. Over time, ownership in the area began to consolidate,
with single entities acquiring multiple properties. This raised concerns that a
controlling interest in the CC&Rs could eventually lead to more industrial uses
being introduced—uses the City felt were not appropriate for the area. As a result,
staff recommended changing the zoning to Business Research Park, which was
more restrictive than the previous manufacturing designation. Steve further
explained that the C-2 zone, now under consideration, had a mixed set of
restrictions. While it allowed a broader range of retail uses not permitted in the
Business Research Park zone, it also prohibited some light industrial uses that the
research park zoning would allow.

Councilmember Harmon asked if the mental health hospital would be a 24/7
location. Steve confirmed that the facility primarily operated as an inpatient
treatment center where people stayed overnight, though it also offered outpatient
programs. He noted that the facility served a variety of patients, including active-
duty military members who traveled in for specialized programs addressing trauma
and veterans’ needs. Other services included acute stabilization for adults, detox,
and rehabilitation. Steve added that approximately 90% of the patients were
voluntary, with only about 10% required to be there involuntarily.

            The applicant owns several office buildings along Decker Lake Drive

            proposing this change to provide more tenant options for his office

       buildings. Included with this report is supporting information from the
       applicant. For reference, the office market data from CBRE shows an even
       greater office vacancy rate for the West Valley/Lake Park submarket than
       what the applicant is suggesting.

       Prior to the comprehensive update to the Zoning Ordinance that occurred in
       March of 2017, medical and dental offices were allowed in the M zone and
       commercial zones. At the time, staff recommended that medical and dental
       offices be prohibited in the M zone for at least two reasons. First,
       commercial zones tend to be located next to residential zones where medical
       and dental office patrons come from while manufacturing zones tend to be
       located further from residential zones. Second, at the time, staff was
       concerned about the State forcing a permanent homeless shelter in an
       industrial location within the City, which would have significantly impacted
       our police and fire calls for service. While medical and dental offices aren’t
       homeless shelters, shelters do at times have nearby medical services tailored
       to transient populations. Hence, the City was trying to make an industrial
       location less desirable for a homeless shelter in that support services could
       not be located nearby.

       The applicant’s proposed requirement that medical and dental offices in the
       M zone be located within 500 feet of a residential use or zone addresses
       staff’s first reason to prohibit this use in the M zone because medical and
       dental office patrons would be close to new medical and dental offices
       within the M zone. On the second reason, the City is still concerned about
       having a permanent homeless shelter forced on the City. However, in staff’s
       view, the proposed ordinance change is unlikely to increase the chances of
       having a permanent homeless shelter in the City because there are so many
       factors in play besides the proximity of supporting medical services.

Mayor Lang asked whether, instead of applying the regulation citywide for one

A.  ACE COURT UPDATE
    Tumi Young, Chief Code Enforcement Officer, presented a PowerPoint
    Presentation summarized as follows:

             -   History and Development
                    o Mid-1990s: Switched from criminal prosecution to
                        administrative court for code violations
                    o Initially run by a paralegal
                    o 1999: Attorney Ryan Robinson hired to lead
                    o Program later led by subsequent city attorneys
                    o Around 2020: Returned to non-lawyer administration.
             -   Key Features of ACE Program
                    o Cases heard within weeks (vs. ~6 months in criminal court)
                    o Daily fines until property is in compliance
                    o Cases heard by city-appointed Administrative Law Judge (ALJ)
                    o Less formal process – more relatable to property owners
                    o ALJ encourages compliance plans, not just fines
                    o No jury trial or public defender rights
                    o Non-compliant properties listed with county recorder
                    o Hinders sale of property/refinance until compliance is met
             -   Process After Notice of Violation
                    o Once the compliance period expires, the case is forwarded to

     Councilmember Harmon asked how a property owner would find themselves
     before the Ace Court. Tumi explained that first, a courtesy notice was issued, giving
     the recipient a minimum of two weeks to come into compliance. If compliance was
     not achieved, a formal notice of violation was then issued, providing an additional
     minimum of two weeks to comply before the matter was referred to court. In total,
     this allowed at least four weeks to resolve the issue before it was sent to an
     Administrative Law Judge.
