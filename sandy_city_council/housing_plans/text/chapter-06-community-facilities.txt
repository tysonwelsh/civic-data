Introduction

Community facilities are a vital part of a city¶s quality of life.  It is that part which makes the
houses and streets come alive with activity and social gatherings, as well as meeting the basic
health, education, welfare and safety of residents.  Community facilities include public buildings
and lands for local, state and federal government and those owned by private enterprises for the
benefit of the community.  They include:

       1.        a variety of buildings to house administrative, educational, religious, cultural, 
                 health, police and fire services, public works;

       2.        public lands to accommodate the buildings and public works facilities and to 
                 provide playgrounds, landscaping and beautification.


Community facilities serve as the prime focus of activity and give residents the opportunity to
meet and get to know each other.  In this way, residents feel more a part of their community, and
pass on the basics of the city¶s history and culture.  It is this common cultural and political
purpose, not size or population, which defines a city.

Each city is unique, and its individual characteristics determine the city¶s special needs for
community facilities.  As the population grows, as the urban area expands, as old facilities prove
inadequate, and as living standards and expectations rise, the demand for more and more varied
community services and facilities increases.

While the demand for traditional services such as water and sewer is always evident, demands for
other services such as health, community schools and junior colleges increase.  What may have
been seen as a luxury a few years ago, now may be regarded as a necessity.  As a result, the
demand for various facilities shifts with time as the needs and desires of the residents change.

The general plan of a city analyzes the location, character and adequacy of facilities and the
extent to which the community facilities will meet the community¶s goals and objectives.  Basic
data includes:

       1.        an inventory of present services and facilities;

       2.        an estimate of the community¶s future population and its distribution;

       3.        a review to determine the extent to which the present level of services relates to

                community goals;

        4.      an evaluation of the level of services the community desires as expressed in the 
                citizens¶ reports for future services; and

        5.      the development of local standards and policies for community facilities.


Some many types of facilities involve permanent structures of considerable cost, their design
should consider not only present needs, but also future needs, possible changes in standards, and
the need for flexibility.

For this study, estimates of space needs for community facilities were based on the judgment of
the officials who will be utilizing them and were broadly measured against national standards. 
 The standards uses are described in each section.   Estimations for space requirements mostly
depend on:  1  the agency¶s services, and  2  whether these services need to be centralized or
decentralized into sub-areas.  For example, it would be better to centralize payment for all utility
bills, secure permits and licenses, etc. at one location  e.g., city hall .  However, fire stations and
health facilities may best serve residents if developed in various geographic areas of the city.

                                            CITY HALL

A city hall represents the major civic center of a city.  The building itself reflects the cultural
values of its people and represents all of the people living within the city boundaries.  Its primary
purpose is to provide for a convenient, economical supply of public services.

In addition, a city hall should enhance the appearance of the community by means of good
aesthetic design.  Variables used to enhance the appearance of the area in addition to satisfying
the functional needs are:  1  landscaping to make the development aesthetically pleasing and
blend in with the natural features surrounding it, and  2  a place and/or fountain and/or
amphitheater to provide an opportunity for people to meet and talk, and to support cultural
events.

Grouping public buildings, art centers, libraries, police, fire and parks in a planned civic center
complex can result in a number of advantages:

       1.      greater visibility and focus emphasizing them as a symbol of city government,

       2.      more opportunity for coordination of aesthetic design

       3.      more efficient coordination among public officials, and

       4.      greater convenience to the citizenry to obtain various services in one place.

A facility study of Sandy City Hall was conducted by Todd/Rather Associates in 1979 to review
the need for city offices through 1985 and to suggest ways in which the facility needs could best
be accommodated.  The report concluded that the present building could handle the needs of the
remaining staff in the building  provided that the Court and Police staffs move into separate
facilities  until 1983, or to 1985 if the gym is used for additional expansion space. After 1985, it
reports, a new facility would be needed to handle these functions of city government.

Other alternatives to the construction of a new facility were  1  for other departments to move out
of the present building, or  2  for further remodeling or construction of additions to the present
building to provide the needed space.  The facility study favored construction of a new facility.

To be able to move into a new city hall by 1985, as recommended by the consultants, a time
schedule for the construction of a new complex would be:

        1980-83        Select site and financial planning for new complex
        1980           Expansion of department into space vacated by police and court
        1983           Select architect and start design of new complex
        1984           Complete construction documents for new complex
        1985           New complex under construction
        1986           Completion of new building


It is recommended that a 20-acre site be purchased for the civic center complex and that the City
attempt to group as many public buildings as possible at the site.  There are several key
considerations which must be examined in deciding the location of the new city hall complexes:

        1.     Will it be visible as a landmark and be sensitive to the surrounding environment?

        2.     Is the site centrally located, both physically and historically, to serve as the focal 
               point of the community?

        3.     Is there enough land available to house all facilities planned, to allow for adequate
               parking, a plaza or amphitheater, and space to expand?

        4.     Is there adequate access available to the site?  Are road improvements necessary?  
               Is the site served by mass transit?

        5.     Does the site allow for the grouping of other community facilities such as a 
               library or fire station?

        6.          Is the site located near offices with whom the city hall employees will interact,

        7.          Can the City afford the proposed recommendation?

                                   SENIOR CITIZENS¶ CENTER

Senior citizen centers area intended to provide opportunities for older residents to meet other
people and to share experiences.  Sandy¶s Senior Citizens¶ Center was previously the site of the
Sandy City Hall.  Located in the heart of Old Town, this facility is on a 1.0 acre site at 151 South
300 East.  In 1979, the Center was remodeled with Community Development Block Grant Funds
to provide more space for activities.

The planned activities, provided by the Salt Lake County Division of Aging, are primarily social
and include noontime meals, sewing, crafts, recreation and nutrition-consumer education.  Meals
are paid for by voluntary contribution and activities are free to anyone 60 years old and above. 
At present the programs are two days a week.  It is hoped that they will be expanded to five days
a week shortly.  

The physical space required for the center should be flexible to meet the changing needs of the
Center in the near future.  The planned construction of a new civic center complex would allow
the present city hall to be partly utilized for additional space for the Senior Citizens Center.  


                                      POLICE PROTECTION

As with other departments of Sandy City government, the Police Department has found that the
rapid residential growth of the City has significantly impacted the City¶s ability to adequately
provide police protection to residents.

A police department which is able to keep crime in a city under control is protecting not only the
city¶s residents and properties, but is also a potential source of community pride and identity, and
makes the community a more attractive place to live.

The Sandy City Police Department is organized into three divisions -- uniform, investigation and
service.  At present, there are 38 sworn officers, 10 support personnel  including two part-time
dispatchers , and five police cadets working for the city.  The ratio of officers per 1,000
population is slightly greater than 0.7.  The national standard is 2.0 officers per 1,000 population. 
However, because of the crime characteristics of Sandy and the fiscal restraints, the Police
Department is aiming to have 1.0 - 1.5 officers per 1,000 population  approximately 100 officers
when the growth has slowed down and the City¶s population has stabilized.

                                       FIRE PROTECTION

Fire protection not only contributes to the safety of Sandy residents, but also the quality of the
living environment within the City and the community pride and identity.  The four principal
objectives of fire protection are:

       1.      to prevent fires from starting,
       2.      to prevent loss of life an property when a fire starts,
       3.      to confine a fire to the place of origin, and
       4.      to put out fires.


The major factor in locating fire stations is response time, i.e., the time between ignition of the
fire and the arrival of the Fire Personnel and equipment with the capability to get water on the
fire ³in sufficient quantity to be effective.´  Response time from the station to the fire is
measured along the most direct street routes.  The distances should be shortest to businesses and
other high hazard districts, and longest to scattered residential districts.  For example, statistics
have indicated that an uncontrolled fire will increase 400 times per minute and cause total
damage to the average residential building if allowed to burn more than 7 minutes.  Total damage
in mobile homes will occur within 1 - 1 1/2 minutes.

General standards for fire protection, based on the response time and other factors, are set and
evaluated by the National Board of Fire Underwriters  part of the American Insurance
Association , a nonprofit association of stock fire insurance companies.  The evaluation

examines such variables as:

        1.     the type of areas to be protected, e.g., business, industrial, institutional, residential
               by density types, or a combination of them;

        2.     the number and location of fire stations;

        3.     the age of structures to be protected

        4.     land use factors -- natural and man-made barriers, location of heavy traffic zones, 
               remote areas to be served, patterns and designs and conditions of streets in the 
               vicinity of fire stations;

        5.     fire flow; and

        6.     response time.


Although these variables are considered in the overall determination of fire protection adequacy,
the city¶s fire rating is determined specifically by analyzing: water supply  fire flow , fire

inspections, structural conditions of buildings, climate and other unusual conditions.  Each of
these factors is measure against standards, and points are assigned when deficiencies exist.  For

The existing fire stations are presently located at  1  1210 East 9400 South,  2  50 South Center

A first response  or one alarm fire  in Sandy sends six on-duty personnel to drive and operate the
equipment  two engines, one ladder truck, and ambulance , and a Battalion Chief.  Volunteer
forces report either to assigned stations or directly to the fire to fill any manpower deficits.  Once
on the scene of a fire, the Battalion Chief has the option to reduce the size of the response, or
sound a second alarm for more equipment and manpower.

The Fire Department has problems with the County islands similar to the Police Department. 
The City recently signed a contract with Salt Lake County whereby the Sandy Fire Department
will answer fire calls in the County islands with a first response team and equipment, and the
County will answer calls in Sandy  with a first response team  when they are located near County
stations.  This will provide coverage until the governmental entity responsible for the are can
arrive at the fire with more extensive equipment.

 In addition to response time, the capability of a fire force is partially measured by their ability to
apply water to the fire  fire flow .  Fire flow is the amount of water in gallons per minute which
is required and applied by the Fire Department to control a fire, and which is needed to maintain
protection of other neighboring properties.  The short range goal of the Sandy Fire Department is
to establish fire flow of 2000 gallons per minute.  A proposed ordinance is now being considered
which would require buildings needing an excess of 2000 gallons per minute to install automatic
sprinkling systems and also would require installation of sprinklers in buildings with smaller
square footage than is now required in the Uniform Building Code.

Education

City planning must be aware of plans for school sizes, locations, and programs because an entire
city benefits from schools, the city may be measured by the quality of its educational
opportunities, and schools are one of the most important factors in making the city attractive for
home buyers.

Schools not only serve to educate residents and their children, but serve as a major focal point of
neighborhoods, along with distinctive housing types, natural features, development patterns,
place names; influence residents¶ perceptions of their community; and serve as a stabilizing
influence.  Although the value a school will be to a neighborhood changes over time, the school
facility and playground continually serve as symbols of the neighborhood and conjure up
residents¶ memories of µhome.¶

This report will review the historical development of schools, the neighborhood cycle, the Jordan

                                         BACKGROUND

Once an individual¶s responsibility, education has come to be viewed as a basic right.  The U.S.
Constitution did not specifically provide for education by the Federal Government, because it
assumed that education was a state responsibility.  In the early years of this country, most states
passed legislation giving local government the power to levy taxes for schools; however, most
communities did not choose to set up a school system.  As a result, the responsibility to educate
children rested primarily with parents.

Between 1830-60, elementary education became an integral part of the American way of life. 
This period is often referred to as the ³Age of the Common School.´  The state and Federal
governments required local governments to provide educational opportunities for its residents

Page 6 - 14                                                         Chapter VI - Community Facilities
                                                                        Sandy City General Plan




and provided monies to assist local governments financing of public education.

The turn of the century enhanced this change in attitude toward education because of  1  the
increase in population,  2  the need for a highly educated citizenry,  3  the passage of child labor
laws coupled with compulsory attendance in schools,  4  the shift from a predominantly rural to
an urban population, and  5  the developing need to foster socialization.  Enrollments began to
increase dramatically.  As state and local governments recognized the need for and the
inadequacies of the school system, they became increasingly committed to alleviating the
problems of inadequate school facilities.


The first half of the century also brought major change in attitude toward secondary schools. 
Originally set up to prepare students for college, high schools gained new importance as
compulsory attendance and vocational training were introduced into the high school curriculum. 
As a result, students were allowed to enter directly into the labor market.

The architectural style of school buildings often reflected the public attitudes toward education. 
Early construction was characterized by multi-story, box-like buildings.  In the 1950 s, the
relationship between students and the environment in which they worked was a major concern in
building design.  As a result, buildings became more attractive and comfortable, and rigid
institutional architecture was replaced by more open space.  Schools became single-story
buildings with recreational and athletic facilities.  Moveable furniture and flexible classroom
sizes allowed adoption of new programs and the introduction of creative new teaching methods.

In recent years, school system efforts for total community involvement is reflected in the
expanded community recreational opportunities and adult education programs offered in the
schools.  As a result, the availability of education has become a fundamental part of community
life.2

                                         NEIGHBORHOOD CYCLE

Another factor in analyzing the adequacy of school sites is a review of the neighborhood cycle. 
The neighborhood cycle refers to the processes by which the nature of a subdivision changes. 
For example, an are often begins with all new houses and a large majority of young married
families.  After 10-15 years, the children grow up and move out, and homes are sold because
family needs change.  New families, often younger than the now mature families, move in.  The
rise and fall and rise again of children/family ratios in neighborhoods is a continual process.  It
must be followed with care because it determines an area¶s demand for government services,
particularly schools and parks.

Enrollment

and maintain the District¶s quality educational standards.

District has had to absorb.  In the fifty year period from 1904-1955, the enrollment had tripled in

these variable affect future enrollment.  For example, the recent increase in the prime lending rate
has severely decreased the demand for housing in the are and so has temporarily decreased the

                                      CHILD CARE CENTERS

Across the nation, there is an increasing demand for child care centers.  Many young mothers are
becoming more involved in community activities, outside personal interests, hobbies or work. 
For example, in 1979 approximately 50% of Utah¶s women worked and in 1976 it had been
estimated that 26.1% of the then working women were married and had children under 6 years
old.  In addition, there has been a growing trend for child educators to encourage parents to get
their 3-4 year old children involved in socializing environments and structured learning
programs.  Because of these demands, friends, neighbors and relatives who used to tend young
children while mothers were out are being replaced by professional child tenders.

Child care centers go by many different names such as nursery school, day care center, and pre-
school depending on the particular type of curriculum and activities offered.  Most child care
centers operate during the weekdays all year round.  Usually they require children to be enrolled
in a weekly program for a minimum of 1-3 months and will not accept drop-ins.  Flexibility is
provided by the various types of programs offered by child care centers to accommodate working
mothers¶ schedules as well as mothers desiring child tending services only one morning a week.

               Sandy City General Plan




Some of the larger centers provide both day care and pre-school programs.  Other centers provide
day care before or after a child attends formal education programs and may even provide
transportation to and from the elementary school.

In Sandy, child care centers provide residents with a wide variety of types of child care centers. 
The City has both small home-occupation centers and large commercial centers which offer day
care, preschool and structured recreational programs.  In addition to the private child care centers
serving the Sandy City Area, the Copperview Community Center has a public funded child care
center and the Alta High School has a small child care center for educational purposes for their
child development class.

Cities have approached planning for child care centers in several ways.  One theory says they
should be built in a location to best serve the immediate neighborhood.  The children should live
within easy walking distance, approximately 1/4 mile, and major streets should not have to be
crossed.  The center should be located near a school, community center or church and should
blend in with the surrounding residential environment.  Others claim child care centers impact
residential areas too much and for convenience purposes should be located on major streets and
near commercial centers, places of employment or transit stops.

Because of these conflicting opinions, cities have zoned child care centers as permitted or
conditional uses in commercial as well as residential zones and have tried to adopt regulation
standards for such things as parking access, open spaces, fences and landscaping for child care
centers.  Though most child care centers have a capacity of 60-120 children, a home-occupation
child care center for 10-15 children may not impact a neighborhood or serve the same clientele as
a commercial child care center for 300 children.

State law and Sandy City ordinances require licensing of certain kinds of child care centers. 
However, since many child care center owners are not aware of require licensing , only
neighbors¶ complaints to the City expose unlicensed child care centers.  Substandard facilities
and programs, therefore, may unknowingly exist in the community since unlicensed facilities
may not meet required child care center standards required through licensing procedures.

Licensing is one way for cities to evaluate community services such as child care centers. 
Because of the number of unlicensed child care centers in the Sandy City area, pertinent data on
all of the child care center services in the community is unavailable.  It is therefore difficult to
assess the present and future needs and demands for quality day care centers in Sandy City.  An
informal survey was sent to all known child care centers in the City and responses indicated a
need for more child care centers especially for elementary school age and handicapped children.

Park Impact Fees: Impact fees are used increasingly by local governments in an attempt to help
meet the costs in providing services and facilities resulting from rapid growth.  In general, the
courts have upheld impact fees as part of a city¶s police power to regulate zoning as long as:

        1.     they are seen as user fees or service charges and not as additional taxes;

        2.     they meet the costs incurred by the city to provide services and are not used only 
               to increase the general fund;

        3.     they are based on studies which show the reasonableness of the decision that city, 
               not just adopted from another city; and

        4.     they are deposited into a special fund and only used for the purpose for which
               they were imposed, e.g., water fees for water systems.

Sandy City impact fee system meets these criteria and are used to partially finance park
development  the other portions are from federal funds and the general fund revenues .  In the
1979-1980 fiscal year, $300 was charged per lot on new development.

School Districts as Special Districts: When local governments have been unable financially or
jurisdicitionally to provide certain services, such as education, they have relied generally upon
special districts.  Created by the state legislatures, these special districts are independent
governments which possess corporate powers, as well as fiscal and administrative independence
from city and county governments.  They have more limited purposes and taxing powers than do
counties and municipalities.

Special districts were used by local authorities throughout the United States before the turn of the
century for maintenance of toll roads, canals, and local roads, and for protection against ravages
of fire and flood.  These units of government generally provided limited services, benefited a
small group of people, and rarely affected programs of the general purpose governments.  During
the 1930 s and the Depression, and during the suburban expansion following World War II,
special districts became more numerous and their purposes and powers expanded.  School
Districts are usually the largest of these districts in most states.9  Other special districts serving
Sandy City are the Salt Lake County Library System, Water Districts, Sewer Districts, Crescent
Cemetery District, etc.

Concern Over Property Tax To Finance Schools: Public schools, historically, have been
supported primarily by local property tax revenues.  The property tax has been used most often
because it can be levied efficiently at local levels and because no other tax is capable of
providing as much revenue.  One of the major reasons for this is that as people and businesses
move to the suburbs, municipalities lose revenue from sales tax and business license fees which
may result in the shrinking of the tax base.  Land, however, is immobile and therefore is a base
upon which local governments can safely levy taxes without fearing tax shrinkage.

But cities and courts throughout the country increasingly have been concerned with the equity of
the tax burden in using property taxes as the major revenue source for schools.  The wealth of the
area, which is usually reflected in revenue received from property taxes, determines the quality
and quantity of government services and varies substantially.

This disparity has been a subject of major court decisions, the most notable to education being
Serrano vs. Priest.10  In this case, the California Supreme Court declared that school financing on
the basis of local property and wealth violates state constitutional requirements for equal
protection.

By using property taxes as the main resource for education, the Court declared, the child¶s
education becomes a function of the wealth of the neighborhood.  The courts have not held that
property taxes may not be used for school support, only that the tax resources must be equalized
among local governments.  As a result, state governments are assuming an increasing role in
furthering local education expenditures through state aid programs based on equalizing formulas
or the µweighted pupil¶ principle.

The 40 school districts in Utah were created as special districts by the state legislature.  Most of
the school districts coincide with county boundaries except in the larger urban areas such as in
Salt Lake County where there are four school districts: Salt Lake City, Murray City, Granite and
Jordan.

Through the Uniform School Fund, the State has tried to insure provision of a minimum school
program in each of the school districts, whether rural or urban.  Each school district is required to
levy a minimum of 24 mills to finance this basic program which includes maintenance and
operating costs.  The required basic school program must meet curriculum, facility, and staff
standards as established by the Utah State Board of Education.

In past years, the Jordan School District has been one of the wealthiest districts in the state due to
the large amount of property tax revenue paid by Kennecott Copper Company.  However, recent
federal legislation has allowed environmental pollution control cost to be tax-exempt and has
caused Jordan School District tax revenues to be dramatically reduced.

If a school district levies more revenue than is needed to cover the costs of the basic program, the
excess is placed in the Uniform School Fund.  Other State taxes, such as the 8% tax on all wine
and liquor sold in the state liquor stores, are also placed in the Uniform School Fund account. 
School districts that are unable to finance the basic program may receive up to 75% of the funds
needed from the Uniform School Fund.  In this manner each student in the state is given equal
opportunity to benefit from the educational services provided in the minimum school program.

To calculate the costs of the basic school program, each school district is guaranteed a specified
amount of funding for each weighted pupil unit in the school district.  In 1979-1980, the
weighted pupil unit was increased from $795 to $852.  A weighted pupil unit  WPU  is an index
to compare different school districts which may have very different average daily attendance,
average class size, and professional staff experience.  For example, in 1979-1980, the Jordan
School District had 51,035,850 WPU and received $43,482,544 though the total student
enrollment was 46,387 in September 1979.

School districts may also vote a maximum of 10 mills to finance their education program in
addition to the required 24 mill levy.  The Jordan School District Board levy was approved
several years ago at only 5 mills.  At present, this levy is only 3.72 mills, though it had reached 5
mills before the reassessment of property values in Salt Lake County in 1978.

The State also has other funding available to distribute among the school districts by
participatory weighted pupil units to help finance special programs.  Demonstrated programs
such as those for talented and gifted children, the handicapped, bi-lingual classes, music classes
in elementary schools, parenting classes, vocational training, and summer home economics may
receive funding in this manner.

However, none of the above described funding sources can solve the problems the Jordan School
District continually faces of accelerating enrollment.  A school district may hold a bond election. 
The Jordan School District has done this on several occasions in the past and in March 1979
bonded to its fullest capacity  16% of the district¶s assessed value .  The District has continued to
pay off its bonds and at present it is in a position to hold another bond election.

The State also has three programs to aid school districts in need of new and/or expanded
facilities: the Continuing School Building Program, the Critical School Building Program, and
the School Building Revolving Fund.  The Continuing School Building Program guarantees each
school district $700 per school for capital outlay projects as long as the school district also levies
tax that would raise equivalent matching money.  The Critical School Building Program requires
 1  the school district levy 18.0 mills, and to have a capital outlay plan,  3  that the building
program is more than can be provided by the Continuing School Building Program, and  3  that
the project be identified in the annual report of the State¶s critical school building needs.  When a
school district has done all this, the building program will be considered as part of the minimum
school program.  The School Building Revolving Account is a $1 million loan available to
school districts which are bonded to 95% of capacity, have levied 18 mills, are unable to
complete a project, and can repay the state within five years.

In addition to these local and state funding sources, there are numerous federal funding programs.
