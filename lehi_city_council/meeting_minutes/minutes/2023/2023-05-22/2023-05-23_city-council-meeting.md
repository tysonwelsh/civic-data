2. Presentations and Reports
2.1 Moderate Income Housing Goals Discussion
Brittany Harris, City Planner, presented on the moderate-income housing goals. She highlighted
the need to provide detailed descriptions of implemented actions to the State to meet their
requirements. She stated that achieving six out of seven goals would secure priority
consideration for funding. Failing to meet the requirements would result in ineligibility for
funding from the state tax commission and there could be daily fees for noncompliance. The
discussion then shifted to the penalties for noncompliance and the duration of ineligibility for
funding. The Council expressed frustration with the requirements and discussed concerns with
the penalty.

Councilor Koivisto shared her reservations about reducing the lot size for detached accessory
dwelling units (ADUs), as it would lead to high density and contradict the purpose of low-
density areas. The councilors agreed on the difficulty of the requirements and the state's
approach.

The discussion then moved to goal six, which aimed to create an overlay zone allowing
moderate-income housing in any zone, subject to certain requirements.

Mayor Johnson expressed frustration about the ongoing growth issues and the need to address
housing supply. They discussed various suggestions to reduce costs, such as decreasing
requirements and impact fees, but Mayor Johnson argued that these measures primarily benefit

developers and may not significantly reduce costs for customers. They also acknowledged the
free market and the ability for individuals to buy homes for profit.

The conversation touched on the challenge of balancing growth and the limitations of band-aid
solutions. The discussion then shifted to the idea of deed-restricted moderate-income housing
and the possibility of allowing density in currently restricted areas. The council members
discussed the potential pros and cons, including concerns about the need to consider spacing
requirements and utility capacity.

The Council explored the concept of an overlay zone that would facilitate affordable housing
without requiring general plan zoning changes. The discussion also touched on setbacks,
detached ADUs (Accessory Dwelling Units), and the benefits of impact fees. The council
members debated the viability of the overlay zone and the potential effectiveness of detached
ADUs in promoting affordability. They emphasized the importance of careful drafting and
tailoring the regulations to achieve the intended goals. The conversation concluded with an
interest in seeing a draft proposal for an overlay zone that allows non-residential use and spreads
out affordable units across different areas. Councilor Condie expressed interest in exploring
creative solutions and promoting homeownership, while Mayor Johnson expressed concerns
about an overreliance on apartments.


2.2 Conservation Easement Discussion
Todd Munger presented on the possibility of exploring Conservation Easements. The focus was
on the 900-plus acres Lehi owned in Traverse Mountain and whether owning it provided
sufficient protection. The concern was that future Councils could change the area plan, so the
idea of conserving the land and putting it out of reach was raised.

The mountains and foothills were considered crucial to the community's identity, and preserving
trails, mountain bike trails, and educational centers was seen as important. Draper's example of
conserving 4,500 acres through a conservation easement was mentioned.

It was discussed that the conservation easement would only apply to property already owned by
the City. The idea of finding a trustworthy party to hold the easement was raised, with Utah open
lands and Utah State Recreation mentioned as potential beneficiaries.

If there was enough interest, further exploration of a model easement language and property
selection would be pursued with assistance from legal. The conversation concluded with a
mention of various properties, including those in the Traverse Mountain and Sky areas, that
could be considered for conservation easements.

It was emphasized that careful consideration would be needed to address conflicts and ensure the
rights of different user groups, such as bikers, hikers, and hunters, were protected.

Overall, the conversation revolved around the importance of preserving open space, considering
conservation easements, and finding the right approach to protect natural areas and accommodate
various interests.

15. Consideration of Ordinance #38-2023 approval of a Development Code Amendment to
    Chapter 37, removing two-family dwellings from the design requirements based on State
    Code.
    Kim Struthers, Community Development Director, stated that a bill was passed in the 2022
    legislative session, limiting cities' ability to regulate aesthetics for single-family and two-
    family homes. However, the city's code still included regulations for two-family dwellings.
    The change proposed was to remove regulations on architecture and building materials for
    regular duplexes in standard zones. It was clarified that this change did not affect planned
    communities or special developments that have separate agreements.

    Motion:     Councilor Koivisto moved to approve Ordinance #38-2023 approval of a
                Development Code Amendment to Chapter 37, removing two-family dwellings
                from the design requirements based on State Code; including the Development
                Review Committee and Planning Commission comments. Councilor Southwick
                seconded the motion.

    Roll Call Vote: Councilor Albrecht, Yes; Councilor Condie, No; Councilor Hancock, Yes;
    Councilor Koivisto, Yes; and Councilor Southwick, Yes. The motion passed with 4 in favor
    and 1 opposed.
