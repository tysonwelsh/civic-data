Amanda Ercanbrack, Director of Customer Service and former City Recorder, presented. Ms.
Ercanbrack shared an overview of GRAMA (Government Records Access and Management
Act). The intent of this legislation grants access for the public to public records, but also
preserves privacy for personal information. A GRAMA request is not a request for information;
it is a request for records that the City possesses. GRAMA requests can be submitted in a variety
of ways, including online at provo.org/grama. The process for the City to respond to a request
involves collaboration between the Recorder’s office and other city departments—the responsive
records are gathered, classified, and prepared accordingly (including redaction if necessary). In
some cases, records may not be available through the City and the requestor is directed to the
agency which manages the corresponding records.

Ms. Ercanbrack reviewed the classification process for records released through a GRAMA
request and the types of records which are classified as private, controlled, or protected. The
volume of GRAMA requests has increased dramatically in the last several years. With the ease
of access with the online form and directing all requests to go through the GRAMA request
process, this number has increased to about 33 requests per month in 2021. Ms. Ercanbrack also
reviewed the process for a GRAMA appeal.

Brian Jones, City Attorney, also presented. During the annual GRAMA training which Mr. Jones
has conducted at the beginning of every year, he typically only reviews the requirements of the
law; he thanked Ms. Ercanbrack for reviewing the process in more detail. Mr. Jones shared
several comments regarding GRAMA and the complexities of the law as written in state code. In
recent years, the City has sent all records requestors through the GRAMA process, which
provides additional protection for the city and for city employees who are responding to a
request. It also gives a record of the request itself and provides additional documentation
regarding what records were released or which may not have been. This maintains transparency
and consistency in the process. Mr. Jones referenced and elaborated on Ms. Ercanbrack’s
comments on the distinction between information versus records. In response to Councilors who
receive requests for information from constituents or members of the public on a regular basis,
Mr. Jones suggested that if the Council or Council office created the record or information, they
can decide when and where it is released. If information came from another department, the issue
would best be referred to that department. This is the same advice he has dispensed to
department directors throughout the city as well. Presentation only.

3.      A presentation from the Housing Committee regarding an ordinance change for
        ADU’s to comply with Utah State Law. (21-102) (0:46:29)

John Magness, Policy Analyst, and Robert Mills, Planning Supervisor, presented. Mr. Magness
explained that the changes to ADU regulations would likely play out in the community in stages,
with initial stages seeing more compliance with existing ADUs, then the broader population
responding to the changes later on. There is typically a lag time between changing ordinances
and the innovation being communicated out to the community. The highest costs will be parking
and knowledge of the process. Mr. Magness commented on the recent changes to BYU’s student
housing policies. Data shows that most students want to live within 20 minutes of their university
(driving or walking time). He shared data from other universities showing how student housing
adapted following similar university policy changes. Mr. Magness displayed several maps which
showed different radii from campus and he noted what acreage each radius would add. Mr.
Magness invited Mr. Mills to review the drafted ordinance and the proposed changes. Mr. Mills
reviewed the current context and what is permitted under the current city code.

Councilors shared comments and asked questions throughout the presentation. Councilor George
Handley asked for clarification about the requirement to allow ADUs and Mr. Mills confirmed
that the language in state code set a specific minimum; the city can exempt 67% of residential
areas. Mr. Handley suggested language be clear on stating “at a minimum, 33%” so the
distinction was clear. Mr. Mills reviewed a summary of the proposed changes. The Housing
Committee forwarded a recommendation for the most permissive language and then will update
this draft based on the Council’s feedback. Mr. Mills reviewed the proposed zones and proposed
requirements for ADUs. Cliff Strachan, Council Executive Director, commented on some of the
limitations which have been common in university cities and historically in Provo City.
Councilor Shannon Ellsworth commented that every other Utah city which does not have a
university, no matter its size or population, has to permit ADUs in 75% of the city. Councilor
David Harding noted several of the enforcement tools added under state law such as fines and
liens. Councilor Bill Fillmore also mentioned his concerns about enforcement challenges. Mr.
Magness responded to Mr. Fillmore’s concern about compliance with HB082 and he elaborated
on the use of liens on the property as a deterrent for illegal ADUs.

4.      A discussion on towing fees (21-105) (2:14:51)

Cliff Strachan, Council Executive Director, presented. Mr. Strachan reviewed the relevant
sections of state code which outlined permitted towing fees. The state code authorizes a
maximum amount, and cities are able to authorize a lower amount. The state rate has changed in
recent years and the City rate has not been adjusted for some time. The proposal is to change the
City’s rate to a percentage of the state’s maximum, so that it would not require frequent
adjustments as the State’s rate changes due to inflation and other factors.

Councilor David Sewell provided additional context as to why Council Leadership raised this
issue. The intent was to maintain a level of fairness for the related businesses—if there is no
inflation adjustment over a long period of time, that essentially acts as regulating this type of
business out of business. If there is support for this general concept, then the Council can
propose bringing back an ordinance amendment to that effect.

Councilor David Shipley commented with his support, as most cities follow the state’s amount.
He recently learned that several cities do not allow non-consent towing and he wanted to learn
more about those situations. However, in principle, he felt it was important to do an inflation
adjustment and by utilizing a percentage, this would tie the rate to State law and could alleviate
any concerns of a potential legal battle in the future over rates.

Brian Jones, City Attorney, shared a clarification regarding the vocabulary on towing in state
statute and what different situations entailed. Non-consent police-generated tows are permitted
everywhere. There are two categories of non-consent, non-police tows—requests initiated by the
property owner are permitted everywhere. The other category is tows that result from the towing
companies patrolling the lot—Mr. Jones uses the shorthand phrase “patrol towing” to refer to
this sub-category only. Mr. Jones clarified that Mr. Shipley’s comments referred to this—some
cities do not allow patrol towing and require a direct request from the property owner to initiate a
non-consent, non-police tow.
