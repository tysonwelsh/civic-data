1. Housing Needs
   Mr. Brierley stated due to the number of items coming before the Commission dealing with
   the need for housing, he felt there should be some discussion relating to housing in an effort
   to create policies and determine appropriate zoning and standards. He stated while there is
   little available data in terms of housing needs, one constant is that there is a need to
   balance various types of housing. There will always be a need for stability such as
   traditional single-family homes in neighborhood development, but there also is a need for
   more transient or rental housing. He indicated the General Plan is 20+ years old, and many
   of its forecasts have been exceeded, but there is little data available forecasting future
   needs. He stated he has made some efforts to review existing sources along the Wasatch
   Front who have done some data forecasting as well as census data and has found some
   trends relating to Ogden and projected population information. It is anticipated that nearly
   5,400 new dwelling units will be needed by 2040. However, the types of housing available
   often depend on household income, which directs the type of units needed. According to
   existing data, it is anticipated housing units should be 65% apartments, 15% townhomes,
   and 20% single-family homes. Apartments could be defined as subsidized housing, student
   housing or accessory dwelling units, which would create a density of 20+ units per acre and
   could be various building types, which are tied to family income levels.

     Commission expressed concern that school population continues to decline and that while
     there is some need for transient housing, it also affects the overall community when
     residents are always changing. It was indicated apartments decrease the net worth of both
     households and communities and other opportunities should be created to increase home
     ownership such as cottage-sized lots or interior development of existing long, narrow lots
     which are common in some parts of the City. Commission expressed concern the number of
     apartments being built exceeds the needs of the growth of the community with many existing

   complexes offering rooms for rent long after their completion. Commission also stated some
   means should be created to attract residents to stay in Ogden as long-term residents rather
   than living here on a temporary basis until income level improves.

   Mr. Brierley described types of housing available based on family income level, stating
   normal housing costs is 30% of the family income, and based on existing data, most
   residents would then be living in multi-family units. He stated Ogden has limited space
   available for new low density residential development and additional options to provide
   needed housing might need to be developed. Commission suggested options such as
   allowing development with smaller yards. Streamlining the process for the approval of
   accessory dwelling units also was discussed, which might include developing several
   engineered plans for new construction which would be pre-approved by the City and allow
   residents to meet codes while reducing review time and costs of creating new plans.
   Commission discussed development options for infill properties, finding that creating
   emergency access often is a deterrent. Commission favored ideas presented such as
   allowing smaller lots, attached row houses and courtyard developments, while noting each
   may have specific fire or building code requirements which could make them more costly to
   construct.

   Mr. Brierley suggested some options may be to create new zone classifications which may
   allow other options relating to lot size, reduced lot widths, access standards and/or reduced
   parking regulations. He also suggested some commercial zoning which is more conducive
   to residential development might be rezoned to a higher density residential zone.
   Commission also felt some efforts should be made to repurpose existing commercial
   buildings.

   Mr. Brierley summarized stating there is a great need to develop a housing plan with current
   data and better information than is now available in the General Plan, noting there needs to
   be a variety of housing types, with all items remaining in balance.

2. Group Dwelling standards

   Mr. Rypien stated while a petition had been submitted to amend specific items in the Group
   Dwelling section of the Code, the Commission has suggested a more comprehensive
   approach be taken in order to make these more appealing options for land development,
   which also may create better options for home ownership as dividing a multi-level apartment
   into condominium space is complicated. The intent is also to create a better product which
   is more desirable in the City with well-planned open space with a variety of housing types
   and unit sizes.

   He stated there is some confusion in the existing code, as group dwellings are listed as
   conditional uses in some zones while other zones are silent and yet others identify the use
   in the General Plan but are not a listed use in the Ordinance. The current code also
   identifies types of group dwellings based on available land, with small lot projects on less
   than one acre and neighborhood projects on properties with 1-8 acres, but is silent as to
   properties larger than eight acres. Staff is suggesting wording be changed to indicate
   neighborhood group dwellings would be development larger than one acre. Staff also is
   suggesting the minimum lot area requirements be changed to allow parcels over 10,000
   square feet in most zones and from 3,000 square feet to eight acres in the R9 zone.

   Mr. Rypien indicated the petition requests consideration of existing density penalties in the
   group dwelling standards due to minimum lot area requirements for each new building

                                             ~2~
    proposed. As an example, a single building may accommodate 20 units on an acre of
    property while if in five separate buildings, units are limited to five per acre. Staff is
    suggesting the minimum lot area requirement be established only for the first building, which
    would then allow 20 units per acre in ten buildings. The petition also is asking for
    consideration in side yard setbacks based on building orientation, which requires a 30’
    setback while a setback for a single apartment building is 10’. Staff suggests the setback be
    established by the underlying zone with no changes based on the building’s orientation.
    The current code also requires additional space when adjacent to single-family residential
    development, and while Staff agrees setbacks could be adjusted based on adjacent land
    uses, buildings should also have the ability to have step-backs to create a vision of space
    without requiring the larger setbacks. Buildings within a single group dwelling project should
    be spaced based on framing in outdoor spaces and providing green space within the project.
    He stated building design and placement should be context sensitive and separation
    between buildings within a project could be reduced from what currently exists. The
    requirements for front-facing buildings and front glazing requirements would not be affected.

    He then addressed existing penalties based on landscaping requirements stating a group
    dwelling project is required 30% on-site landscaping, excluding required setback areas.
    This often creates unusable and poorly designed open space and amenities. Staff suggests
    consideration might be to reduce on-site landscaping to 15% while requiring usable
    recreational opens pace with high-quality amenities which results in better use of the space
    by tenants. He also expressed Staff has some concerns with the tree and shrub
    requirements based on the number of units, as there are now larger projects now being
    developed. Staff is suggesting this be revised to require one tree and five 5-gallon shrubs
    per 1,500 square feet of lot area.

    Commission agreed the proposed amendments would encourage the use of group dwellings
    rather than single apartment buildings, and felt open space within a project should be a
    gathering area with seating and amenities rather than a patch of grass. They also felt these
    should be located to be easily accessible to all residents and not on the fringes of the project
    where they would be close to some but far from others.

3. 2023 Legislature Follow Up
   Mr. Rypien reviewed recent State legislation which will affect both the General Plan and
   Zoning Codes as follows:
   1) Moderate Income Housing Plan/Report – Our initial report has been completed but
      subsequent reports now have also been identified which will be ongoing with the
      complete report due August 1st . He also reviewed consequences for noncompliance and
      Commission expressed hope that outlying communities would do more to accommodate
      moderate income housing rather than the burden being only in Ogden.
   2) Accessory dwelling units may be considered an internal unit if it shares a common wall
      with the primary dwelling. This would allow loft apartments in attached garage to be
      considered an internal ADU.
   3) Station Area Plans which are required of cities where a dedicated transit line exists are to
      be certified by December 31, 2025. New legislation identifies the ability to use previous
      plans so long as they promote the identified objectives.
   4) Water Preservation Plan is required to be adopted before December 31, 2024 identifying
      ways to improve water conservation, reduce waste water, analyze current and future
      water usage, development patterns and allow low-water landscaping.
      Commission discussed ability for the use of gray water.
   5) Subdivision Review Process for single-family, two-family and townhomes require an
      administrative land use authority and may include both Staff and Planning Commission

                                               ~3~
      review, with separate applications for preliminary and final approval but is limited to one
      public hearing and prohibiting review of the final plat by Planning Commission or City
      Council. Application review process for both preliminary and final approval are identified
      and corrections must cite applicable City ordinances. Lot line adjustments do not qualify
      as subdivision amendments.
   6) Annexation. Partial annexations of islands or peninsulas are allowed where previously an
      entire island or peninsula was required to be annexed.
   7) Temporary Land Use Regulations may be enacted by the City Council without Planning
      Commission recommendation, must be tied to a compelling public interest and is limited
      to six months without extensions. If new language is adopted applicants are required to
      follow the new rules if an application is made during or after the six months.
   8) Infrastructure/Landscaping. Local residential streets maximum width of 32’ of pavement.
      Financial bonding only for public landscaping on public property or will be dedicated as
      public property maintained by an HOA or is adjacent to a public trail. If all health and
      safety requirements are satisfied a Certificate of Occupancy can only be denied if
      financial assurance has not been provided for public landscape or infrastructure
      improvements. Financial guarantees are limited to 110% of improvement costs, requires
      a minimum of two methods of assurance and allows bonds to be released in phases as
      inspections occur.
   9) A developer must be notified if any rights waived by a development agreement.
   10) Referenda on land use regulations is prohibited if adopted by a unanimous vote and
      Station area plan regulations receive a 2/3 vote.
   11) Public Noticing process identifies how cities are required to notice land use items and
      other procedures, requiring the same process for all cities.
   12) All mobile businesses shall be regulated equally between jurisdictions, including food
      trucks.
   13) Cities cannot adopt regulations that treat co-owned homes differently than other types of
      housing. Accessory dwelling units are not considered co-owned housing.
