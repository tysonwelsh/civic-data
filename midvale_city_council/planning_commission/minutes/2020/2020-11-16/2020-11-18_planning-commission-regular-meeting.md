Legal Considerations: Hypothetical
A developer has submitted an application to rezone a property from RM-12 to RM-25. The proposal
includes a number of affordable housing units. The project has left you with a number of legitimate
concerns about traffic, the proposed driveway locations, and proximity of the project to adjacent
homes. The audience has not brought up any of these concerns. Instead, they have made subtle and
even openly discriminatory comments about the possible future residents’ color, race, national origin,
religion, and family status. Because this is a legislative decision, can you deny this application?

• Possibly. I would recommend closing the public hearing and tabling the decision after some
  discussion. Although it is a legislative decision, the record is now filled with discriminatory
  statements that cannot be used to justify your decision. You will need to add your legitimate
  concerns to the record. I would recommend giving the applicant an opportunity to address your
  legitimate concerns. If they fail to address them, then you can deny the application. If they address
  the concerns, the public’s comments may require approving the project to avoid costly litigation.

Ethics
Ethics: Personal Bias
Being Objective is Everything.
As Planning Commissioners, you have been
asked to fairly apply the law, protect
established property rights, and advocate for
good planning principles in the City. Your
biases, if not set aside, will cause you to
violate your role, make unfair decisions, and
undermine the Public’s trust.

And, remember, putting aside personal bias
does not mean that you will always agree. It
means that you will allow the record and not
your feelings to determine your decision.
Ethics: Personal Benefit
No Planning Commission “Perks”
You are prohibited from using your position for
personal gain. This includes:

• Using confidential information to further your
  or someone else’s economic interest;
• Securing or attempting to secure special
  privileges or exemptions for yourself or others;
• Receiving or soliciting, directly or indirectly, a
  gift of substantial value or economic benefit.

A violation may result in criminal charges.
Ethics: Conflicts of Interest
Municipal Officers’ and Employees’ Ethics Act
Under State law, you are required to declare the following conflicts of interest:

• Assisting a party with a transaction with the City for compensation;
• Having an interest in a business entity regulated by the City;
• Having an interest in a business entity doing business with the City; and
• Having an investment that creates a conflict between your personal interests and your public
  duties.

You are required to disclose if you have a conflict of interest on any agenda item at the beginning of
the meeting. You are required to recuse yourself from that agenda item when it is discussed. Failure
to do so will result in removal from office and may result in criminal penalties.
Ethics: Conflicts of Interest (Cont.)
Other Conflicts of Interest
Utah law has a relatively narrow definition of a conflict of interest. The Planning Commission’s
Bylaws and Rules of Procedure have expanded this definition. It reads:

A conflict of interest is generally defined as gaining a private benefit either directly or indirectly, or material
personal gain or advantage for one’s self and to relatives, friends, employers, businesses, investments, or
groups and associations which hold some share of a person’s loyalty. Mere membership itself, in a group or
organization, is not considered a conflict of interest as to Commission action concerning such group or
association unless it is concluded such membership in itself would prevent an objective consideration of the
matter.

You are required to disclose if you have a conflict of interest on any agenda item at the beginning of
the meeting. You are required to recuse yourself from that agenda item when it is discussed.
Ethics: Ex Parte Communication
Avoiding Ex Parte Communication
Decisions are required to be made with information
gathered in public hearings and deliberations
completed in public meetings. People, including
applicants, neighbors, community members, and
maybe even elected officials or staff, are going to try to
lobby you on agenda items. Avoid these conversations.
They undermine the Public’s trust. Encourage those
lobbying you to speak at or to submit comments at a
public hearing so their concerns can be placed in the
record.
Ethics: Abstention
What About Ethical Gray Areas?
There are going to be ethical issues that are not black
and white. If you believe you cannot remain objective
or you believe that your impartiality may be
questioned, we ask that you disclose the issue and
abstain from voting. Although you will not vote, you
may continue to participate in the discussion.

As an example, you may choose to abstain from voting
if the applicant has a religious, fraternal, political, or
similar position of influence over you, and you feel that
your impartiality towards that person may be
questioned.
Ethics: Hypothetical
In your personal work, you work as a civil engineer. A company
has hired your company to complete the engineering work for a
new house in Midvale. The project will require direct
communication with the City Engineer and the Planning
Director. Normally your company is good about letting you
avoid these types of direct conflicts, but they’re swamped right
now and need you to get the project done. What do you do?

• You need to immediately disclose to the City that you are
  assisting a business in a transaction with the City. When the
  project comes before the Planning Commission, you will
  need to disclose the conflict at the beginning of the meeting
  and recuse yourself when it is discussed.
Ethics: Hypothetical
You recently had the opportunity to become a
silent partner in a small floral shop here on the
border of Sandy and Midvale. The business
books look great, and the floral shop is loved by
the community. You decided to invest. Do you
have any duty to disclose your investment to the
City? And if so, when?

• It depends. Is the shop located in Midvale or
  Sandy? If it’s in Midvale, you must disclose
  your interest to the City immediately. If it’s in
  Sandy, you don’t need to disclose anything.
Ethics: Hypothetical
A new project has been proposed on Main Street. Half of the
residents are thrilled, and the other half are upset because they
believe it threatens Main Street’s historic charm. A neighbor
begins causally discussing the project with you. They probably
don’t know you’re on the Planning Commission and that the
project is on next month’s agenda. What do you do?

• I would recommend disclosing that you’re on the Planning
  Commission and that you’re ethically obligated to not engage
  in the conversation. I would then tell them how excited you
  are that they are aware of the project and encourage them
  to submit their thoughts during the meeting.
Ethics: Hypothetical
A new project is being presented to the Planning Commission.
You know that it is controversial, but you are genuinely excited
for the project. You believe that it will kick off additional
economic development in an identified opportunity area. During
the presentation, you learn that your best friend when you were
growing up is the owner of the company purchasing the project
if it is developed. What do you do?

• I would err on the side of disclosing the relationship to be as
  transparent as possible. However, unless you believe that
  this jeopardizes your impartiality, I believe this relationship
  is probably too removed to suggest abstaining from voting.
Ethics: Hypothetical
A friend of yours also happens to be a developer.
Since being appointed to the Planning
Commission, they have begun treating you to Jazz
games. You believe that their generosity has
nothing to do with your position, and the timing is
just a coincidence since the Jazz season started
about the same time as your appointment. You
estimate the cost of your ticket to be about $60.
Can you accept their generosity?

• This is a difficult question. If you choose to go, I
  think the safest option is to either pay for your
  ticket or reimburse them for the cost of yours.

OPMA: Definitions
Electronic meeting means a public meeting convened or conducted by means of a conference using
electronic communications.

Meeting means the convening of a public body or a specified body, with a quorum present,
including a workshop or an executive session, whether in person or by means of electronic
communications, for the purpose of discussing, receiving comments from the public about, or
acting upon a matter over which the public body or specific body has jurisdiction or advisory power.
Meeting does not mean a chance gathering or social gathering.

Quorum means a simple majority of the membership of a public body, unless otherwise defined by
applicable law. For our commission, this means 3 members.
OPMA: Notice and Agenda
Notice
Public meetings must be noticed at least 24
hours in advance of each meeting. The notice
must include the date, time, place, and
agenda of the meeting.

Agenda
The agenda must reasonably specify the
topics being considered at the meeting. Each
topic must be listed as an agenda item. The
Planning Commission may not take final
action on an item unless it is on the agenda.
OPMA: Meeting
Ex Parte Communication and Deliberation
During Meeting.
Avoid communicating with interested parties or deliberating
with other Commissioners during the meeting using
electronic communication. The public’s business needs to be
conducted in public.

After-Meeting Meeting
One of the most common ways OPMA is violated is when
officials have an after-meeting meeting in which decisions
continue to be discussed or deliberated. Avoid this
temptation.

OPMA: Closed Meetings
Closed meetings are limited to very specific
purposes. Some examples include:

• Discussion of the character, professional
  competence, or physical or mental health of an
  individual; or
• Strategy sessions to discuss pending or
  reasonably imminent litigation.

Decisions may not be made in a closed session.

For our purposes, I do not foresee a need to ever
enter a closed session.
OPMA: Hypothetical
During a public meeting, a resident identifies a
particularly concerning loophole in the City’s zoning
code. You are horrified because you know that there
have been rumors about a project that could abuse this
loophole in the works. A simple modification to the
ordinance could close the loophole. The citizen is
demanding immediate action. What can you do?

• The item is not on the agenda so you cannot act on
  the issue in tonight’s meeting. However, the Chair
  may use their discretion to discuss the item and
  identify the issues and potential solutions.
OPMA: Hypothetical
A developer has requested that the Planning Commission reduce
the landscape buffer on its commercial project. This project has
been controversial and has frustrated many neighbors. It has
already been through a public hearing. The last thing the neighbors
want is to have the buffer reduced. This type of request does not
require a public hearing. At the meeting, comments are submitted
and members of the public demand to be heard on the issue. What
do you do?

• The item is not entitled to a public hearing. It is only entitled to
  be discussed in a public meeting. To avoid a multitude of issues,
  I would suggest not opening the item to public comment.

Tools for Difficult Decisions
If you are faced with a difficult decision
and don’t know how to proceed, here are
a few suggested tools:

• Stop and think. Silence is generally
  preferable to a poor knee-jerk reaction.
• Ask for help. My responsibility is to help
  you decipher the legal and ethical
  implications of your decisions.
• Table the item. Buy yourself and the
  other decision-makers time by passing
  over the item and coming back to it.
