2.      A presentation on the South Utah Valley Animal Shelter's euthanasia techniques
        (21-084) (0:21:49)

Captain Brian Wolken thanked Michael Sanders, Council Intern, for his thorough and balanced
report on the related issues to animal euthanasia. Kierstan Munford, Director of South Utah
Valley Animal Shelter, shared information about SUVAS’ use of carbon monoxide to euthanize
animals. Euthanasia is used to end animal suffering due to injury or other quality of life concerns
and to protect the public from vicious or feral animals. SUVAS has determined that carbon
monoxide is the best method for the shelter, as it creates safer conditions for the staff. Use of a
carbon monoxide unit is best-suited for these animals, which are not used to and do not prefer
human contact, and it is a painless, rapid method for animal euthanasia. This setup also reduces
the stress to veterinary staff and employees.

Councilor David Sewell asked whether the shelter has to euthanize many stray animals that are
turned in. Ms. Munford explained that SUVAS has not had to euthanize any adoptable animals
for over five years. They work with many fantastic rescue organizations and outlets who can
adopt animals. They also work with Petco in Spanish Fork to house some adoption-eligible
animals. Over 90% of cats and dogs go to loving homes. Mr. Sewell asked if that was unusual
compared to other parts of the country. Ms. Munford indicated that they have achieved this
through hard work to promote these programs.

Councilor George Handley asked if they have capacity for lethal injections at their site. He also
asked if they had data or information about the animals they euthanize that would be done by
injection. Ms. Munford explained that if an animal was too young for carbon monoxide, then
lethal injection is used. They typically consult with a veterinarian who will assess whether an
animal needs to be injected. In response to Mr. Handley’s follow-up question regarding the use
of space and resources, Ms. Munford indicated that lethal injection is more time-consuming. It
also presents more dangers to the staff if they are dealing with a dangerous animal.

Cliff Strachan, Council Executive Director, asked what the cost impact would be to their facility
if they were to move to a lethal injection process. Ms. Munford thought it would be more time-
consuming, so she believed that making this change would require staff time. Councilor Shannon
Ellsworth thanked Ms. Munford and her colleagues for the professional work they do and their

Captain Wolken explained that SUVAS adopts about 80% of animals that come in. Other
animals cannot be adopted or are injured, such as wild animals, feral cats, or animals that pose a
real danger to humans who come in contact. He explained that the language in the Utah Animal
Rights Coalition petition was inaccurate and misleading. He explained that the carbon monoxide
unit was professional-grade equipment. It stays on for 16 minutes, including the time used for
venting of the unit to ensure that the carbon monoxide has safely dissipated. He has never seen
the conditions described in the petition. Carbon monoxide blocks the uptake of oxygen into the
respiratory system, so the process is considered insidious death due to the gradual effect—the
animal falls asleep, goes unconscious, and dies painlessly. The American Veterinarian
Association considers carbon monoxide units acceptable with conditions—this system meets or
exceeds these conditions, which primarily relate to staff safety. The process is not cruel at any
time; sedation is given to animals before lethal injection, which is actually quite painful. Captain
Wolken noted also that the U.S. Humane Society finds carbon monoxide an acceptable method.

Captain Wolken explained that most of the issues come down to feral cats. Animal advocates do
not want these animals to be euthanized, but the costs would be borne emotionally by the
employees of the local animal shelters. Captain Wolken highlighted details of the Trap, Neuter,
and Release program that is used. Feral cats are negatively impacting the wildlife on the west
side and it is an ecological concern. He explained that the Humane Society of Utah has said that
methods of euthanasia must be pain-free, stress-free, and fear-free. Lethal injection is much more
anxiety-inducing for animals; thus the use of carbon monoxide units is the safer and more
humane way to put animals down.

Mr. Handley wanted to understand why, if this was the case, most animal rights organizations
were still advocating for lethal injection. Captain Wolken thought that these advocates did not
want any animals euthanized; he suspected they thought that shelters would ultimately not use
the injection method because it was stressful. Councilor David Harding echoed Ms. Ellsworth’s
comments earlier, and thanked Director Munford and employees at the shelter for the services
they provide the community. Mr. Strachan indicated that the City contributes $98,000 annually
to SUVAS. He noted that there were no specific decision points here for the Council; they

Michael Sanders, Council Intern, presented. Staff prepared a review of the academic and
legislative literature on animal euthanasia techniques. The method chosen needs to balance the
safety of the worker and what is most humane for the animal. Mr. Sanders reviewed the various

methods. There is no federal standard for use of carbon monoxide by animal shelters, and state
laws are quite mixed on the issue. In 22 states, there is a complete ban, with several states having
a partial ban. The remaining states have no formal ban, including Utah. For the past nine
legislative sessions, a bill has been introduced to the Utah Legislature regarding a ban on carbon
monoxide euthanasia, but it has not been passed before the session’s conclusion. Mr. Sanders
highlighted results from several studies regarding the use of carbon monoxide. Mr. Sanders also

4.      A presentation regarding the Westside Levee Plan. (21-079) (1:34:35)

Brian Torgersen, Public Services Division Director, and Ben Rood, CRS Engineers and Project
Manager for the West Side Levee Study, presented. Mr. Torgersen introduced several members
of staff. Mr. Torgersen reviewed the City’s levee system—this refers to earthen embankments
adjacent to the Provo River and Utah Lake. These levees were built in the 1980s in response to
historic flooding to provide protection for the river and lake. These were constructed by the
Army Corp. of Engineers, but are owned and maintained by Provo City. Mr. Torgersen shared a
timeline outlining the history and past process relative to the levees and floodplain mapping from
FEMA. In order to modernize these maps, FEMA updated the maps in the early 2000s. As of
2007, FEMA no longer considered the City’s levees sufficient to protect the city, despite the fact
that the levees are still functioning. The mapping process stalled for a time and resumed in 2017.
Since Jordanelle and Deer Creek Reservoirs serve as flood mitigation, this has relieved the need
for some of the levees on the Provo River. Provo City has asked FEMA to reevaluate several
factors which impact the flood conditions of this area. Mr. Torgersen highlighted some of the
frustrations arising from this process and the tensions between FEMA and the City.
