                       SPECIAL CITY COUNCIL
                             MINUTES
                           Tuesday, February 04, 2025
                           Approved February 26, 2025

The following are the minutes of the City Council meeting of the Herriman City Council.
The meeting was held on Tuesday, February 4, 2025, at 9:00 a.m. in the Herriman City
Fort Herriman Conference Room, 5355 West Herriman Main Street, Herriman, Utah.
Adequate notice of this meeting, as required by law, was posted in the City Hall, on the
City’s website, and delivered to members of the Council, media, and interested citizens.

Presiding: Mayor Lorin Palmer

Councilmembers Present: Jared Henderson, Teddy Hodges, Sherrie Ohrn, Steven Shields

Staff Present: City Manager Nathan Cherpeski, Assistant City Manager Wendy Thomas,
City Recorder Jackie Nostrom, Finance Director Kyle Maurer, City Attorney Todd Sheeran,
Communications Manager Jonathan LaFollette, City Planner Michael Maloy, Public Works
Director Justun Edwards, Community Development Director Blake Thomas, Police Chief
Troy Carr, City Engineer Bryce Terry, Management Analyst Trevor Ram.

9:00 AM – WORK MEETING: (Fort Herriman Conference Room)
1. Council Business
Mayor Lorin Palmer called the meeting to order at 9:03 a.m.

2. Administrative Reports
    2.1. Roundtable discussion of the proposed Southwest Athletic Complex
Mayor Palmer indicated the purpose of the meeting was to gather feedback, as not all
members of Council were able to attend the community meetings. He emphasized the
importance of reviewing the feedback together in a public meeting before deciding on the
February 4, 2025 Special City Council Work Meeting Minutes                            Page 2 of 9


      next steps. Assistant City Manager Thomas outlined the plan to start by reviewing public
      comments, followed by a presentation from Management Analyst Trevor Ram, who would
      run models related to economic development and housing.

      Assistant City Manager Thomas explained that she distributed packets of materials to the
      Council, including planning notes, sign-in sheets, and written comment forms from the first
      community meeting. This meeting focused on the 5600 West property and 12600 South
      property, where she mentioned concerns raised by attendees, particularly from the Miller
      Crossing Pod 1 neighborhood, which was located west of the fire station. One key concern
      was the unfinished precast wall, which had been started but left incomplete, resulting in a
      mix of precast pillars with vinyl fencing.

      Councilmember Sherrie Ohrn asked whether the precast wall issue had been investigated,
      as someone had indicated it was part of an agreement. Community Development Director
      Blake Thomas clarified that the agreement did not require the precast wall, though the
      Planning Commission had requested it, and the plans submitted were in line with what was
      built, columns with vinyl fencing.

      Assistant City Manager Thomas then highlighted that the Miller Crossing neighborhood had
      requested fewer lots, larger setbacks, and single-story homes for the 12600 South property.
      Concerns from the 5600 West property area included noise from the nearby fire station, as
      well as concerns about sales tax, property tax, density, traffic, crime, and community
      impact. Quality of life issues were frequently mentioned across all three open houses,
      especially regarding density and traffic. Many residents, particularly those near open
      spaces, wanted to maintain the area as it was.

      Councilmember Steve Shields asked for clarification on what "quality of life" meant,
      questioning whether it referred to opposition to housing types, changes to open spaces, or
      traffic impacts. He sought to better understand the specific concerns, wondering if these
      issues applied to all parcels or just certain ones. Assistant City Manager Thomas noted that
      people near agricultural properties were particularly concerned, fearing that higher density
      might spread further into the area. Councilmember Ohrn mentioned that the new proposal
      was much denser than anything else in the area. She brought up the differing definitions of
      "high density" shared by community members, noting that one individual opposed high-
      density housing like apartments, duplexes, and townhomes, but considered detached single-
      family homes acceptable. Assistant City Manager Thomas confirmed this was indeed the
      individual’s interpretation, and the Council acknowledged that these definitions often vary
      based on personal perspectives.

      Councilmember Shields reiterated the need for clarity on what "quality of life" meant for
      residents. He wanted to ensure that the concerns about density, changes to open space,
      traffic impacts, and housing types were clearly articulated. He also pointed out that
      perspectives on quality of life and traffic often depend on where people live or their life
      stage. For example, residents who moved from urban areas like Portland appreciated the
      lack of traffic, while long-time locals felt the traffic had worsened significantly over the
      years. Councilmember Teddy Hodges offered a perspective that emphasized how different
      types of communities’ appeal to different people, with walkable communities offering
      amenities that some residents prefer. He also mentioned that the discussion around traffic
      impact was central, as it directly affected residents’ day-to-day lives.

      Assistant City Manager Thomas addressed feedback from the 5600 West property (referred
      to as the Walker parcel). The community raised concerns about the density of the proposed
      development, specifically the 212 units. Traffic on 5600 West was identified as a major
      concern since it was already a well-traveled road. Residents also asked for alternative
      solutions to mitigate the negative impacts of the development, particularly in terms of
      traffic and overall community disruption. There were additional concerns about the
      negative community impact from development, and requests for a timeline on the project,
      with assurances that the new development would not lead to transient housing, such as
      apartments.

      Councilmember Ohrn stressed the importance of addressing community concerns early on
      and finding ways to mitigate potential issues. She emphasized that community input was
      crucial to ensuring the development works for everyone and help avoid long-term
      problems. Community Development Director Blake Thomas shared the preliminary traffic
      analysis, which indicated that while the development would increase traffic, it would not
      exceed the current road capacity. However, he acknowledged that a more detailed traffic
      study, including peak hour analysis, would be necessary. He noted the detailed study would
      be required as part of the subdivision approval process.

      Councilmember Shields indicated that as the council reviews individual parcels, it would be
      essential to look at the specific concerns associated with each one, understanding that each
      area may have unique issues that need to be addressed. He stressed that articulating the
      concerns would help make informed decisions and ensure that the development aligns with
      the community’s needs.

      The Council further discussed the issue of road congestion versus road capacity,
      particularly in relation to the proposed developments and traffic impacts. City Manager
      Nathan Cherpeski emphasized that when residents claim a road is "failing," it might be due
      to congestion, but engineering standards might still show that the road has the capacity to
February 4, 2025 Special City Council Work Meeting Minutes                                 Page 4 of 9


      carry traffic. The Council discussed the issue of road standards and questioned what level
      of service (such as Level D or E) was considered acceptable. City Manager Cherpeski
      clarified that while the City could collect traffic impact fees to improve roads, the City's
      ability to set a specific standard, such as aiming for Level C, would require taxpayer
      funding. Director Thomas explained that roads near schools often experience peak-hour
      congestion, particularly during school pick-up and drop-off times. He noted that despite
      past efforts by city engineers, school traffic is a persistent issue that cannot be fully solved.
      The road itself might function well overall, but peak times cause temporary delays. The
      Council agreed that while this congestion was an issue during certain hours, it did not
      necessarily mean the road has reached its full capacity—it's more about peak-hour delays
      versus overall road function.

      Assistant City Manager Thomas and Councilmember Ohrn discussed how this issue was
      perceived by residents, noting that while peak-hour congestion feels like a failure to those
      stuck in traffic, the average daily usage of the road still falls within its capacity. They also
      pointed out that delays around peak times are common frustrations for residents.

      City Engineer Bryce Terry reiterated the point that traffic planning focuses on average
      daily traffic, not just peak hours. While there will always be delays during busy times, a road
      is only considered to have "failed" when it exceeds its designed capacity.

      The Council turned the conversation to concerns raised by residents about property taxes
      and density. Councilmember Ohrn responded to a comment from a resident who preferred
      higher property taxes to increased density. She explained that it was not feasible to rely on
      higher property taxes to solve issues like congestion or funding infrastructure
      improvements, as taxing residents excessively could price people out of the area. She
      stressed the importance of finding more reasonable solutions to challenges.

      Councilmember Ohrn added that some residents believed the City was focused solely on
      money, but she clarified that the need to fund infrastructure was a real concern. She also
      pointed out that the City needed to provide more local recreational opportunities for
      children rather than relying on neighboring communities. This, she argued, would not only
      contribute to a more sustainable local economy but also improve the quality of life for
      families. Councilmember Shields further explained that by building facilities within the City,
      such as recreation and educational centers, the City could potentially reduce the traffic
      burden on major roads, as people would have less need to travel outside their local area for
      these services. He noted that while this was a theoretical benefit, it should be part of the
      overall planning conversation.

      Councilmember Jared Henderson pointed out that many of the issues related to this
      property, such as concerns over a wall that was required by the Planning Commission but
      not included in the agreement, could be addressed through the Planning Commission
      process. Director Thomas clarified that the Planning Commission could recommend certain
      conditions, like the wall, but it wasn’t part of the initial agreement, and the Commission did
      not have the authority to enforce it after the agreement was approved.

      The Council shared their perspectives on the broader issue of building more units in the
      City. They agreed that the discussion wasn't solely about increasing the number of
      residential units or building a park but involved larger, more complex issues, including
      infrastructure needs and the impact of such developments on the city's overall character.

      Assistant City Manager Thomas then shifted the discussion to the Creek Ridge
      neighborhood. She shared that there had been a sizable turnout at the open house with
      concerns expressed by Creek Ridge residents which included the expectation that the area
      would be a park, as originally planned, with some feeling they had paid more for their lots
      due to the park’s proximity. Many residents preferred a passive park to an athletic complex,
      citing a desire for nature and wildlife, and if the complex were to go in, they requested a
      buffer—such as a smaller park or a trail system—between the complex and their
      neighborhood. Additionally, there were concerns about light pollution, noise, density, and
      traffic, similar to the concerns raised by other residents.

      Councilmember Shields and Councilmember Ohrn acknowledged these concerns, noting
      that the residents’ understanding of the park’s purpose had shifted. Originally, they had
      anticipated a park with more natural features, but the athletic complex was not the same
      kind of development. The Council discussed how to address the residents' concerns,
      particularly around preserving the character of the neighborhood and mitigating negative
      impacts from the athletic complex. Councilmember Ohrn expressed her concern about the
      lack of space for kids to play, particularly in areas like Creek Ridge, where homes are built
      closely together, making it feel intimidating. She wondered where children in these areas
      could go to play, especially if the athletic complex replaced the park. Assistant City
      Manager Thomas pointed out that parts of the City hadn't provided good outlets for kids,
      and this issue needed to be addressed. Councilmember Henderson clarified that there were
      existing agreements to improve park spaces; however, some of the park areas discussed
      were designed for commercial use and had already been earmarked for specific
      developments. He highlighted that there would be a trail which could provide some
      recreational space, and that part of the agreement with developers involved making sure
      certain areas were set aside for park space. Councilmember Shields acknowledged that
      while the athletic complex might meet specific needs, the lack of a truly open and accessible
February 4, 2025 Special City Council Work Meeting Minutes                             Page 6 of 9


      park would remain an issue for the community, especially for those who hoped to see more
      open recreational spaces.

      Councilmember Teddy Hodges indicated that homeowners may have been sold land with
      the promise of a park; however, as developments move forward, this park space might not
      be exactly as envisioned by the buyers, which creates frustration. Councilmember Shields
      mentioned how developers sometimes present idealized images that don't match the reality
      of what gets built. Councilmember Ohrn agreed, explaining that people were led to believe
      there would be a park in the area when, in reality, things had changed. She also pointed out
      that, though things had changed, it wasn't necessarily a guarantee that a park would ever
      come to fruition, which further complicated the situation for residents’ expectations.

      City Manager Cherpeski provided some clarifying details about the land. When the
      development was originally approved in 2015, it was understood that not all of the 40 acres
      would be usable park space. He explained that much of the land, including areas under
      power corridors and drainage spaces, was not usable for a traditional park, and the total
      usable park space was closer to 28 acres. He emphasized that the expectation of a full 40-
      acre park was never realistic. Mayor Palmer shared that while the residents understood the
      park might take time to build, they were willing to wait rather than see the land developed
      for houses. City Manager Cherpeski clarified that the proposal for homes was for
      properties similar to Copper Fields, with matching density.

      One specific issue raised, was about people parking in the neighborhood and walking
      through it to access the athletic complex. Councilmember Ohrn emphasized that it was
      important to avoid creating issues like parking congestion or unwanted visitors wandering
      through the neighborhoods. She also proposed using park hours and limiting amplification
      to mitigate noise, understanding that while noise may still be an issue, certain steps can be
      taken to reduce impact. Councilmember Ohrn further suggested considering the potential
      for shuttle services from nearby locations like the high school to manage parking overflow.
      The Council agreed.

      Mayor Palmer acknowledged that these decisions are challenging and highlight the need for
      careful consideration. At the heart of the discussion is the recognition that the City must
      strike a delicate balance between preserving the quality of life for current residents and
      planning for future growth and sustainability. Councilmember Shields emphasized the
      difficulty in addressing residents' concerns. He raised the question of how to reassure
      residents that their concerns were being heard, especially when the decisions made might
      not fully alleviate their issues. He expressed the need for transparent communication and
      for explaining the reasoning behind decisions, even if those decisions didn’t meet
      everyone’s expectations. He highlighted the importance of acknowledging conflicting
February 4, 2025 Special City Council Work Meeting Minutes                               Page 7 of 9


      priorities, such as preserving quality of life while promoting growth, which would inevitably
      lead to some people being disappointed.

      Councilmember Henderson acknowledged that it was challenging to meet everyone’s needs
      and that emotions and feelings often complicate communication. He noted that the group’s
      role was to listen to concerns, explain decisions, and make compromises when possible.
      However, he recognized that no decision could make everyone happy, and some residents
      would inevitably feel that their issues weren’t fully addressed. Councilmember Hodges
      agreed.

      Management Analyst Trevor Ram discussed potential revenue generation from new hotels.
      He explained that the complex could position the City as a destination for regional
      tournaments, increasing demand for goods and services, particularly hotel stays. He
      projected that the addition of two hotels in the City, with or without the complex, could
      generate increased tax revenue, especially during tournament weekends. Analyst Ram
      emphasized that these numbers were based on assumptions and noted that the timing of
      hotel construction was uncertain. Councilmember Henderson added that the City might
      attract more hotels even without the complex, due to other commercial interests, which
      would increase the revenue generated from those businesses.

      Councilmember Shields highlighted that the baseline model for hotel revenue was solid but
      raised concerns about the feasibility of maintaining a consistent occupancy rate. He pointed
      out that hotels typically need to operate at around 70–80% occupancy to be financially
      viable, which could be challenging without enough demand. He noted that while a significant
      portion of hotel revenue comes from occupancy, the real economic impact also involved
      secondary spending at local businesses like restaurants and convenience stores, which
      would generate additional tax revenue.

      Director Thomas highlighted the impact of sales tax revenue from hotels and commercial
      developments, suggesting that while hotels brought in revenue from transient guests, the
      property tax structure differs compared to residential developments like apartment
      buildings. The conversation delved into the cost of providing services, especially police
      services, which could see an increase in demand from both hotels and apartments. Police
      Chief Troy Carr noted that apartment complexes in the City generated around 7-8% of the
      police service calls, which led him to estimate that hotels might contribute a similar increase
      in calls for service, potentially 8-10%. However, he acknowledged that growth in a city that
      is still expanding presented a different challenge compared to more stable communities.

      Councilmember Shields raised concerns about the lack of clear, realistic predictions about
      the long-term economic impact of new developments, questioning whether the additional
February 4, 2025 Special City Council Work Meeting Minutes                               Page 8 of 9


      revenue from a hotel or commercial development would be enough to justify the increased
      service costs. He emphasized that it would be irresponsible to approve projects based
      solely on hope for future tax revenue. Councilmember Ohrn questioned whether hotels
      would generate enough additional tax revenue to cover the extra cost of services, especially
      compared to apartments, which could have long-term residential service costs. She also
      pointed out the need for more comprehensive city planning, noting that the commercial
      footprint of areas like Olympia Hills is small, and while developments like hotels may
      replace certain residential projects, they could contribute significantly to the city’s revenue
      over time.

      Councilmember Shields raised concerns about the challenges of dealing with the impacts of
      urban growth, noting that every new house, road, or commercial development changes the
      City's dynamics. He emphasized that such decisions cannot be made in a vacuum, as every
      new development has a ripple effect on city services and the quality of life. Councilmember
      Ohrn highlighted the complexities of balancing property tax revenue from developments
      like apartment complexes, which increase the demand for services, compared to other
      developments such as hotels, which might bring in more sales tax but also increase service
      calls.

      The discussion also touched on the need for more specific data, especially regarding the
      regional impact of new developments. Teddy Hodges mentioned that residents had been
      asking for clearer data, and Mayor Palmer indicated that the next steps would involve
      negotiating with property owners. Steve Shields stressed the importance of knowing
      exactly what the public wanted and how much room there was to negotiate before diving
      into speculative discussions about future developments.

      Assistant City Manager Thomas pointed out that the next step would likely involve
      discussions of operations, maintenance, and negotiations with property owners. Mayor
      Palmer concluded that once all the facts were reviewed, the Council would come back to
      the public with the next steps.
