30   2) Conflict Resolution Training
31
32   Dr. Danya Rumore introduced herself. She is a professor at the University of Utah with a Ph.D.
33   in city planning.
34
35   She shared her outline of the trainings she would be doing. She planned on discussing what
36   conflict is, what conflict competence is, and then introducing and practicing three basic skills to
37   navigate conflict, namely understanding interests vs. positions, effective listening, and effective
38   framing and reframing.
39
40   She began the training with an exercise where pairs of attendees thumb-wrestled each other. This
41   took place over two separate ‘rounds’ the first of which was conducted in silence, and the second
42   of which allowed competitors to talk and strategize beforehand. After hearing about experiences
43   during the exercise, she shared that she had some unique responses from the group, and that she
44   almost always has a variety of different responses regardless of what group she does the exercise
45   with. She discussed how the exercise is meant to show how conflict stems from interdependence
46   on each other and differences in concerns and needs within that interdependence. People often
47   have false assumptions that conflict is a zero-sum game, but it rarely is. Communicating and
48   implementing an effective joint strategy is the way to create mutual gains. Rumore explained that

49   talking is different than communication. For communication to happen, parties must have
50   confidence in each other to follow through and also be able to look out for their own interests.
51
52   Rumore defined conflict as the intersection of different needs, wants, ideas, and concerns that are
53   in tension with each other and not easily reconciled. She discussed the root of the word
54   ‘collaboration’ being ‘co-labor,’ meaning to work together to make conflict productive and
55   rooted in interest-based negotiation. Conflict competence is the ability to deal with conflict
56   skillfully. It isn’t just being nice, compromising, disagreeing better, or sitting down shutting up
57   and getting along. Rumore discussed positionality, and how it can make conflict worse as the
58   fundamental issues aren’t being addressed. Conflict competence comes from the ability to focus
59   on the interests of the parties (also known as the ‘why,’ or the fundamental needs) rather than on
60   positions (also known as the ‘what’ or ‘how,’ and strategies). Interests can be procedural,
61   substantive, and emotional/relational. She explained that at the end of the day, most people just
62   want to be heard and considered.
63
64   Rumore conducted an exercise where individuals thought about a conflict in their life in order to
65   identify the interests at play in that conflict. She discussed the importance of finding the core
66   drivers of conflict. She mentioned the ‘nine whys’ method, which encourages people to continue
67   asking why until they can arrive at the root of something. She explained that values and identity
68   can cause instinctual responses that perpetuate conflict, rather than ones that are helpful in its
69   resolution.
70
71   Rumore shared some advice for identifying and focusing on interests in conflict. It is important
72   to be patient and practice, as it is a radical paradigm shift for many people. Understanding
73   interests is like peeling back layers of an onion, and it is a philosophy that is applicable to all
74   aspects of life. Effective listening is a vital skill to have.
75
76   Effective listening means truly listening to understand what others are trying to communicate. It
77   is a core skill for working through conflict, collaborating, and coexisting well with others.
78   Curiosity is a big help in developing this skill—and open mindset and a willingness to
79   understand are prerequisites for effective listening. It is not something that happens naturally, it
80   is a choice and it takes work. Some tips for effective listening are: focus on truly trying to
81   understand, practice curiosity, be attentive, be inviting and avoid judging, clarify what you’re
82   hearing, confirm or summarize what you have heard and revise as needed, and pause to create
83   space.
84
85   Commissioner Eyre asked how to avoid sounding robotic and practiced when confirming or
86   summarizing what has been said. Rumore recommends inviting questioning and emphasizing
87   genuine interest in the situation.
88
89   An effective question is one that helps you truly understand what the other person is trying to
90   communicate. Open-ended, non-leading questions are the best way to accomplish this. Rumore
91   discussed how being heard and understood is a fundamental human need. People typically
92   become better listeners once they have expressed their feelings and concerns. Often people don’t
93   listen because they don’t feel they have been heard. She recommends seeking first to understand
94   before being understood.
95

 96   Rumore finished her presentation by explaining that effective listening takes practice. It’s
 97   important to set yourself and others up for success by managing dysregulation and taking
 98   responsibility for making a good space for listening. She emphasized that when people feel like
 99   their identities are on the line, communication falls apart.
100
101   Rumore gave the Commission a listening self-assessment worksheet, as well as instructions to
102   review the slides and handouts before the next meeting on October 2nd.
103
104   Commissioner Kunze asked about responding to nonverbal communications. Rumore
105   recommends being kind and firm and not being the person who lobs aggression first. She also
106   recommends taking a break from the message and coming back to formulate a response.
