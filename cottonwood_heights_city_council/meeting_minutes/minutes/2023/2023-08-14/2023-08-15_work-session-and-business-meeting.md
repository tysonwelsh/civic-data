MINUTES OF THE COTTONWOOD HEIGHTS CITY COUNCIL WORK SESSION HELD TUESDAY, AUGUST 15, 2023, AT 4:00 P.M. IN THE COTTONWOOD HEIGHTS CITY COUNCIL WORK ROOM LOCATED AT 2277 EAST BENGAL BOULEVARD, COTTONWOOD HEIGHTS, UTAH

Members Present:  	Mayor Mike Weichers, Council Member Scott Bracken, Council Member Shawn E. Newell, Council Member Matt Holton, Council Member Ellen Birrell

Staff Present:	City Manager, Tim Tingey; City Attorney, Shane Topham; Records, Culture, and Human Resources Director, Paula Melgar; Community and Economic Development Director, Michael Johnson; Police Chief, Robby Russo; Administrative and Financial Services Director, Scott Jurges; Systems Administrator, Alex Earl; Public Works Director/City Engineer, Matt Shipp 

WELCOME – Mayor Weichers.

Mayor Mike Weichers called the meeting to order at 4:00 p.m. and welcomed those present. 

REVIEW OF BUSINESS MEETING AGENDA – Mayor Mike Weichers.

The Business Meeting Agenda items were reviewed and discussed.     

The first action item was consideration of Resolution 2023-44 Approving and Ratifying a Bid and Awarding a Construction Contract for the Storm Drain Access Restoration Project.  Public Works Director/City Engineer, Matt Shipp, reported that the contract is to raze manholes.  There were issues last fiscal year obtaining bids.  They were ultimately able to get a legitimate bid from a contractor to raze approximately 250 manholes.  Once complete they will begin installing cameras, cleaning pipes, and analyzing what is there.  The project is from last fiscal year with the funding having been carried over.  It was clarified that the contract is only for the cost of razing the manholes.  The work was expected to be completed in a few months. 

The second action item involved consideration of Resolution 2023-45 Awarding a Retired Police Officer his Badge and Approving his Purchase of a Police Firearm.  It was reported that Officer Potter will be retiring.  Police Chief, Robby Russo indicated that Officer Potter worked with him at the Sheriff’s Office and was assigned to this precinct for his first five years as a Rookie.  He has spent the last 15 years serving in Cottonwood Heights.  He has hit his 20-year mark and after one year he may come back into service.  As is customary, it was recommended that his badge be awarded to him in recognition of his dedicated service.  It will be done at a farewell in September.  Mayor Weichers reported that Officer Potter has served as a School Resource Officer for many years and is beloved by many youth in the City.  He was sad to see him go.  

The Council will receive public comment on the post-Sensitive Lands Evaluation Development Standards (“SLEDS”) ordinance update.  Community and Economic Development Director, Michael Johnson, stated that this will be the second continued public hearing for the draft ordinance that has been published.  The Council received recommended changes from one resident and another in the last hour that would be forwarded to the City Council.  The first round of comments did not seem substantive and most can be accommodated.  

Mr. Johnson reported that a comment was made at the last meeting asking that the Design Review Committee (“DRC”) take minutes for each meeting and create an Executive Summary of the minutes, which staff did not support.  The thought was that if minutes are taken they will encapsulate what was discussed at the meeting.  Preparing an Executive Summary seemed unnecessary.  Mr. Johnson stated that currently there is an internal system for taking notes and documenting meetings.  Sensitive Lands DRC meetings usually result in a Pre-Scoping or a Land Disturbance Permit, which is already documented and includes numerous conditions.  Documents that are part of the public record are reviewed by the City Engineer or GeoStrata Engineering.  There is also a recording or a written summary of the meeting.  The proposed ordinance specifies that the DRC shall take summary notes, minutes, or recordings of each meeting.  The ordinance does not include verbiage specifying that minutes must be posted within a certain period of time.  It was noted that the record is always available.

Council Member Birrell felt it was important for the public to be able to access minutes and have a specified time frame to avoid having to file a Government Records and Management Act (“GRAMA”) request to access the information.  She suggested that language be added to specify that minutes will be posted within a reasonable time to make things more transparent.  City Manager, Tim Tingey, stated that preparing minutes for all internal City meetings will significantly impact efficiency and effectiveness.  Those interested in obtaining information can do so by filing a GRAMA request.  He was concerned about micromanaging the City’s processes beyond that.  

Records, Culture, and Human Resources Director, Paula Melgar, stated that she is sworn to provide the documents that are requested.  Having a request allows them to follow a paper trail and protects the City against lawsuits.  Mr. Johnson stated that if it takes less than 15 minutes to provide the requested information there is no fee.  Council Member Bracken had concerns with people wanting to anonymously submit GRAMA requests and asked if there was a provision regarding a Private Record Request.  Ms. Melgar stated that if she is asked to provide a public record for 2023 she can provide it and will not be able to divulge any public information on the person making the request.  

Council Member Birrell did not understand the additional staff time necessary to make the information public.  Council Member Newell commented that it seems that all of the parameters for receiving public information exist.  He did not see the need to add another step or process and burden staff.  He had not heard concerns from his constituents about issues obtaining information.  He stated that the system is very transparent.   In terms of receiving a name, he stressed the importance of having backup records showing who made and fulfilled the request.  

Mr. Johnson stated that there will likely be an updated draft with cleanup language incorporated.  He clarified that a public comment was made at the last meeting that there is still language that prevents tree replacement, which was not read properly.  After the meeting, staff spoke to the resident and cleared up the issue.  

STAFF REPORTS

Cottonwood Heights Business Association/ South Valley Chamber Discussion – City Manager Tim Tingey and Community and Economic Development Director Mike Johnson.

Mr. Johnson reported that they were approached by the South Valley Chamber of Commerce who asked the City to join as a municipal member.  Several meetings were held and the matter was brought preliminarily before the Cottonwood Heights Business Association (“CHBA”).  The intent was to get Council input.  The South Valley Chamber adds to what the CHBA is doing to provide a larger business network.  Mayor Weichers reported that currently, the South Valley Chamber includes the cities of Sandy, Draper, South Jordan, and Riverton.  

Mr. Tingey was supportive of chambers and associations and believed that for individuals and businesses to build relationships is incredibly valuable.  Holistically he was in favor of the City’s participation.  Mr. Johnson clarified that it is not part of the current budget and the City joining does not automatically enroll City businesses.  They can join currently but may get a discounted rate if the City is a member.  It was noted that packages start at $500 per business.  Currently, without ribbon cutting services, it is a three-year contract proposal starting at $20,000 for the first year, $25,000 for the second year, and $30,000 for the third year. 

Mr. Tingey reported that if the Council would like to proceed, staff will bring back a Business Services Agreement that will be incorporated into a Budget Amendment.  Council Member Birrell asked if City business owners have been surveyed as to whether this is something they desire.  Mr. Johnson stated that an email was sent out but there has not been much response.  He expected to see interest from medium to large City businesses.  Council Member Birrell asked what the advantage would be for the City and local business for the City to join.  Mr. Tingey explained that it would give staff and potentially a Council Member a seat at the table to help shape policies and decisions the Chamber is making in a way that can benefit City businesses.  Staff also sent a few small businesses to business startup training programs that are sponsored by the South Valley Chamber of Commerce.  

Mr. Tingey commented that chambers were very active when the non-compete legislation was running.  Companies in the state were locking down employees so that they could not go work elsewhere.  That was something they were very active with that made a big difference.  In his opinion, it is less relevant with respect to what the City specifically gains other than networking, and is about showing businesses that it is a priority. 

Mr. Tingey reported that the Contract Agreement addresses the services they provide.  They would act as a liaison between the City and the business community, promote and support businesses, act as a repository of business resources, provide networking opportunities, and monitor state legislative issues.  They also assist with the Business Retention Program.  

Council Member Bracken suggested that instead of the City signing up, that the money be used to subsidize City business memberships.  Council Member Birrell supported that idea and supported helping small businesses.  

Mayor Weichers had studied the issue and met on multiple occasions with representatives from the South Valley Chamber of Commerce.  He felt that it shows a commitment to businesses in the City to belong.  He considered it to be a bigger commitment to businesses in Cottonwood Heights.  He felt that it communicated to the business community that the City is interested in helping with networking and that they would pay less than if the City was not a member.  

Council Member Newell reported that he is an active member of the Chamber of Commerce.  One of the benefits is that economic development allows them to attract business opportunities.  

Mr. Tingey stated that staff will meet with the CHBA the following week and get feedback.  He did not consider the City joining the Chamber as taking over what the CHBA does as they are focused on small business outreach.  The consensus of the Council was to bring the matter back after the CHBA Meeting.   

Design Options for Highland/Bengal Boulevard- Road Safety Improvements – Public Works Director/ City Engineer Matt Shipp.

Brandon Cloward was present from HW Lochner, the Consultant who prepared the City’s roundabout design.  Mr. Cloward was the Lead Engineer on that project.  Mr. Shipp stated that the City has a good working relationship with Lochner and invited them back for this project.  

Mr. Shipp reported that there are three projects involved in the project.  Two are federal aid projects and one is County funded.  Mr. Cloward and his team were asked to work on the preliminary engineering for all three projects to ensure that they are cohesive.  They are currently doing a 30 to 35 percent design.  The required Environmental Study for the federal project has been completed and signed off on.  There are two federal aid projects.  The original involved the intersection of Highland Drive and Bengal Boulevard, which was intended to improve the efficiency of the intersection and the safety of pedestrian and bicycle safety.  The other project involved adding a multi-use trail on the north side of Bengal Boulevard through the intersection.  

Some of the goals of the project included the following:

Improve the efficiency of the intersection at Highland Drive and Bengal Boulevard; 
Improve the safety of pedestrians and bicyclists through the intersection; 
Improve safety for bicyclists along Bengal Boulevard;
Connect the path from Bengal Hills Cove to the federal project; and 
Provide traffic calming on Bengal Boulevard through the corridor. 

In response to a question raised by Council Member Birrell, Mr. Shipp explained that the intent is to construct a multi-use trail along the north side and improve the bicycle lanes on Bengal Boulevard.  It was noted that the bike lane will be in the roadway.  The preliminary layout was presented.  

Council Member Birrell commented on behalf of cyclists and stated that many who use Bengal Boulevard want to turn left and go south on Highland, which can be extremely dangerous.  

Mr. Shipp reviewed the various sections.  On Highland Drive on Wednesdays, there is a line of garbage cans on the sidewalk and road.  The area shown in pink on the rendering is owned by the City.  It was proposed that they build a turnout lane to improve safety for residents coming onto Highland Drive.  A multi-use trail will also be constructed that serves as a gateway to City Hall.  Mail carriers can also use the turnout lane area.  Mr. Shipp stated that the Utah Transit Authority (“UTA”) is reluctant to pull off of the road but they may be able to work with them to relocate the bus stop.  It was noted that the bike lane will add a nice aesthetic.  

Mr. Shipp next addressed the intersection of Highland Drive and Bengal Boulevard.  One of the goals was to improve efficiency with one issue being traffic that backs up the hill during peak times.  The original proposal was to add a double left turn lane onto Highland Drive to allow traffic to make the double left.  The onramp onto Highland Drive will remain going north and a turn was added to allow vehicles to pull out.  Turning left on Highland Drive was an issue they could continue to explore.

Mr. Cloward reported that they coordinated with UTA and recommended that a problematic bus stop be moved to the far side of the intersection.  They were supportive of the proposed change and requested that it be elongated to allow them to install a shelter.  Other changes to improve traffic circulation were discussed.  Mr. Cloward explained that one of the goals was to reduce the overall design speed to 20 MPH.  The two lanes going up Bengal Boulevard followed the new design speed adopted by the City Council last year.  As a result, they were able to change the lane configuration aesthetic.  It was noted that the lanes will be 11 feet wide.  Traffic calming and circulation issues were discussed.  

Council Member Birrell pointed out that Mr. Shipp represents Cottonwood Heights on the SS4A Committee for the Wasatch Front Regional Council (“WFRC”) and asked if SS4A is a safe system.  She hoped they could get away from radii that are built for fire trucks.  Mr. Shipp explained that it is a design consideration and a schematic drawing.  They do, however, have to take turning radii into consideration.  It has to be large enough that they can turn larger vehicles around the corner.  

Mr. Cloward commented that because of the addition of the multi-use trail, the existing sidewalk on the north side of the road will be removed.  The Butler Hills intersection was identified and has the same concept.  It is intended to accommodate students.  They will eliminate the 40 feet and install a different type of crosswalk.  Feedback on the general concept was sought.  

Mayor Weichers felt that the proposed design accomplishes the goal of traffic calming.  He liked the aesthetic of medians and vegetation.  He expressed his support.  

Council Member Newell commented on the indentations and asked if there will be a parking deterrent there.  Mr. Shipp stated that they were built because of the nearby homes coming onto Bengal Boulevard which provides a buffer. 

Council Member Bracken suggested that it be presented to the school district as there could potentially be concerns with removing turn lanes to pick up students.  Mr. Shipp explained that because it is in a school zone, more can be done in terms of parking.  The issue could be examined in greater detail as they move forward through the process.  

Council Member Birrell wanted to encourage residents in the area to come across.  She recognized that people who live on the north side of Bengal Boulevard will support what is proposed while those on the south side may not be as supportive.  She did not understand the design facilitates for pedestrians or cyclists who are coming to Highland Drive to travel south.  Council Member Bracken stated that the most common direction pedestrians travel is down the sidewalk on the south side and straight across.  Making it a split space will likely increase student safety.  

Mr. Shipp confirmed that they will finalize the 30% design and bring back the final design contract, which will most likely be with HW Lochner.

HILLSIDE PLAZA LEASING DISCUSSION – City Manager, Tim Tingey and Community and Economic Development Director, Michael Johnson.

Mayor Weichers reported that the above matter involves whether the City has an interest in accepting a request to lease at Hillside Plaza. 

Mr. Johnson reported that the previous week Council Members Birrell and Newell visited the vacant spaces in the Central Plaza area.  Some were structurally intact if not dated.  Many spaces have remained unoccupied since before the City’s incorporation and have never been leased out.  Some spaces are restaurants or lower impact uses such as office spaces that are in varying conditions.  When due diligence was done for the Plaza, it was not performed to the level it would have been if the intention was to leave the strip mall and continue leasing space.  He was not aware of the condition of the utilities, water, and sewer, in those spaces.  The roof condition was also unknown and needed to be assessed.  The intention was to get through the Master Plan process and begin redevelopment as soon as possible.  Entertaining a lease on the site would likely be on a year-to-year basis.  The City has been contacted by people who are interested in the large spaces but they primarily want space that is ready to occupy.  The large spaces, in particular, are not currently in leasable condition.  When the City purchased the property, an analysis was not done as to whether consideration should be given to leasing the space.  Because there has been interest, staff thought the issue was worth bringing to the Community Development and Renewal Agency (“CDRA”).  The next step would be to engage a property broker to determine what types of businesses would be interested in relocating to the site for one year.  

Even if the structure of the buildings is good there will still be remodeling and renovation costs.  Potential occupants may not want to sign a year-to-year lease with the investment required.  It would also be necessary to look at the tax implications since it is owned by a public entity.  Any parcel that is occupied by a private use does not pay property taxes.  If tenants begin to occupy spaces that are currently non-taxed, the entire parcel will become taxable.  It will be necessary to weigh the costs and benefits of what the tax payment will be versus rent.  There had been talk of sharing uses in a portion of the parking lot for a Park and Ride ski traffic.  Potential uses of the parking lot as well as maintenance costs will need to be considered.  

If the CDRA is serious about leasing, they should meet with a contractor and assess the current condition of the leased spaces.  Maintenance and repairs will also need to be negotiated to bring them to a leasable state.  Mr. Johnson expected there to be challenges associated with attracting the type of businesses they want in the short term.  Mayor Weichers commented on the parking lot and liked the idea of generating income.  It could also be used for parking for resort employees.  

Council Member Holton questioned whether there should be an investment in something temporary and did not consider it to be a wise financial decision.  If, however, there are ways to use it without incurring additional costs and it is an increase for the City financially, he would not be opposed.  

Council Member Birrell commented that when they walked the property she did not see signs that would indicate major water problems exist.  Council Member Newell noted that there were signs of leakage.  With the two larger buildings that was one of the issues.  He was concerned about entering into Lease Agreements because the maintenance costs and the burden it puts on staff are of concern.  He did not see how it would work.  With regard to the parking lot, he had security concerns.  

Council Member Bracken commented that he has been negotiating a lease for the City space and understood the burden that creates.  In terms of the parking lot, he expected to be restricted to the middle area.  The east side is where businesses currently exist.  He reported that DPI was actually charging people to park there last year.  Mayor Weichers reported that the resorts allowed their employees to park there and travel together in a van up the canyon.  

Council Member Birrell referred to the area to the west of the old Reams Store where there is a clean and tidy area with tables and landscaping.  If the intent is to mitigate problems in the winter during peak times, parking could be made available for the ski resorts.  Cars stack on the roadways and could potentially be steered to the area where food could be served.  Her concern was that the providers are across Fort Union Boulevard, which includes four lanes of fast-moving traffic. If people park there while waiting for Big or Little Cottonwood Canyon to open, they could potentially get coffee or pastries. 

Mayor Weichers liked the thought process but was not sure it would be feasible.  Council Member Birrell stated that it would be through a private provider and would not be something she would expect the City to take on.  Council Member Bracken commented that potentially someone could buy a donut or coffee, get in a van, and be taken to the resort.  Council Member Holton was not opposed to a coffee or food truck in the parking lot.  He was not in favor, however, of doing anything in the actual structures.  

Mr. Shipp commented that often when a new use is proposed in a building, there are Building Code requirements that come into play.  Any new tenant would have to adhere to the current standards. 

CANYON CENTRE PARK DEVELOPMENT – City Manager, Tim Tingey and Community and Economic Development Director, Michael Johnson.

Mr. Johnson reported that the developer of the Canyon Centre, Chris McCandless, intends to proceed with an Amendment to his Development Agreement to extend the construction deadline for the office building and change the use of the office building to mixed office or retail with condominium units.  In exchange for an extension of the deadline, Mr. McCandless will forego some of his collections of the tax increment until the buildings are completed.  The park parcel will also be deeded to the City earlier than agreed upon in the agreement.  The City could begin designing the future park in the meantime.  The only caveat is that Mr. McCandless would retain the usage of the parcel for construction access.  The question was whether there was interest in utilizing some of the increment that has been received to begin the design work for the public park.  

Council Member Birrell commented that a portion of the property is very steep.  She was concerned that extensive excavation will be required.  She suggested that this be taken into account in the design.  Mr. Johnson referred to a large area at the intersection of Wasatch Boulevard and the Canyon Centre.  There is a retaining wall and the area is flat enough that it does not need to be retained.  He acknowledged, however, that it is very steep.  He reported that the portion shown in yellow is 1.11 acres in size

Council Member Birrell reported that area residents have had problems with trash cans and trying to control parking.  That is the area where Mr. McCandless wants his trucks to enter.  Originally, if he had been unable to keep his timeline, the trucks would enter across from the Weekley Townhomes.  Mr. Johnson stated that that will have to be negotiated.  Mr. McCandless is working with the Board as part of the Development Agreement.  Ultimately, however, the City will have the 1.11-acre parcel.  Park design can get underway but the park will not be constructed until Mr. McCandless no longer needs the access.  Mr. Johnson agreed that there are issues that need to be addressed.  Mr. Tingey reminded the Council that they are still a few years away from the tax increment being collected.  That time allows them to get a head start on designing the park and preparing for future development.  The cost of the design work was not expected to be exorbitant but will come out of the CDRA budget.  

Council Member Birrell stated that the residents she has spoken to are excited for the project to move forward.  The decision of the Council was to move forward with the design work.

Don Smart shared comments about pickleball in the community.  He stated that there are a lot of pickleball courts in neighborhoods.  His backyard connects to approximately six other homes, which means there is a potential for conflict.  Issues associated with pickleball include noise and the fact that lights are needed to utilize the courts in the mornings and evenings.  He understood that people want to do things on their property but with pickleball, it is not possible to contain the sound or lights.  The use becomes a neighborhood issue.  Mr. Smart asked the City Council to protect residents from sound and light nuisances.  There is a Homeowners Association (“HOA”) where he lives but the HOA wants to support what the City dictates.  

Dan Eagen reported that he resides in the Willow Brook Farm Subdivision on the south side of the City.  He also wanted to share comments about pickleball courts.  There is a void in the City Ordinances about this issue.  The placement of the facilities in private neighborhoods as opposed to public areas, such as parks and schools, deserves prompt and urgent attention from the City.  Noise and light penetrating beyond lot boundaries and onto neighboring properties is a real concern.  Those impacted are left to complain to neighbors or the neighborhood HOA.  This impacts the overall sense of community.  Currently, there are only the Salt Lake County noise rules to establish a baseline limitation.  Even a casual reading of the rule indicated that it did not contemplate outdoor private sports facilities or the repetitive nature of the sounds involved.  Mr. Eagen believed there needs to be better and more specific guidance in place.  There were examples that could be considered from other cities, such as Park City, which recently adopted an ordinance. Renee Cundick reported that her family decided to live in Cottonwood Heights because they loved the area.  She was concerned about the new trend where pickleball courts are added to quiet, private, residential areas.  Neighborhoods are being turned into recreational spaces.  There are rotating leagues, lessons, and hourly rentals taking place.  People travel in and out of neighborhoods for these purposes, which results in more noise, traffic, and other nuisances.  Over the last year, her family has been directly impacted by this.  She resides in an HOA and the Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (“CC&R”) state that all game courts need to be located in a way that will avoid impacting adjacent properties with light and sound.  It also states that no noxious or offensive activity should be carried out on the property. She and her husband both have home offices and work from home.  Light and sound generated from a Pickleball court in the residential neighborhood impacts their ability to work and rest.  

Ms. Cundick reported that noise from Pickleball courts consistently reaches 70 decibels.  However, the decibels greatly increase in enclosed spaces, echoing off of the close proximity of structures. She reiterated that Pickleball greatly exceeds that number.  After consulting several fence and sound professionals, there is no fence that will block or muffle the associated sounds.  The proximity of the homes causes the sound to echo off of the structures and amplify the noise.  This is clearly a nuisance to all neighbors and residents surrounding the private courts.  She commented that Cottonwood Heights needs to have clear ordinances in place to protect the existing neighborhoods.

Mark McDonald noted that the Park City Planning Commission recently approved a new Land Management Code Amendment related to outdoor pickleball courts in residential areas. Due to the unique noise produced through pickleball play, Staff recommended that the Planning Commission implement new Land Use regulations for outdoor pickleball courts in residential areas as a way to address noise and other impacts.  Mr. McDonald shared details of what was approved by the Park City Planning Commission.  He was upset that some neighbors in Cottonwood Heights will negatively impact others with private courts. PUBLIC COMMENT

5.1	The City Council will Receive Public Comment on the Proposed Sensitive Lands Evaluation and Development Standards (“SLEDS”) Ordinance – Introduction by Community and Economic Development Director, Michael Johnson.
 
Community and Economic Development Director, Michael Johnson, shared information about the proposed Sensitive Lands Evaluation and Development Standards (“SLEDS”) Ordinance.  He explained that the SLEDS Ordinance already exists but updates had been made.  The technical Staff review and update began in 2019.  The primary purpose was to update all standards to the highest and best practices.  There was also a desire to identify, study, and mitigate hazards in the City, particularly during new development activities.  For many of the provisions in the Ordinance, development activity is the trigger for the regulations.  The recommendations throughout the SLEDS Ordinance came from different experts.  Formal discussions and public hearings began in early 2022 at the Planning Commission level and continued throughout the year.  In August, the Planning Commission voted 4-to-3 to recommend approval to the City Council.  From there, the City Council started to discuss the proposed SLEDS Ordinance language.  

Numerous updates were made subsequent to the recommendation by the Planning Commission.  Those updates were based on Planning Commission feedback as well as continued discussions with members of the public and Town Hall Meetings.  Mr. Johnson reported that there was a well-attended Town Hall Meeting in February where there were discussions about riparian protection standards.  He reiterated that changes had been made since the Planning Commission vote.   

Mr. Johnson reported that one of the changes following the Planning Commission vote included clarification on the sensitive lands review procedure.  There was a slightly different review process initially proposed for sensitive lands scoping studies.  When development was about to occur, a piece of property needed to be scoped to determine whether there was a fault line, what risk the steep slope provided, and whether there was groundwater on the property.  There was language in the Ordinance to state how those areas need to be studied.  Changes were made but feedback indicated that the previous version was preferred, so it was changed back.  

Language was added to clarify that anywhere in the Ordinance that refers to the City or City Staff making a decision runs through the Development Review Committee (“DRC”).  The DRC was defined in the Ordinance and is a Committee that includes various Staff Members and Department Heads.  There was language added to state that the City Council will vote on the membership of that Committee at least once a year.  Minutes will be taken for any DRC Meeting or a recording will be available afterward for City Staff.  That documentation would be accessible to the public.  

The Riparian Protection Standards were updated and modified.  Mr. Johnson explained that the largest change came as the result of early public comments and the Town Hall Meeting held in February.  The modifications split the regulations into two categories.  The first category applied to residential properties, specifically low-density zones as well as the Residential Office Zone and the R-2 Zone.  A lot of the previous standards and restrictions for development in those areas were now recommendations.  There was another set of standards for commercial, high-density, and more intense development.  Those were areas where more dense and intense land uses were anticipated.  Essentially, there were advisory recommendations for residential areas with only a few restrictions, and standards for pending and future commercial redevelopment.  

The Ordinance contained language related to slope disturbance.  There was now a clearer definition of Wasatch Boulevard.  Mr. Johnson reported that the majority of the amendments made since February were related to the Riparian Protection Area language.  Public comments were received prior to the City Council Meeting and were forwarded to the City Council.  Some of the comments would be incorporated into another Ordinance update, however, he did not feel the changes would significantly alter the intent of the Ordinance.  Mayor Weichers informed those present that the Utah League of Cities and Towns (“ULCT”) shared Legislative updates recently.  Something that the Legislature was working on was to require cities to pass riparian-related ordinances.  That was something that could potentially come from the State in the future.  

Mayor Weichers opened the public comment period.

David Berry reported that he previously submitted comments in writing.  He found a paragraph that he disagreed with pertaining to the definition of the creek banks.  If the creek comes through the bank and erodes out 10 feet, the way the statute is written, the eroded-out section would become the new creek bank.  He believed it needed to be based on the historical creek bank instead.  Mr. Berry also pointed out that there were references to Paragraph 3A.  However, 3A included an approval process, so that was not necessarily allowed.  Something was either allowed or not allowed.  If there is an approval process, it is not allowed.  Mr. Berry believed that the final draft should be circulated once more for additional feedback and clarifications. 

Dale Harris thanked the City for their work and Mr. Berry for his efforts.  If he had to vote for the riparian ordinance currently, he would not vote in favor.  He referenced fires that occurred and a close call with his home.  The ordinance had a tone that indicated that property owners are the hazard.  There was language that addressed property owners restricting their movements, which decreased their ability to respond to serious and significant hazards like fire and flood.  The biomass needed to be managed.  It was a fuel source that had to be handled appropriately.  Mr. Harris did not believe the Ordinance addressed flood or fire, which were two of the most significant hazards facing the community.  He asked that those hazards be clearly addressed in the Ordinance language.  Mr. Harris expressed concerns that the Ordinance would lead to more hazards.  

Mark Johnson thanked Council Member Bracken for visiting his property several months ago to better understand the situation in the area.  He also thanked everyone involved in the SLEDS Ordinance process.  Mr. Johnson explained that if he wanted to update his house, he would need to go through an approval process.  It seemed that some of the language placed the rights of the community over the rights of private property owners, which was of concern.  He suggested that a clause be added to state that the homeowners’ rights will not be reduced.  

City Manager, Tim Tingey, reported that written public comments were submitted.  Those comments were part of the official record.  The comments were from Allan Packer, Mark Johnson, and Jennifer Follstad Shah.  All of the comments pertained to the SLEDS Ordinance.

STANDING QUARTERLY REPORTS

6.1	Monthly Financial Report – Finance and Administrative Services Director Scott Jurges.

Finance and Administrative Services Director, Scott Jurges, shared the Monthly Financial Report with the City Council.  He explained that this is the preliminary year-end financial update for the fiscal year that ended on June 30, 2023.  A graph related to property tax was shown.  Property taxes normally stay fairly stagnant but there had been a small bump in 2013, which represented catching up on delinquent taxes from the previous two years.  In fiscal year 2019, there was a property tax increase that accounted for the jump shown on the graph.  Taxes were fairly flat after that point.  The average was approximately $50,000 net property tax increase.  That came in the form of new taxes from either new structures or structure expansions in the City.  The 2023 budget was $8,184,829 and $8,185,000 was collected.  Mr. Jurges noted that $50,000 was added to the budget for next year.  That amount would be $8,224,000.  

Mr. Jurges shared a chart related to sales tax.  He explained that sales tax is one of the more volatile taxes.  From 2020 to 2021, it increased significantly.  That amount continued to increase from 2021 to 2022.  The June sales tax for the year had not yet been received but the City was currently sitting approximately $347,000 ahead of 2022.  Council Member Bracken wondered if it was anticipated that the 2022 number would be exceeded.  Mr. Jurges believed it would be but stated that it was difficult to determine as there could be some fluctuations.  He shared a chart related to the County Option Highway Transportation Sales Tax that was implemented in 2020.  It was fairly volatile as well.  Last year, $780,000 was received in taxes.  Currently, the number is at $722,000, which was approximately $19,000 ahead of last year for this time.  Final numbers were expected to come in shortly. 

Information about the Transient Room Tax was shared.  Mr. Jurges explained that this did not used to be a major source of revenue for the City.  For many years, under $50,000 was received.  However, another hotel was created, and that drastically increased the revenues.  Last year, $144,700 was received, and this year, the amount was approximately $34,000 ahead of the previous year.  He believed the finalized number would be approximately $175,000.

Mr. Jurges reviewed information related to Municipal Energy Tax.  He reported that this tax came from gas, natural gas, or power that was sold in the City.  When those entities changed their rate structures, it impacted the sales tax that was generated.  There has been a bit of revenue increase from 2020 on.  Some of that was the result of the rate adjustments that the energy companies had put in place.  Last year, the City received just under $2.2 million.  This year to date, the amount was a little over $2.5 million.  That was $381,000 ahead of what was collected last year.  Mr. Jurges pointed out that extremes in temperatures could impact the numbers as well.  

Cable Franchise Tax information was shared.  Mr. Jurges reported that the tax stayed fairly stable.  The amount was currently $8,000 behind what was collected in 2022.  If that held true to the end of the year, it would be somewhere in the $315,000 range, which he felt was a reasonable estimate.  Those taxes came in on a quarterly basis.  The budget for next year was $300,000.  

Mr. Jurges discussed the Class C Roads Funds.  He explained that those funds came from a cents per gallon fuel tax.  It was based on the quantity of fuel that was sold and distributed.  Some increases have been seen there over the last few years.  The number was approximately $1.4 million to date for the year, which was $40,000 ahead of last year.  The budget for next year was $1.4 million.  He thought that was a good representation of where the overall collections would be.

The General Fund Expense trend graph was shared.  Expenses were largely driven by salary costs, whether those costs were the result of salary increases or Cost-of-Living Adjustments (“COLA”).  There had been larger COLA increases over the last several years.  This year, the City was sitting at $20,214,000, which was approximately $1.3 million ahead of the prior year's spending.  That being said, Mr. Jurges reminded Council Members that the numbers were not finalized.  There would be a few more expenditures and revenues coming through.  The numbers should be mostly finalized within the next two weeks or so.  From there, some analysis would be done and the five-year plan would be updated accordingly.  The auditors would come in at the end of September.
