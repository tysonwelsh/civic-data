Manure Management, Storage Location and Design 
Manure will be collected daily from stalls, paddocks, and holding areas and transported to a

On-site land application will occur on the south ~six acres using a manure spreader and will only 
be conducted under suitable conditions and in appropriate amounts. Manure will not be applied 
during frozen ground conditions, saturated soils, active runoff, snowmelt conditions, or when 
application could result in runoff.

Off-site removal will occur using a dump trailer and/or dumpster to an approved disposal facility, 
(most likely Henefer Landfill) when land application is not appropriate and when storage 
capacity requires additional removal.   Removal will occur on a weekly or bi-weekly basis, 
depending on conditions. 
If manure volume approaches storage capacity or if conditions limit removal, hauling frequency 
will be increased. 
 
 
Winter Operations and Snow Management 
Winter operations will include continued daily manure collection and transfer to the storage 
area.  Land application will be reduced or paused during frozen or unsuitable ground conditions.  
Hauling frequency will be increased as needed. 
Snow will be stored in designated areas away from the manure storage area to prevent spring 
meltwater from contacting stored manure. 
 
Fly and Pest Control 
Fly control will be managed through an integrated approach focused on sanitation and removal 
frequency. 
Manure will be removed daily from animal areas and maintained in a managed storage pile. Fly 
pressure will be monitored, and removal frequency will be adjusted as needed. 
If necessary, a labeled larvicide may be applied in accordance with manufacturer instructions as 
a supplemental control measure. 
 
Dust Control 
Dust will be managed as needed during dry conditions. 
Control measures will include watering high-traffic areas such as arenas and equipment routes, 
maintaining appropriate surface conditions, using arena footing that is designed to minimize 
dust, managing vehicle speeds, and maintaining vegetative cover where feasible outside of 
traffic areas.

Background 
Blue Sky Lodge Subdivision consists of two lots and was recorded March 15, 2022. Blue Sky 
Ranch Subdivision consists of two lots and was recorded December 2, 2024. Parcel NS-86-A 
contains 3.55 acres. The applicant proposes to re-subdivide the aforementioned parcels. Based 
on the AG-80 zone district, the applicant is eligible to divide the property into twelve lots. The 
applicant proposes nine lots and 3 remainder parcels. Three of the nine lots are proposed for 
utility use; two for water wells and one for a wastewater treatment plant. Each of the lots is 
buildable. The following proposed lots have existing structures: Lot 102—High West Distillery, 
Lots 103 and 109 —wells for Blue Sky Ranch Subdivision public water system, Lot 104 – Sewer 
Treatment Plant for Blue Sky Ranch Resort Wastewater Treatment Plant, Lot 105—Blue Sky 
Lodge and Spa.

LANDSLIDE HAZARD
Landslide
Many Utah landslides are considered dormant, but recent slope failures are commonly 
reactivations of pre-existing landslides, suggesting that even so-called dormant landslides may 
continue to exhibit slow creep or are capable of renewed movement if stability thresholds are 
exceeded. Past slope failures can be used to identify the geologic, hydrologic, and 
topographic conditions that may reactivate existing landslides and initiate new landslides. 
Developing on landslides will always involve a level of irreducible, inherent risk. Landslides 
often impact more than one parcel or lot and attempting to stabilize a landslide on only one 
parcel or lot is rarely successful. The map below shows mapped landslides for your area of 
interest and the boundary of previously mapped areas from the Utah Landslide Database. 
Landslides in the landslide compilation were mapped post-2008 and are less accurate and 
detailed than landslides from the landslide inventory section of the landslide database.
How to Use This Map
Landslide Compilation
One or more landslides have been mapped in your area of interest.  These mapped landslides 
are part of an older compilation of landslides mapped during routine geologic mapping and 
these maps were created at various scales, quality, and accuracy.  Based on these mapped 
landslides, future landsliding is possible, depending upon the site, water drainage, cuts and 
fills, on-site septic systems, etc. A landslide hazard investigation is highly recommended. The 
2018 International Building Code (IBC) and International Residential Code (IRC), adopted 
statewide, require a geotechnical investigation where landslides (slope instability) may be 
present beneath or aﬀect a building. Contact your local building department for requirements, 
and a Utah-licensed geotechnical engineer for investigations. Note that many landslides 
impact more than one parcel and mitigating one of these landslides on only one parcel is 
rarely successful, resulting in continued building and property damage.

nalysis and Findings 
A
 condominium means the ownership of a single unit in a multi-unit project together with an 
undivided interest in the common area of the property. The Code does not contain specific 
standards for approval of a condominium plat; however, Utah State Code does require approval 
and recordation of a condominium plat prior to selling the units. 
Staff finds that the condominium plat is consistent with all prior approvals for the project and is 
necessary for the units to be sold separately. Planning Staff recommends Conditions of 
Approval to ensure easements are in place as required by Eastern Summit County Development 
Code 11-4-5 (D) which states, “To the extent feasible, the location and dimensions of all

Dear Ms. Williams, 
The Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (DWR) provides the following comments regarding the Blue 
Sky Corporate Ranch LLC Preliminary Subdivision Plat Application (BSL-1, BLUE SR-1, NS-86-A, 
Zone District: AG-80). The DWR participated in a site visit with this applicant in 2025. 
The DWR’s mission is to serve the people of Utah as trustee and guardian of the state’s protected 
wildlife, and our Strategic Plan outlines goals and objectives in support of maintaining existing wildlife 
habitats and increasing the quality of critical habitats and watersheds throughout the state. 
The proposed subdivision is within crucial elk and mule deer winter range in Summit County. Currently, 
the area offers necessary forage, thermal cover, and open movement corridors that animals depend on to 
survive winter and avoid deep snow. The development will reduce forage and fragment habitat. 
To reduce disturbance to big game that utilize this area for winter habitat, we recommend avoiding 
construction activities from December 1 through April 15. 
We also recommend the applicant avoid constructing fences that impede wildlife movement. If fencing is 
necessary, it should allow wildlife to move safely. This can be achieved by fence heights of less than 42 
inches (40 preferred), described as wildlife-friendly fencing. Vertical bars with points should be avoided 
as they can impale big game. 
It is important to note that big game animals will consume landscaping and other vegetation as food 
sources. The DWR is not responsible for these damages. Utah code 23A-1-206 states: “ ...new 
development in the state is considered infringing on existing wildlife habitat and a person who makes a 
wildlife damage or nuisance claim related to the new development may not qualify for a wildlife damage 
or nuisance claim against the state or a political subdivision.” 
Reducing Human-Wildlife Conflict 
The likelihood of human-wildlife interactions increases as homes and infrastructure overlap with areas 
traditionally used by wildlife. We recommend the following information to be included in any 
“Covenants, Conditions and Restrictions” documents, and that information be posted around the 
development to notify residents and guests about the potential for wildlife interactions and best practices.

Proposal 
The applicants, Garff Rogers Ranches LLC and Ivory Development, have applied to amend  
the Eastern Summit County Development Code to create a new zone called the Lost Creek 
Community Zone (LCCZ). The zone would enable the creation of public/private 
infrastructure, multiple types of residential uses, and neighborhood commercial uses.  
Background 
The applicant is the owner of approximately 407  developable acres in the Brown’s Canyon 
area. Currently the property is zoned AG -80, which means one unit of density for every 80 
acres of land. Access to the property would be from Brown’s Canyon Road.  
The applicant stated: 
“The intent of the LCCZ is to allow for a flexible, master -planned approach to land 
use, infrastructure, and design that responds to site -specific conditions, protects 
environmental resources, and advances long -term community objectives in a 
predictable and transparent manner.” 
The creation of the LCCZ would be the first step in the process. If the application to amend 
the Development Code is successful, the applicant would then apply for a Master Planned 
Development/Rezone process that would get into the specifics of the project (project layout, 
utilities, number and type of units etc.).   
Thi
s application was reviewed previously by the Planning Commis sion on April 2, 2026. At 
the meeti ng, the applicant  presented the project to the Commission and Commission 
members provided initial feedback. The applicant  has made modifications to the draft 
language and is now back for further discussion.

Applicable Review Process 
 
The process for modifying the Eastern Summit County Development Code is established in 
Section 11-5-3. The Code outlines two distinct but related processes: (1) amendments to 
the text of the Development Code, and (2) amendments to the Official Zone Distric t Map 
(rezoning). 
 
A. Development Code Text Amendments 
 
Amendments to the text of the Development Code involve changes to the written regulations 
and standards. The review process includes the following steps: 
 
1. Planning Commission Review: The Planning Commission conducts a public hearing 
and forwards a recommendation. 
2. County Council Action: The County Council conducts a public hearing and takes final 
action to approve or deny the proposed amendment. 
 
The current request is an application to amend the text of the Development Code to 
establish the proposed Lost Creek Zone.  If the text amendment is approved, 
implementation of the zone would require a separate application to amend the Zone District 
Map (rezone) for any specific property. That future application would be reviewed under the 
criteria and procedures outlined above. 
 
Analysis 
 
The proposed Lost Creek Zone introduces a new development framework for properties in 
the Brown’s Canyon/Lost Creek area. Staff have identified areas for discussion. 
 
Scale & Intensity of Development  
 
• The LCCZ enables large-scale, master planned development (100+ acres minimum) 
in a currently low-density, largely undeveloped area.  
• Density and intensity are not defined in the proposed language and would instead be 
established through a future Community Plan and Development Agreement.  
• This limits predictability regarding the ultimate buildout and potential impacts.  
 
Infrastructure & Service Availability  
 
• The subject area currently lacks established utility infrastructure. County code 
typically requires that development demonstrates the ability to provide an adequate 
level of service for water, wastewater, transportation, and other public facilities.

3 
 
o Currently there is n o requirement to demonstrate that water, wastewater, or 
other utilities are: Available , Funded, or Constructed concurrent with 
development prior to construction.   
 
• Brown’s Canyon is an area with limited infrastructure. Staff are concerned that the 
proposed language creates a risk of development proceeding ahead of 
infrastructure.  
 
Environmental & Site Constraints  
 
• Encourages sensitivity to natural features (topography, drainage, habitat), but 
primarily through narrative and conceptual standards.  
• Lacks clear, measurable criteria tied to environmental capacity or resource 
protection.  
 
Open Space (10%)  
 
• The proposal includes a requirement for a minimum of 10% open space. For projects 
of 100 acres or more, this would result in a substantial amount of developable land. 
The Planning Commission may wish to consider whether a higher open space 
requirement or additional design standards would better support resource 
protection and community character objectives. 
 
Affordable Housing and Transportation 
 
• The proposal includes a provision for 10% affordable housing. 
• Because the Eas tern Summit County Development Code does not have specific 
requirements regarding duration of affordability , Unit distribut ion etc. It w ill be 
necessary to clearly define the details in the development agreement.  
• The proposed location for the development currently does not have access to transit 
or other transportation options for residents. Consideration of transportation 
availability is important when evaluating long-term livability and accessibility. 
 
Flexibility vs. Predictability  
 
• The pro ject relies heavi ly on t he conceptual Community P lan and development 
agreement. This provides flexibility to the applicant but  reduces code-based 
certainty and consistency in application. B asic development standards, including 
density, setbacks, building height, and parking , are not explicitly established in the 
proposal and would be subject to negotiation. While flexibility can support creative 
site design, the absence of defined parameters will reduce predictability in the review 
process.

4 
 
General Plan & Precedent  
 
• The proposal is a site-specific zoning tool that is not clearly tied to any of the County’s 
adopted planning documents such as the Eastern Summit County General Plan.  
• The proposed zone may  establish precedents for similar requests in other 
undeveloped areas.  
 
Questions: 
 
• Should baseline development standards (density, height, setbacks, parking) be more 
clearly defined? 
• How does the proposed zone align with General Plan policies for the Brown’s Canyon 
area? 
• What, if any,  infrastructure and phasing requirements should be established to 
ensure adequate levels of service? 
• Is the one-mile proximity standard appropriately scaled? 
• Should a maximum project size or additional controls be considered to manage 
development patterns? 
• Is the proposed open space requirement sufficient? 
 
Recommendation 
 
Staff recommend that the Eastern Summit County Planning Commission review the 
proposed amendment to the Eastern Summit County Development Code and provide 
direction to the applicant and staff.

This Community Plan package is being submitted in order to establish the Lost Creek 
Community Zone (LCCZ) and to provide a comprehensive, policy-aligned framework for the 
coordinated planning and development of the Lost Creek area, as required by Summit County 
Code 11-3-_.C. The intent of the LCCZ is to allow for a flexible, master-planned approach to 
land use, infrastructure, and design that responds to site-specific conditions, protects 
environmental resources, and advances long-term community objectives in a predictable and 
transparent manner. This Community Plan serves as the primary planning document guiding this 
review and provides the basis for Planning Commission consideration, recommendation, and 
subsequent County Council action. 
The Lost Creek Community Zone is initiated through a petition to amend the County Zoning 
Map and rezone a defined geographic area as LCCZ. The proposed zone boundary is established 
by participating property owners and is evaluated in accordance with applicable county code 
requirements. The proposed boundary for the LCCZ can be found in the attached Section A.  
This package is structured to clearly demonstrate how the proposed Community Plan meets the 
requirements of the Lost Creek Community Zone establishment process. It includes a conceptual 
land use plan illustrating the location of proposed residential, neighborhood commercial, and 
mixed-use areas (Section A); an analysis of proposed density ranges (Section B); an outline of 
development standards to be formalized through a development agreement (Section C); a 
description of the public and private spaces (Section D); an description of the infrastructure that 
will serve the LCCZ area (Section E); and a narrative addressing the overall development goals 
for the LCCZ area. The Community Plan is intentionally conceptual in nature, allowing for 
refinement through future subdivision, site plan, and phased development approvals while 
maintaining consistency with the approved vision. 
In addition, the Community Plan addresses the integration of public and private spaces, 
infrastructure provision, and site design considerations necessary to support a complete, 
functional, and resilient community. Particular emphasis is placed on respecting existing 
topography, preserving natural features and wildlife corridors, mitigating impacts to adjacent 
land uses, and providing safe and efficient access for vehicles, pedestrians, bicyclists, and 
emergency services. 
As part of the LCCZ process, the Planning Commission’s recommendation will inform the 
content of a subsequent development agreement proposed to the County Council. Together, the 
Community Plan and development agreement are intended to ensure that future development 
occurs in a manner that is consistent with county policies, responsive to community needs, and 
aligned with the long-term public interest. 
This introduction is intended to orient reviewers, decision-makers, and the public to the purpose, 
structure, and intent of the Community Plan materials that follow and to establish a clear 
framework for evaluating the proposed Lost Creek Community Zone.

Community Plan Design Narrative 
 
This Community Plan reflects a deliberate, flexible and context-sensitive approach to growth, 
shaped first and foremost by the site’s existing natural systems, topography, and access 
constraints. Rather than imposing a rigid development pattern, the plan organizes land uses, 
circulation, and open space in direct response to the land, reinforcing environmental stewardship 
while creating a coherent and functional community framework. 
 
The overall layout is structured around the site’s natural drainage corridors, ridgelines, and 
slopes. Hillside preservation areas are strategically designated to protect steeper terrain and 
visually prominent landforms, maintaining the natural character of the landscape and minimizing 
disturbance. These preserved hillsides also function as natural buffers between development 
areas, reinforcing ecological continuity and long-term slope stability. 
 
Open space is not treated as residual land, but as an organizing element of the plan. A continuous 
network of community and neighborhood parks is woven throughout the site, aligned with 
riparian corridors and low-lying areas that already support natural drainage and vegetation. This 
approach enhances habitat value, supports stormwater management, and creates meaningful 
recreational and visual amenities that are accessible from surrounding neighborhoods. The 
central community park will serve as a focal point for gathering and recreation, while a robust 
trail system and smaller neighborhood parks will provide localized open space integrated into 
daily life. 
 
Circulation is carefully designed to respect existing grades and minimize earthwork. Road 
alignments follow natural contours, reducing visual impacts and infrastructure costs while 
improving safety and drivability. Primary access points are limited and strategically located 
along Lost Creek Road, providing efficient connectivity while avoiding unnecessary 
fragmentation of the site. Internal streets are designed to balance mobility with neighborhood 
character, supporting walkability and intuitive wayfinding. 
 
Development areas of the project are clustered in the locations that are best suited for 
construction. These areas are generally on gentler slopes and outside of sensitive environmental 
corridors. This clustering allows for more efficient infrastructure extension, preserves larger 
contiguous open spaces, and reinforces a clear transition between developed and natural areas. 
The result is a pattern of growth that feels embedded in the landscape rather than superimposed 
upon it. 
 
Overall, the Community Plan demonstrates a thoughtful integration of land use, infrastructure, 
and open space that responds directly to existing site conditions. By aligning development with 
natural systems and topography, the plan achieves a balanced framework that supports long-term 
sustainability, visual quality, and community livability, while remaining flexible enough to adapt 
over time as individual phases are implemented.

Below the specific topics regarding the development of the project are addressed.  
1) Neighborhood Connectivity 
The proposed Community Plan is designed to establish strong physical and functional 
connections within the project area and to surrounding properties, neighborhoods, and regional 
destinations. Connectivity is achieved through a coordinated system of vehicular access, internal 
streets, and bicycle/pedestrian focused facilities that respond to existing conditions and 
anticipated future development patterns. 
a. Vehicle Access 
Primary vehicular access to the site is provided from Lost Creek Road at three (3) 
strategically located entry points. These access locations are designed to align with 
existing roadway geometry, sight distance, and topography, ensuring safe ingress and 
egress while minimizing impacts to adjacent properties and natural features. The internal 
street system design supports local or internal circulation rather than conflicting with 
regional traffic movements. 
b. New Streets 
New streets are developed as part of an interconnected internal network that follows 
natural contours and drainage patterns to minimize grading and visual impacts. The street 
system provides multiple route options for residents, visitors, and service vehicles, 
enhancing emergency access and dispersing traffic loads. Street connections are planned 
to allow for logical extensions to adjacent properties as they develop, supporting long-
term area connectivity.  Street dimensional standards have been provided within the 
project exhibits as part of this application. 
c. Pedestrian and Bicycle Routes 
Pedestrian and bicycle connectivity is a core design element of the plan. Sidewalks and 
shared-use paths are integrated along internal streets, while off-street trails are aligned 
with open space corridors and the Lost Creek riparian areas. These facilities provide safe 
and convenient connections between neighborhoods, parks, and key destinations within 
the site, as well as links to existing and planned regional trail systems where feasible. 
 
2) Availability of Neighborhood Facilities and Services 
The proposed development is located within reasonable proximity to existing and planned 
community facilities, including parks, recreation areas, and regional services. The internal trail 
and street network is designed to support walking and bicycling access to on-site neighborhood 
and community parks, reducing reliance on vehicular trips for daily recreation. Vehicular 
connections to nearby retail, employment, and educational facilities are direct and efficient, 
supporting access to broader community services while maintaining a residential character 
internally. 
 
3) Meeting Housing Needs 
The Community Plan advances local housing goals by providing a range of residential lot sizes 
and housing types distributed throughout the site. This variety supports diverse household needs,

life stages, and income levels, promoting inclusivity and long-term community stability. The 
flexible Community Plan framework allows for phased development and adaptation over time, 
enabling the project to respond to evolving housing demand while remaining consistent with 
community objectives. 
 
4) Character 
Architectural and site design character objectives emphasize compatibility with the surrounding 
landscape and regional context. Development is envisioned to reflect a mountain- and valley-
responsive design approach, incorporating natural materials, articulated building forms, and 
muted color palettes that complement the terrain. Building orientation and massing respond to 
the street network as well as to climate considerations such as solar access, wind, and snow 
management, while maintaining a cohesive neighborhood identity that supports a sense of place. 
 
5) Site Design 
The proposed development is intentionally designed to work with existing site conditions rather 
than reshape them. Steep slopes, ridgelines, and sensitive hillside areas are preserved through 
designated hillside preservation zones and open space areas. Development is clustered on gentler 
terrain, allowing roadways and building pads to follow existing contours and reducing the need 
for extensive grading. Natural drainage corridors and wildlife movement areas are maintained 
and enhanced through integrated open space and trail systems, supporting ecological function 
and visual continuity. 
 
6) Complete Street Design 
The internal circulation system is designed using complete street principles appropriate to a 
semi-rural and residential context. Streets accommodate vehicles, pedestrians, bicyclists, and 
emergency services while prioritizing safety and ease of navigation. Road widths, curvature, and 
intersection design promote slower travel speeds and intuitive wayfinding. Walkways, sidewalks, 
shared-use paths, and trail connections are incorporated where appropriate to provide safe, 
multimodal travel options throughout the community. 
 
7) Parking Areas 
Parking is provided in a manner that balances functional needs with visual quality and 
environmental sensitivity. On-street parking, where appropriate, supports neighborhood 
character and traffic calming, while off-street parking areas are located to minimize visibility 
from primary roadways and open spaces. Parking areas are designed to follow natural grades, 
reducing cut-and-fill, and are visually softened through landscaping, topographic screening, and 
controlled lighting to limit nighttime impacts. 
 
8) Public and Private Outdoor Spaces 
The development includes a robust system of public and private outdoor spaces that support 
recreation, habitat preservation, and community interaction. The community and neighborhood 
parks are strategically located to be accessible from surrounding residential areas, while larger

9) External Storage 
Project-related external storage needs, including refuse collection, recycling, and maintenance
