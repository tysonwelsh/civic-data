       General Plan Process Discussion

Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, noted that while some elements of the
general plan are determined at the city level, state code increasingly dictates the approval
process and specific elements that must be included. Recent amendments to state code have
influenced the general plan, particularly how it integrates with other plans, such as the
transportation master plan and moderate-income housing plans.

Mr. Lethbridge explained that the focus of the Council's recent general plan amendments has
primarily been on the land use map, which outlines the desired land uses for different areas of
the City. The land use map has been the most utilized and important element of the general
plan. However, the text portion of the general plan, which includes definitions and criteria, is
outdated and overdue for revision.

Mr. Lethbridge said the City will also be updating its transportation master plan, which includes
road classifications, transit considerations, and other transportation-related goals. The
transportation master plan will need to align closely with the general plan, land use map,
moderate-income housing, and water plans, as required by state code.

Mr. Lethbridge stated that funding for the transportation master plan has been secured through
the City budget, grants, and funds from the Wasatch Front Regional Council (WFRC) and the
Utah Department of Transportation (UDOT). The City plans to select a consultant to assist with
the data-heavy aspects of the transportation master plan, which will then inform the general plan
update. The City will also engage a consultant to help with the general plan amendment,
particularly the text portion, which will include demographic and statistical data. Although
Riverton City does not anticipate many large undeveloped projects, decisions regarding land
use will still need to be addressed as part of the general plan update.

Councilmember Pierucci expressed a desire to engage the public in the general plan process in
a more accessible and less formal setting than regular council meetings in the council
chambers. He suggested organizing open houses or public hearings in the various districts,
closer to where residents live, to encourage more participation.

Councilmember McDougal emphasized the importance of having accurate data on approved
and permitted housing units before engaging with the public or making decisions related to the
general plan. He pointed out that residents often focus on the current state of development
without considering the density that has already been approved and is in the pipeline. He
requested that the consultant include this data in their process, particularly for discussions
related to transportation issues.

         Town Days Discussion

Sheril Garn, Events and Operations Director, explained that City staff, along with the police and
fire departments, held a debrief meeting to review the recent Town Days event and identify
areas for improvement. The event, which was organized by five full-time employees, required
the participation of 80 staff members working overtime, including 22 employees who worked a
22-hour shift on July 4th.

Key highlights from the event included a record participation of 55 teams in the three-man
sorting competition, showcasing the respect and popularity of the event among participants. The
rodeo, extended to three days this year, sold out quickly, collecting $20,000 in sponsorships.
Suggestions were made to adjust ticket sale timing to align with brochure distribution.

The car show, organized by a City volunteer, saw 225 entries, though some complaints were
received regarding advertising and participation fees. The parade, noted as the most
challenging event to organize, required extensive coordination, including 23 crossing guards, 23
police officers, 18 City staff, and multiple volunteers. Mandatory training for participants
improved the parade's organization, and safety measures were emphasized.

Other events included a successful flag ceremony featuring a performance by the Madrigals, a
breakfast serving 2,100 people, and the country races with 891 participants. The children's
games, though well-attended, faced challenges due to increasing demands from parents,
leading to considerations for changes in the future. Vendor participation included 24 food
vendors and 23 artisan vendors, with feedback suggesting a preference for artisan vendors over
service vendors.

         Cargo Container Discussion

Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, discussed cargo containers and their
compatibility with current ordinances. The existing ordinance prohibits cargo containers but
allows them under specific conditions if they meet exterior material requirements similar to those
for accessory structures.

A key point of discussion was the treatment of agricultural buildings. The ordinance provides a
lighter standard for agricultural accessory structures, allowing materials that are appropriate and
complementary to the main dwelling, though these structures must be used exclusively for
agricultural purposes such as housing animals or storing feed. The flexibility in materials does
not extend to general cargo containers, which must still adhere to the ordinance’s requirements
for exterior materials.

The discussion also covered practices from other cities. For instance:

        Herriman City allows cargo containers for living or accessory space but not as accessory
         dwelling units (ADUs). They require containers to be painted and finished to match the
         primary dwelling.
        St. George and South Salt Lake generally do not allow cargo containers except for brief
         storage periods.
        Ogden’s standard is similar to Riverton’s, where cargo containers are not allowed unless
         enclosed within an accessory building.
        West Valley City permits cargo containers only in agricultural zones.
        Lehi allows them with restrictions against stacking.

Councilmember McCay supported potential adjustments to Riverton’s current ordinance
regarding cargo containers. She agreed with the Mayor's suggestion to allow cargo containers
for “primarily” agricultural use rather than “exclusively,” which could accommodate additional
uses such as garden supplies. She also supported allowing cargo containers on lots over half
an acre and positioned in the backyard, behind the house, to avoid the issues seen with storage
units in front driveways.

Councilmember Pierucci clarified his preference for allowing cargo containers citywide, rather
than restricting them to agricultural uses. He suggested that containers should be subject to
similar standards as storage sheds, including placement behind the property and adherence to
materials or colors that match the building. He was open to exploring specific size requirements
and other details but emphasized the need for a consistent and practical approach.

Councilmember McDougal expressed support for allowing cargo containers citywide, aligning
them with standards similar to those for storage sheds. He recommended that containers be
allowed on various lot sizes, but with clear specifications regarding acceptable exterior finishes.

Councilmember Haymond emphasized the importance of maintaining the aesthetic appeal of
the community while allowing cargo containers. He noted that even in large agricultural areas,
industrial structures can look out of place, and advocated for careful planning to prevent an
industrialized look in Riverton and to keep the overall design inviting and visually appealing.

Councilmember Buroker expressed strong reservations about permitting cargo containers in

         Motion to Reconsider Ordinance No. 24-14 – Firework Restrictions in City Parks

Councilmember Haymond reflected on a previous discussion regarding restrictions on fireworks
in public parks. He acknowledged the reasoning behind the proposed restrictions, particularly
the concern over potential damage to public property. However, he pointed out that there are
already ordinances in place against damaging public property. He emphasized that public parks
are ideal locations for fireworks, especially for those who live in areas where space is limited
and setting off fireworks could pose risks to neighboring properties. He suggested that the
Council reconsider the restrictions, as he believes existing protections are sufficient and do not
want to deprive the community of the opportunity to enjoy fireworks in public parks.

Mr. Carter discussed concerns regarding the potential legal liabilities associated with allowing
fireworks in city parks. He acknowledged that while there are existing ordinances against
damaging public property, these may not fully address the risks posed by fireworks. He
explained that if a venue, such as a park, allows activities like fireworks that could foreseeably
cause harm, the City could be held liable, especially if an incident occurs due to negligence.

Mr. Carter further elaborated that while existing laws prohibit intentional property damage, they
may not be sufficient to prosecute cases where fireworks unintentionally cause harm, such as
when a garbage can is accidentally set on fire. He suggested that an ordinance specifically
prohibiting fireworks in parks could prevent such incidents.

Councilmember Pierucci expressed understanding of the challenges in proving intent in criminal
cases, particularly regarding property damage caused by fireworks. Initially opposed to
reconsidering the issue, he explained how discussion with another member led to new insights
and concerns that had not been fully explored in previous meetings. These included questions
about specific types of fireworks and the broader implications of restricting their use.

Councilmember Pierucci MOVED that the City Council Reconsider Ordinance No. 24-14 –
Firework Restrictions in City Parks. Councilmember Haymond SECONDED the motion.
Mayor Staggs called for discussion on the motion. Mayor Staggs called for a roll-call vote. The
vote was as follows: Buroker-yes, Haymond-yes, McCay-yes, McDougal-yes, and Pierucci-yes.
The motion passed unaminously.
