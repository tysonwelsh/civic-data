improvements to the building's architecture by adding more definition and changing some color
schemes, which made the building look better and allowed the signage to be more visible. The
Council also approved the modified berm solution in lieu of the precast wall, appreciating the

   1.2. Presentation and discussion of potential amendments to the Crescent Commercial
        Development Master Development Agreement for ±15.5 acres of property located
        approximately at 5452 W Herriman Boulevard in the C-2 Commercial Zone and owned by
        Herriman 73 Partners LLC – Michael Maloy, Planning Director

Planning Director Maloy introduced Larry Myler, the property owner, and Steve Lichtie, a potential
partner developer and tenant. He explained that the current MDA document for the property
includes prohibited land uses despite being in a C-2 zone. The C-2 zone is designed primarily for
commercial services and retail, not wholesale or light manufacturing.

The potential tenant, Black Clover, has a business model that involves merchandise coming in and
out of the location with some retail component, but also includes reselling and light manufacturing
with textiles. The developer is interested in attracting other tenants with similar uses, as well as
possibly quick-serve restaurants or other food/retail establishments.

The proposed amendments would modify the allowable uses, make changes to the transition
between commercial and residential areas (using landscape buffers in exchange for precast walls),
and adopt design standards that mirror what has already been approved in the Auto Mall Special
District.

The developer’s partner Steve Lichtie presented renderings of the proposed development,
explaining that the building would be approximately 34 feet high, with architectural elements to
avoid flat walls. The warehouse portion would be about 75,000 square feet and would employ
approximately 140-150 people working Monday through Friday with operations closing at 5:00 PM.
The property would not be open on weekends, making it a good neighbor to surrounding residential
areas.

The loading docks would be located on the rear of the building, and retail spaces would be
positioned toward the front along the main road. A retention basin would be required at the lowest
part of the property. Mr. Lichtie suggested he might incorporate a pickleball court or putting green
in the basin area for employees to use.

Commissioners expressed concerns about:

       •   Ensuring appropriate screening of loading docks visible from Herriman Boulevard
       •   Addressing potential light intrusion from delivery trucks into adjacent residential areas
       •   Creating a proper transition between commercial and residential properties

Larry Myler, developer, noted that this proposed use would be far less intrusive to neighbors than
many other potential commercial uses, with limited hours of operation and minimal traffic on
evenings and weekends.

   1.3. Presentation and discussion of a Utah Code requirement to add a Water Use and
        Preservation Element to the Herriman City General Plan – Susan Petheram, AICP, FFKR
        Architects (consultant)

Susan Petheram from FFKR Architects presented information about the new Utah state requirement
for cities to add a Water Use and Preservation Element to their general plans. She proposed creating
a standalone element that would follow the same structure as the existing general plan.

The proposed outline includes:

   1.   Introduction - Framework explaining why the element is required

   2. Context - Information about Herriman's water supply, conservation goals, and the regional
      context

   3. Planning for the Future - Development patterns, water demand, infrastructure, and
      strategies for reducing demand

   4. Goals, Aims, and Aspirations - What the city wants to achieve

   5. Action Items - Implementation plan

   6. Best Practices and Resources

She explained that the state code requires this element to address at least four areas: The effect of
permitted development patterns on water demand and infrastructure, methods for reducing water
demand and efficient use for existing development, methods for reducing water demand and
efficient use for future development, and modifications to city operations that can reduce wasteful
water practices. She noted that Herriman is already ahead of many cities in terms of water
conservation efforts, including water efficiency standards and metering of secondary water. The
element would connect to the city's overall vision of "planning for stewardship" and its four key
initiatives in the general plan.

Commissioners discussed several water-related concerns, including the balance between water
conservation and maintaining attractive landscaping, the challenges of moving away from traditional
lawns to more water-wise alternatives, and issues with heat islands created by rock landscaping
around trees. They also noted the limited options for water collection at individual homes, currently
restricted to two rain barrels, and the potential for gray water systems and more drought-resistant
grass types. Cost barriers to changing existing landscaping were also mentioned. Susan indicated
that the draft element would likely be ready for review in October, with the goal of having it adopted

city's tree management plan as another element to add to the general plan.

    1.4. Presentation and discussion of potential single-family residential development
        patterns and standards for Herriman – Michael Maloy, AICP, Planning Director
Planning Director Maloy presented on his research regarding residential infill zoning tools for
parcels of 1-5 acres. He reminded the Commission that current tools like PUDs (15+ acres) and
MDAs are not suitable for smaller infill parcels. The city has been concerned that despite allowing
smaller lots in some areas, they aren't seeing smaller homes—developers are building the same size
homes on smaller lots. While there has been interest in attainable or workforce housing, such
programs typically require density or financial incentives that the city currently lacks. Based on his
research, Director Maloy recommended the following standards for a potential new zoning tool: lot
widths between 45-65 feet, lot depths between 90-125 feet, lot coverage between 30-35% (lower
than he initially expected), maximum impervious surface as a separate metric from lot coverage,
single-wide driveways with flaring at garages, alley access when available with a mix of front and
rear access, and a height allowance up to 2.5 stories.

The Commission discussed concerns about side yard setbacks (recommended at 5 feet minimum)
and ensuring at least one side yard remains fully accessible for emergency access. They also
considered whether the tool would be too restrictive for developers to use, the market challenges
(as builders make more money on larger homes), the potential for shared driveways (generally
recommended for no more than two homes), and where this type of development might fit in
Herriman. Director Maloy acknowledged that the tool is intentionally restrictive to encourage
developers to engage in a design process. He indicated that the next step would be to update the
draft based on the Commission's feedback and identify potential locations where this zoning tool
might be applied. The Commission generally supported the proposed approach as a useful tool for
future infill development.

    1.5. Presentation and discussion of Herriman City Land Development Code, standards, policies,
         and best practices to ensure compliance with the Utah State Code and the adopted General
         Plan – Michael Maloy, Planning Director

Chair Bradford presented findings from a recent Parks Committee meeting she and Director Maloy
attended. A statistically valid survey of 609 residents revealed several key findings: Quality of life in
Herriman is rated high, but satisfaction decreases the longer people have lived in the city. Park use
has increased while trail use has decreased. Residents are satisfied with the amount of parks,
amenities, and trails but want improved quality and more features. Residents want more outdoor
markets, vendor fairs, and cultural events. Common complaints focused on high density, traffic, and
overcrowding. Residents praised parks, trails, and sense of community. Desired amenities included
pickleball courts, splash pads, more trees and shade, and better trail connectivity. Residents
expressed interest in an amphitheater, arts/community building, and a climbing park. The current
level of service is 3.3 park acres per 1,000 people, which includes the cemetery and detention
basins. Without additional park land, this ratio would decrease to 2.1 acres per 1,000 people as the
population grows. To maintain the current level, the city would need to add 92 additional acres by
2035 and 163 acres by 2050.
