Item 1: An ordinance amending the zone map classification of real property, generally
located at 2000 N Canyon Road, from the one-family residential (R1.8) zone to the one-
family residential (R1.6) zone. Pleasant View neighborhood. (PLRZ20250097)

Council members discussed concerns regarding the steep slope of the property and the potential
risks associated with future retaining walls. Nancy Robison, Planner, explained that geotechnical
studies had been conducted and that city engineering staff were confident in the soil quality and
the proposed grading plan. Director Peperone clarified that the current proposal did not include
retaining walls and that the slope would be redistributed to create a more gradual incline.

Councilor Christensen expressed concern about the risks tied to large retaining walls, referencing
structural issues at Wasatch Elementary School. Director Peperone responded that the school’s
problems were due to subsurface water seepage, a condition not anticipated at this site.

Item 2: An ordinance amending the zone map classification of real property, generally
located at 3185 West 550 South, from the agricultural (A1.5) zone to the airport industrial
(AI) zone. Provo Bay neighborhood. (PLRZ20250011)

Aaron Ardmore, Planning Supervisor, presented the proposal to rezone the property from
Agricultural (A1.5) to Airport Industrial (AI). He stated that the applicant had previously
developed similar speculative warehouse office buildings on nearby parcels and intended to
extend this pattern of development to the newly acquired land. Staff supported the request,
noting that the proposal aligned with the surrounding zoning and land uses.

Councilor Bogdin voiced support for the rezoning but expressed concerns about maintaining
commercial development potential across the street to the east. She highlighted the importance of
preserving options for hotels and restaurants near the planned 600 South connection to the
parkway. Director Peperone acknowledged these concerns and noted the difficulty of using
zoning as a tool to compel market-based outcomes. He advised against eliminating warehousing
from the zone entirely, stating that doing so could negatively impact the property owner and
significantly delay potential development.

adjacent to South Fork Park as surplus property. She explained that the property was acquired in
2017 as part of a land swap agreement with the Giles family. The terms of the agreement
included a 99-year lease, with an understanding that the parcel would be deeded back to the
Giles family upon request. That request has now been made.

Riddle noted that all relevant city departments had reviewed the proposal and consented to the
surplus designation, contingent upon the preservation of an access easement for both a city water

Item 4: A consideration to authorize the Council Chair to sign an engagement letter with
Richard H. Bowler for internal audit services. (25-062)

Justin Harrison, Council Executive Director, presented a proposal to engage Richard H. Bowler
for internal audit services. He explained the various types of audits conducted by the city—
financial audits, single audits, and internal audits—and emphasized the value of internal audits in
identifying organizational risks, validating internal controls, and enhancing the Council's
financial oversight capabilities.

Harrison stated that securing an internal auditor had proven difficult, but Mr. Bowler, a retired
audit professional with significant experience, had been identified as a qualified candidate. The
proposed agreement would authorize up to $10,000 for services rendered through the fiscal year
ending June 30, 2025, at a rate of $125 per hour.

Councilor MacKay asked about the confidentiality protocols in place for such services. City
Attorney Jones explained that auditors are held to strict professional ethical standards and risk
losing their certification if they breach confidentiality.

Councilor Christensen expressed confidence in Mr. Bowler’s qualifications and noted his
involvement in the local community.

The Council briefly discussed the defined scope of the audit and acknowledged the potential for
seeking additional funding if the audit's scope were to expand.

Motion: Councilor Christensen moved to authorize the Council Chair to sign the engagement
letter with Richard H. Bowler for internal audit services. Councilor Whipple seconded the
motion.

Item 5: A resolution regarding year-end appropriations in the Airport Fund, General
Fund, and Justice Court Fund for the fiscal year ending June 30, 2025. (25-063)

Budget Officer Andrea Wright presented the proposed year-end appropriations for the Airport
Fund, General Fund, and Justice Court Fund, explaining the rationale for each adjustment.

A proposed increase of $267,000 in the General Fund is recommended to support police and fire
department services at the airport. Wright noted that this increase would be offset by
corresponding revenue from the Airport Fund, reflecting the airport’s operational responsibility
for these services. In parallel, an appropriation of $267,000 is designated within the Airport Fund
to finance these services.

An additional $80,000 appropriation in the General Fund was proposed to provide personal
protection equipment and ammunition for the police department, supporting continued
operational readiness.

Wright also recommended a $75,000 appropriation in the General Fund to cover increased utility
costs, particularly water, associated with park maintenance.

A further $20,000 appropriation was outlined for Indigent Defense within the General Fund. This
allocation, managed by the Finance Department, aims to ensure continued access to legal defense
for individuals requiring financial assistance.

Councilor Bogdin questioned why police and fire personnel assigned to the airport are not simply
paid directly from the airport budget, rather than transferring funds to the General Fund. Andrea
Wright explained that although these personnel serve airport functions, they report to the police
and fire chiefs and are formally part of their respective departments. Because of this
organizational structure, their salaries must be paid from the police and fire departments’
budgets, with the airport reimbursing those costs. Wright confirmed that this process is standard
practice each year and clarified that the airport is indeed covering the full cost of those services,
with no subsidy from the General Fund.

Wright concluded by stating that these appropriations are scheduled for formal approval during
the Council meeting on June 3rd.

Item 7: A presentation regarding projected revenues for FY26 (25-025)

John Borget, Administrative Services Director, presented revenue projections for the FY26
budget. He reported that General Fund revenues are projected at $72 million, with sales tax
comprising the largest share at 37% of the total.

Borget noted a projected decrease of $1.8 million in sales tax revenue compared to FY25,
reflecting recent economic trends and changes in consumer behavior affecting tax receipts.
Conversely, revenues from municipal fees are expected to increase by $1.5 million, due to recent
adjustments across various departments.

Overall, the projections indicate a net increase of $763,000 in revenues. Additionally, transfers
are anticipated to contribute another $2 million to the General Fund.

Borget also discussed the restructuring of the debt service fund, which has resulted in surplus
revenue that will further support the General Fund.

Item 8: A presentation regarding transfers in the proposed tentative FY25-26 budget (25-
025)

Dan Follett, Finance Division Director, presented information on the proposed transfers from
Provo City’s utility enterprise funds to the General Fund for FY 2025-2026. He explained that
these transfers serve as a dividend for city taxpayers, recognizing their investment in city-owned
utility infrastructure and supporting general municipal services.

The total proposed transfers for FY26 amount to approximately $18.1 million and include two
key components:

       Utility Rate Transfers: Approximately $16.36 million, reflecting 12.5% of utility
        revenues. This portion functions similarly to dividends returned to taxpayers.
       Administrative Overhead Transfers: Around $1.71 million to cover the cost of
        centralized services such as legal, human resources, IT, and finance that benefit all
        departments.

Follett emphasized that these transfers are instrumental in funding critical city services such as
police, fire, and parks, while contributing to lower property tax rates. He compared this structure
to dividend practices in the private sector, where publicly traded utilities typically pay dividends
ranging from 0% for growth-oriented companies to over 10% for established utilities.

Councilor Bogdin noted that dividend rates from publicly traded utilities are generally under
10%, which aligns with the city’s current transfer rate.

Item 10: A discussion regarding the FY26 General Fund (25-025)

David Pyle, Policy Analyst, presented an overview of the tentative FY26 General Fund, with
projected total revenues and transfers amounting to approximately $89 million. About one-third
of these revenues are derived from sales tax, including the Recreation, Arts, and Parks (RAP)
tax. Pyle noted that sales and RAP tax revenues are trending downward compared to the prior
year and clarified that a slide incorrectly reported the RAP tax figure; the correct projection
shows a 9 percent decline.

Transfers from other City funds now comprise approximately 19 percent of total General Fund
revenues, marking an increase over FY24. Pyle acknowledged that this ratio significantly
exceeds the average of roughly 7 percent in comparable Utah municipalities.

Expenditures and transfers out are balanced at roughly $89 million, reflecting fiscally
responsible operations. Departmental allocations are led by the Police Department, which
receives the largest share—approximately one-third of the General Fund—followed by the Fire

Department and Parks. All departments are slated for increased expenditures in FY26, with the
exception of the Engineering Department, which achieved a year-over-year decrease.

Pyle reviewed three key fiscal ratios:

    1. Debt-Service Ratio: Currently at zero, indicating no General Fund dollars are needed to
       support the City’s Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) debt service.
    2. Intergovernmental Revenue Ratio: Standing at 1.1 percent, this reflects a high degree
       of financial independence but also highlights potential for increased grant acquisition.
    3. Debt-to-Assessed-Value Ratio: At an exceptionally low 0.5 percent, this suggests
       minimal reliance on bonded debt.

Pyle concluded that the FY26 General Fund is financially healthy, with balanced operations, low
debt obligations, and a modest dependence on external funding sources, though tempered by
declining tax revenues and rising inter-fund transfers.

During the question-and-answer period, Councilor Handley expressed concern that inter-fund
transfers now constitute a growing share of the General Fund and asked whether staff views this
trend as worrisome or immaterial—particularly in relation to property-tax policy and its differing
impacts on homeowners versus renters. Mr. Pyle responded that while transfers currently
represent about 19 percent of Provo’s General Fund revenues—compared to roughly 7 percent
among peer Utah cities—this can be defensible when enterprise funds are financially healthy and
help maintain lower property-tax rates. However, he cautioned that “chronic” reliance on
transfers could strain donor funds and leave the General Fund vulnerable if those revenue
streams weaken. Handley then inquired how Provo’s 36 percent reliance on sales tax compares
statewide. Pyle acknowledged that this figure had not yet been benchmarked but agreed to
provide that comparison.

Councilor MacKay emphasized the need for a holistic analysis that considers sales-tax, property-
tax, and transfer ratios collectively, recognizing that each municipality has a different fiscal
profile. Councilor Handley added that sales tax is inherently volatile, reinforcing the need for
such broader context. Councilor Bogdin requested that the forthcoming comparative table
specify which utilities—such as electricity, water, sewer, and garbage—are operated by each
peer city to enable an accurate interpretation of the 7 percent transfer benchmark. Pyle concurred
and committed to including those details in the follow-up materials.
