          3.4        Ranked Choice Voting Presentation – Kory Holdaway

        Kory Holdaway expressed his appreciation to the mayor and council as a Taylorsville
resident. He described the history of Ranked Choice Voting and stated it was already being used
in various parts of the country, had been used at some of Utah’s political conventions and had been
used by both Payson and Vineyard during the most recent municipal election. He anticipated
additional cities using it the following year and hoped that Taylorsville’s City Council would send
letters of interest to both the Salt Lake County Clerk and the Lieutenant Governor’s office.

Mr. Holdaway explained how RCV worked, saying it identified the candidate with the majority of
votes once voters ranked their choices on any given race. If a candidate received 50% or more of
all votes cast, the election would be over, and the winner declared. However, if no candidate
received 50% or more of the votes cast, the candidate with the least number of votes was dropped
from the race. At that point, the second choice of those who had voted for the dropped candidate
would come into play. The votes would again be counted, and a winner declared once a candidate
received 50% or more of the votes. Mr. Holdaway felt RCV was a better way to run elections and
asserted that it:
    • saved the City money by reducing the number of necessary elections (no primary was
        needed—only a general election would be held). Therefore, he indicated the cost would be
        cut by 50%.
    • reduced the amount of money spent by candidates because again, there would only be one
        election to participate in
    • would compress the campaign season into a shorter window of time
    • was ready for cities to implement for the next election
    • better expressed voters’ will
    • ensured election winners received a majority of the votes cast

     •    ensured no votes would be “wasted”
     •    increased civility among candidates as they would wish to ensure that even if they weren’t
          a voter’s first choice, perhaps they could be their second choice if they remained civil and
          professional in their campaign

Mr. Holdaway stated that Salt Lake County was in the process of upgrading their equipment to
accommodate RCV. County Clerk Sherrie Swensen was unsure if she could run an RCV election
on the new equipment and was not yet willing to commit to doing so. Still, the Salt Lake County
Council was interested in having her explore the possibility of using RCV.

Chair Harker confirmed with Mr. Holdaway that if Taylorsville opted to use RCV, voters would
use RCV for municipal elections but the traditional method for state and federal elections (unless
proposed legislation was approved during the next legislative session.) Therefore, voters would
have to get used to voting two different ways, depending on what type of election was taking place.

Voters in both Payson and Vineyard were surveyed after using RCV for the 2019 municipal
election. Eighty-six percent of the respondents and 82% wanted to use it again. A far as the
candidates’ response to the survey, 87% of them—both those that were victorious and those who
were not—were happy with RCV.

The State legislature had passed a resolution commending Payson and Vineyard for using RCV
and encouraged other municipalities to participate in the method. Mr. Holdaway indicated that if
Taylorsville wished to participate in RCV, we would have the option of contracting with someone
other than Salt Lake County for election services.

Apparently, the Utah County Clerk loved RCV and Mr. Holdaway believed more of Utah County
cities were going to participate in it going forward. He feared that the Salt Lake County Clerk
would not adapt to the change. However, he stated that Utah County Clerk’s office would contract
with any city who was interested in participating in RCV—even if that city were outside Utah
County. He explained that if interested in using RCV for the next election, Taylorsville needed to
notify the Lieutenant Governor’s office by April 15, 2021. He finished his remarks by stating that
as a non-profit entity with a 501c3 status, Ranked Choice Voting could offer additional support
for cities that opted into the program.

Chair Harker made it clear that Taylorsville was not interested in running its own election. Still,
she saw many positive things about Ranked Choice Voting.

Councilmember Barbieri inquired about Mr. Holdaway’s background. He responded that he
became interested in RCV when he was serving in the legislature. Councilmember Barbieri felt it
was a good conversation to continue to have.

     7. OTHER MATTERS

          7.1        Taylorsville City Internet and Fiber Discussion – Tracy Cowdell

      City Attorney Tracy Cowdell presented information regarding broadband, fiber and the status
of technology in Taylorsville. He began by explaining that City administration had met with
existing partners, consultants of potential new partners, researched the approaches that other
municipalities had taken, reviewed and evaluated various options and examined the demands of
the Taylorsville community. He gave a brief overview of internet access, how it worked and the
evolution of technology and the law over time. He described the general growing concerns within
municipal government before delving into three primary options for addressing the emerging
challenges, including the pros and cons of each.
    • The traditional or market model
    • The municipal service model
    • The hybrid model

Regarding the status of broadband in Taylorsville, Mr. Cowdell explained that 98% of the
community had sufficient internet access and bandwidth speeds. While he felt those who needed
synchronous connectivity had valid concerns, they were still a very small group of citizens
compared to the City as a whole.

John Taylor pointed out that some people seemed to believe that Taylorsville was keeping fiber
out of the community when in fact, the City would love to have it. Unfortunately, Utopia would
only come if the City covered their financial risk. Mr. Cowdell said he had serious concerns about
a model that leveraged the taxpayers. It was a very serious commitment.

Councilmember Cochran asked if the trend was to go wireless, why would the City want to make
such a big investment in fiber? Mr. Cowdell responded that most people would say that a fiber
connection was the absolute best. Regardless, the City was actively working on things and was
not in a reactive posture. The administration was happy to continue to update the council as
necessary.
