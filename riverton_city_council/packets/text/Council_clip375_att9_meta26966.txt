43   Sanitation Rate Study Results
44
45   Kevin Hicks, Administrative Services Director, spoke in reference to Strategic Priority #8.2 which
46   states: “Sustain a diversified revenue stream with a reasonable tax and fee burden, while finding
47   ways to reduce the financial burden when appropriate.” Mr. Hicks explained that the sanitation
48   fund is an enterprise or a business type fund. He explained that with a business type fund is
49   sound financial practice for all enterprise funds to be self-sustaining. He further explained that
50   the current waste collection fee is set at $5 per month for the first waste container and there is
51   no charge for the first recycling container, which is included with the waste container.
     Page | 3 Minutes – City Council Meeting

 1   Customers with an additional waste container are charged an additional $6 per month and an
 2   additional recycling container costs $3 per month. Mr. Hicks said these rates are extremely low
 3   when compared to other communities and they do not cover current costs. He said that most
 4   communities charge more than $10 per month for curbside services. He then presented a table
 5   showing how Riverton City’s current monthly rates compare to those charged in other
 6   communities.
 7
 8   Aaron Montgomery explained that Zion’s Public Finance, Inc. was retained by Riverton City to
 9   perform a study of the City’s sanitation fees. The purpose of the study was to analyze current
10   City Trash and recycling services and rates, identify deficiencies and opportunities, and provide
11   solutions to assist the City in developing revenue stability to achieve its goal of providing
12   sustainable service at a low cost to its growing population.
13
14   Mr. Montgomery explained that the City has contracted with a third party to provide curbside
15   pickup of waste and recycling in Riverton City. A monthly fee for waste collection is charged to
16   all customers for the purpose of covering the operational costs which accompany waste
17   collection services. Under the current rate, the revenues for waste collection covered roughly
18   66.8% of the operating expenses in the 2018-2019 fiscal year. This deficit has been subsidized
19   by transfers from the City’s REDIIF fund. Unless there is a change to the fee amount, this deficit
20   will only increase as expenses are expected to grow more quickly than the revenues that will be
21   brought in through growth in developed properties and annual subsidies from the REDIIF fund
22   will continue at an increasing rate. Due to the large deficit between expenses and revenues in
23   the 2018-2019 fiscal year and the 2019-2020 fiscal year projections, he presented four options
24   with the goal to balance revenues and expenses without imposing undue hardship on the
25   residents in Riverton City.
26
27   Four different options were reviewed and discussed and Mr. Montgomery said that all four
28   options would require the City to consider increasing the rate by a substantial amount in the
29   2019-2020 fiscal year to meet the current and increasing costs of waste collection. This deficit is
30   then subsidized by the City’s REDIIF fund. The following options were outlined in detail:
31
32       1. A one-time increase on all rates that would allow the City to meet all the operating
33          expenses that are not being met with the current rate and would end the need for
34          subsidies for about ten years.
35       2. Increase the rate in two steps; the first increase during the 2019-2020 fiscal year and the
36          second increase during the 2021-2022 fiscal year.
37       3. Increase the rate at smaller amounts through the next four consecutive years, starting
38          during the 2019-2020 fiscal year.
39       4. Increases designed to offer the City an alternative that allows for rates to be reweighted
40          so that the rate for additional waste containers would be more comparable to other local
41          rate structures.
42
43   Mr. Montgomery said in summary that there is currently a large deficit between the revenues
44   produced by the current waste collection fees charged by the City and the operational expenses
45   incurred. This deficit, and the subsequent subsidy from the City’s REDIIF fund, will continue
46   unless the City considers lowering costs or increasing rates to help the Sanitation Fund become
47   more financially independent. The rate increase options outlined in the study were specifically
48   designed to allow the City to achieve revenue stability while providing sustainable service at a
49   low cost to its growing population.

33   Rocky Mountain Power re. HB411
34
35   Brad Knowles, Community Manager Rocky Mountain Power, presented information
36   regarding HB 411 and said that in the spring of 2019, the Utah Legislature enacted the
37   “Community Renewable Energy Act” (HB 411), which will create a framework for municipalities
38   like Salt Lake City, Park City and Moab to work with the power company toward the goal of
39   providing net 100% renewable energy by 2030. He said this would mean Rocky Mountain
40   Power would produce at least as much renewable energy as the city uses in a year. The
41   purpose of the Community Renewable Energy Act is to enable an electrical utility provider, such
42   as Rocky Mountain Power, to restructure its power generation system for delivery of electric
43   power sourced from renewable energy facilities. This naturally leads to the future construction of
44   power generating facilities that do not rely on fossil fuels.
45
46   Mr. Knowles said that HB 411 calls for, but does not require, municipalities to opt into a city-
47   wide renewable energy program. He reviewed costs for the early implementation of the
48   program and said that cities that have opted in by December 31, 2019 will determine items such
49   anticipated rates, the impact to customer rates, the estimated participation of residents in the
50   community into the program, and the cost of additional the renewable energy that would replace
51   or early retire some of the thermal generation assets. He said the costs were unknown at this
     Page | 5 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     November 12, 2019



 1   time; they will be studied, developed, and known later on in the process. He suggested that the
 2   community should ask themselves whether this was a good fit and the estimated rate of
 3   participation. He said the cost would be solely born by the municipalities that are in the program;
 4   they would not be spread out among all the rate payers at Rocky Mountain Power, so what
 5   would the impact be and what is the risk tolerance of the City if a majority of the residents opt
 6   out of the program and agree to stay on the standard rates?

 7       a. Proposed Resolution No. 19-80 – Establishing a Community Renewable Energy
 8          Program; Authorizing the Riverton City Manager to Explore Opportunities with
 9          Rocky Mountain Power to Create a Community Renewable Energy Program; and
10          Related Matters
11
12   Ryan Carter, City Attorney, explained that in the spring of 2019, the Utah Legislature enacted
13   the “Community Renewable Energy Act” (HB 411). He said the purpose of the Community
14   Renewable Energy Act was to enable an electrical utility provider, such as Rocky Mountain
15   Power, to restructure its power generation system for delivery of electric power sourced from
16   renewable energy facilities. This naturally leads to the future construction of power generating
17   facilities that do not rely on fossil fuels. He said the legislation calls for, but does not require,
18   municipalities to opt into a city-wide renewable energy program.
19
20   Mr. Carter said that, if the Riverton City Council was interested in launching the program, it must
21   state its interest in seeing a supply of renewable energy be provided to Riverton Residents by
22   adopting a resolution that “states a goal of achieving an amount equivalent to 100% of the
23   annual electric energy supply for participating customers from a renewable energy resource by
24   2030.” He explained that the opt-in resolution does not carry with it any financial obligation. The
25   Community Renewable Energy Act requires the Council to approve such a resolution by
26   December 31, 2019, if the Council elects to support such a program. Sometime following the
27   adoption of the resolution, he said the City would then be presented with an agreement, which
28   does financially obligate the City to pay for “third-party expertise contracted for by the [Utah
29   Division of Public Utilities], and the [Office of Consumer Services], for assistance with activities
30   associated with initial approval of the community renewable energy program.” He said it was
31   premature to know what the costs of such a contract would be, but adopting a resolution did not
32   require the City to execute the agreement. Following adoption of a resolution and approval of an
33   agreement to pay for expenses previously mentioned, he said the City would also need to adopt
34   an ordinance that enforces the agreement and any additional terms or conditions required by
35   the Utah Division of Public Utilities. Ultimately, the costs of delivering power sourced from
36   renewable energy, if any, would be borne by rate payers within Riverton City. He said the latter
37   components (i.e. the agreement and ordinance) for launching a community renewal energy
38   program may take another year to develop. Therefore, the costs associated with entering an
39   agreement and the ultimate costs that ratepayers would experience by funding renewable
40   energy remains to be determined. He said that in the event the costs become too high, adoption
41   of the proposed resolution would not prevent the City from opting out of the program in the
42   future. He also said that if the Council was committed, they would have to be committed to a
43   public awareness campaign.
