24   1.0    Mid-Valley Active Transportation Plan.
25
26   City Planner, Andrew Hulka introduced Thomas McMurtry from Avenue Consultants.
27   Mr. McMurtry reported that he serves as Project Manager for the Mid-Valley Active
28   Transportation Plan. The study began in early 2020 and is the largest Active Transportation Plan
29   (“ATP”) completed in Salt Lake County. This plan was designed to be a regional plan involving
30   six cities including Cottonwood Heights, the City of Holladay, Midvale, Millcreek, Murray, and
31   Taylorsville.
32
33   Mr. McMurtry reported that they received a lot of participation from each of the cities. Matt
34   Taylor served as the Principal Representative from Cottonwood Heights before Mr. Hulka took
35   over. He identified the website as www.midvalleyatp.com, which includes all of the information
36   developed by the study along with an interactive map that contains the final list of recommended
37   projects. He highlighted the Cottonwood Heights tab on the website that contains a .pdf of the
38   draft reports.
39
40   Mr. McMurty mentioned that Cottonwood Heights was unique among the six participating cities
41   in that it recently completed a Bicycles and Trails Master Plan, which they integrated into the
42   Mid-Valley ATP. He applauded the City for the good work that was completed to create that
43   Master Plan. The level of collaboration was important and was a major focus of the study. The
44   study began with a Vision Workshop that was designed to bring everyone together to develop a
45   Vision and a set of Goals. The Vision Workshop included the participating cities, the Utah

 2   Salt Lake County.
 3
 4   Mr. McMurty read the Vision statement as follows: “Working together to create a connected
 5   active transportation system for all ages and abilities.” He explained that the individual goals
 6   include prioritizing safe routes, creating a connected backbone network, collaborating with the
 7   public, and access to key destinations. These goals guided the work performed for the study.
 8   Mr. McMurtry pointed out that Cottonwood Heights has more existing active transportation
 9   facilities than any of the other participating cities, and is starting from a place of strength. He
10   presented a graphic illustrating the existing facilities and trails in all of the participating cities.
11
12   He reported that they looked at several other factors, including bicycle fatalities and Strava data
13   in developing the plan. They found that there was a lot of heavy use on Cottonwood Canyon
14   Trail, general biking across Wasatch, and use of the Jordan River Parkway trail. They conducted
15   a bike tour early on to look at some of the facilities and different design types and the tour
16   allowed them to experience what cyclists do, which in turn helped them with the planning
17   process. Mr. McMurty presented the infrastructure included in the ATP, which included bike
18   lanes and neighborhood byways, protected bike lanes, and shared-use paths.
19
20   On March 9, 2020, they hosted a Brainstorm Workshop where participants from the various
21   communities marked up maps that resulted in a new consolidated map, which he presented to the
22   Commission. They also invited the public to attend the Brainstorming Workshop and
23   emphasized that they considered everyone’s input. They had over 650 people participate in the
24   exercise. Their efforts continued remotely after the pandemic struck, which included Zoom
25   meetings and interacting via the websites. He reported that they received over 5,000 public visits
26   to the website and over 900 responses to their surveys. The public came up with over 550 new
27   ideas that were drawn on the maps during these meetings. In sum, Mr. McMurtry felt that even
28   with the pandemic, they received good participation from the public.
29
30   Mr. McMurtry reported that some of the input was specifically from Cottonwood Heights
31   residents regarding the anticipated use of walk/bike facilities, if available, and 46% of residents
32   responded that they would walk daily, while 38% said they would walk weekly. These numbers
33   were higher than in other cities. He outlined the recommended projects and noted that they
34   recommended 240 projects across all of the participating cities. A major theme for all cities was
35   connectivity, and he identified the recommended backbone projects, which are those of the
36   highest priority. He commented that several of the most difficult backbone projects were carried
37   forward into some level of design, and highlighted Project No. 7 along Fort Union Boulevard in
38   Cottonwood Heights. Mr. McMurtry commented that the recommended project list was still in
39   draft form and they continue to make changes. Their goal is to have the Planning Commission
40   issue a recommendation to the City Council to adopt the plan.
41

44   separated bike lanes through a section of Fort Union Boulevard. The specific section depicted is

33          1.2    Cottonwood Heights Parks, Trails, and Open Space Master Plan.
34
35   Mr. Johnson recalled that the Commission, along with the Parks, Trails, and Open Space
36   Committee, made a positive recommendation on the Parks, Trails, and Open Space Master Plan
37   several months ago. It was presented to the City Council and a public hearing was held the
38   previous month. He reported that one part of the Plan received a significant amount of public
39   input and concern so they prepared a proposed amendment as directed by the Council. He
40   explained that the scope of the Plan was to provide a long-range broad vision to help future City
41   leaders make recommendations as it pertains to budget decisions, City policies, new City codes
42   and ordinances, and development requirements as they relate to parks, trails, and open space. He
43   stressed that the Plan was not a construction plan but includes recommendations in the maps
44   included within the Plan.
45

 1   Mr. Johnson reported that at the request of the Council, they added an acknowledgment of
 2   private property rights, which states that the City respects private property rights and the Plan
 3   does not give the City authority to make improvements that would impact private property rights.
 4   The amendment also specifies that the Plan does not give the City eminent domain authority to
 5   make any of the improvements outlined in the Plan and the City has no eminent domain authority
 6   when it comes to parks, trails, and open space. He stated that this was not recommended as an
 7   implementation strategy; rather, any future improvement envisioned in the Plan that affects
 8   private property would either be required during substantial redevelopment or through real estate
 9   negotiations with willing landowners. The Council also requested references to nationally
10   recognized park standards, which would help justify recommendations for more park space or
11   trails consistent with those benchmark standards.
12
13   Mr. Johnson addressed the Future Trails Chapter, as it was the part of the Plan that received the
14   most public interest. The purpose of the chapter was to envision interconnected active
15   transportation and recreational trail network that connects neighborhoods to City and regional
16   parks to other cities. He added that they envisioned a trail network through the City that could
17   be used by casual recreationists as well as a legitimate means of transportation. They also hope
18   to provide access to public lands for City residents and visitors without interfering with the
19   quality of life of nearby residents.
20
21   Mr. Johnson reported that the Little Cottonwood Trail caused a lot of concern from
22   neighborhoods along that trail alignment. The Council was also very concerned about the
23   original alignment. He noted that the original intent of the Little Cottonwood Trail was
24   envisioned as a long-range trail improvement where, over a long period of time, land would be
25   set aside as areas aged up and were redeveloped. Most of the trail alignment crossed private
26   properties, which was the reason it was included as a long-range vision. Mr. Johnson
27   commented that there was concern and confusion over this vision, as many believed it was a trail
28   development plan. The Council communicated that staff needs to provide an alternate solution
29   to that particular future trail.
30
31   Mr. Johnson presented the alternative and explained that it would still involve a trail envisioned
32   in the area of the Little Cottonwood Creek; however, they moved it off the waterway alignment
33   and instead proposed that it be located along public rights-of-way. It remains a long-range
34   project but by locating it in public rights-of-way as an urban trail system, they felt it would create
35   much less conflict and concern over private property rights and privacy. This change also allows
36   them to achieve their goals of an interconnected trail network. He stressed that this is not a
37   design document at this point. Issues regarding accommodating active transportation with
38   recreational uses would be addressed at the design stage. He added that the alternative trail plan
39   is now in the draft Plan that is published online.
40
41   Mr. Johnson provided examples of urban trails and stressed that they are only visual examples of
42   different trail types and what they could look like. He referenced public concern about increased
43   crime with urban trails. He presented the Big Cottonwood Trail that runs near some residential
44   properties in the City and noted how the different configurations achieved the same desired
45   result.
