Location and Description
The city occupies 12.6 square miles (32.6 km²). Riverton shares its borders with South Jordan to the
north, Draper to the east, Bluffdale to the south, and Herriman to the west. It is located in the
southwestern corner of the Salt Lake Valley.


Population
The city of Riverton had a population in July 2018 of 45,600, 1 an increase of 14.4% from the April 2010
census, which reported a population of 44,419.

Demographics
The city of Riverton is a growing community characterized by its diverse demographics. The median
household income in Riverton is approximately $115,869, reflecting the city’s economic stability and
attraction for families. The poverty rate in the area is around 3.5%, which is lower than the national
average, indicating that most residents have access to economic opportunities. The median age of males
in Riverton is about 33 years, while for females, it is slightly higher at around 35 years. 2 This age
distribution suggests a family-oriented community, with many residents in their prime working years.
Overall, Riverton’s demographics depict a prosperous and thriving suburban environment. 90% of the
population speak English at home, 5.4% speak Spanish, and 1.8% speak Asian and Pacific Island
languages. 3


Brief History
The area that is now the city of Riverton was settled in the 1850s. The people lived in crude, widely
scattered dugout homes along the river bottoms. Archibald Gardner was the largest landowner, and he
might have been the first to live on Riverton land, though early accounts disagree. Because of this, the
land along the Jordan River and the surrounding area was called Gardnerville. Due to the lack of irrigation
water, initial growth was slow. However, the town began to grow as settlers developed a cooperative to
build a ditch, which was later expanded to a canal. This expanded cultivation, which attracted new
residents. Riverton became incorporated into a town in 1948 and a city of the third class on July 3, 1967.

By 1914, Riverton began to prosper as an agricultural community, due to additional water and people.
Riverton’s business district also thrived. In 1879, a judicial precinct was established, and the settlement’s
name was officially changed from Gardnerville to Riverton by Judge Charles Smith. The first
meetinghouse was constructed in 1879, which also served as a church and a schoolhouse. A new
meetinghouse was planned, and the architect selected was Richard Kletting, who also designed the Utah
State Capitol. The entire community worked to haul material, by wagons, including granite from Little
Cottonwood Canyon. The Old Dome Church, as it came to be known, was used by the residents until it
was torn down in 1940.

In the late 1800s, Riverton farmers gradually changed from self-sufficient to commercial farming. They
specialized in alfalfa, wheat, sugar beets, tomatoes, poultry, sheep, and dairy cows. At this time, the LDS
Church began storing tithed produce and livestock on land at 1150 West 12400 South. This area is now
known as Tithing Hill. In 1912, electricity came to Riverton, and in 1913, the Salt Lake and Utah Railroad
(Orem Line) passed through Riverton west of Redwood Road. It stretched from Salt Lake to Payson and
was used as a commuter and freight line. Riverton had its own train depot, and trains used this line from
1914 to 1945, after which the rails and ties were removed and the depot was torn down.

The city of Riverton has worked with residents to continue traditions such as the annual Town Days
event. Residents in the Salt Lake Valley have quick access to many recreational activities. The Wasatch
Mountains, ski resorts, and Utah Lake are just a short drive to satisfy the city’s many active residents.


Climate
The average high temperature is 67.3°F, and the low temperature is 43.1°F, with an overall average of
55.2°F. The city gets 16 inches of rain each year and 43 inches of snow. Although warmer than many
other places in Utah, the city of Riverton has around 133 days each year in which nighttime temperatures
drop below freezing, and it has an average of 46 days that reach a temperature above 90°F.


Public Services
The city of Riverton provides a full range of services to its residents and businesses. General
governmental services include building inspection, construction, and maintenance of street lighting,
roadways, and parks, as well as recreation and cultural events. The city also provides utility services for
culinary water, secondary water, sanitation, and stormwater. In July 2019, the police department was
created.


Governing Body
Riverton, Utah, is a third class city as defined by the Utah State Code and operates as a “six-member
council” form of government. That is, the governing body consists of a mayor and five council members
(see: Utah Code § 10-3b-301). City Council members are elected for terms of four years. The city council
holds all authority to adopt ordinances, pass resolutions, adopt annual budgets, regulate zoning and land
development, establish city fees, create long-range plans for city services and utilities, and set regulatory
standards for providing other city services. The mayor serves as chair of the city council and chief

Development Trends
According to its website, the city of Riverton is one of the fastest-growing cities in America. It has grown
from a rural farming town into a suburban city. Businesses, housing, and roads have replaced many
farms and cattle ranges. The growth was supported by the opening of the Intermountain Riverton Hospital
in November 2009 and the Mountain View Village shopping center in 2018. The city anticipates continued
growth and business expansion while maintaining its 30 community parks. Much like the rest of the
southwestern corner of the Salt Lake Valley, rapid growth in the community is anticipated in the coming
years. To encourage new businesses to come to the city, business licensing fees were eliminated in
2018.

prioritizing risks in a given context. It considers various factors, such as probability, impact, and urgency,
to determine the level of risk associated with events or situations. The results for each hazard, including
its risk factor (RF) value, are shown in Table 4. The results are based on the criteria in

Table 5 and the equation that follows it. The CPRI helps organizations and individuals make informed
decisions about risk management and mitigation strategies. It provides a systematic approach to
identifying and addressing potential issues, allowing for a more efficient allocation of resources and
proactive risk prevention. With the CPRI, stakeholders can prioritize their focus on the most critical risks,
leading to more effective risk management and, ultimately, better outcomes.

   RISK FACTOR (RF) EQUATION

RF Value = [(Probability x 0.30) + (Spatial Extent x 0.10) + (Severity of Life/Property Impact x 0.30) +
(Warning Time x 0.10) + (Duration x 0.10) + (Response Capacity x 0.10)]


Hazards with an RF value greater than or equal to 2.5 are considered high risk. Those with RF values of
2.0 to 2.4 are considered moderate risk hazards, and those with an RF value less than 2.0 are considered
low risk. The highest possible RF value is 4.

Additional Public Involvement
Public involvement in Riverton, Utah, is crucial for enhancing community resilience to various hazards,
including public health epidemics and natural disasters. Community newsletters effectively disseminate
vital information, keeping residents informed about ongoing initiatives, upcoming events, and safety
protocols. Programs like Firewise and StormReady proactively engage citizens in hazard awareness
campaigns, educating them on ways to mitigate fire risks and severe weather risks. Severe Weather
Awareness Week raises community consciousness about potential threats, encouraging preparedness
and proactive planning. In addition, school programs play a vital role in instilling hazard awareness in
younger generations, fostering a culture of safety and resilience from an early age. Through these
collaborative efforts, Riverton strengthens its collective response to emergencies and enhances public
health and safety outcomes.

Riverton provided several opportunities for public participation in the update of this plan. Besides the City
Journal mailer that went out to residents, an announcement of the plan update and link to public outreach
survey was posted on social media sites. Figure 1 shows examples of public outreach.

Plan Integration
Incorporating the underlying principles of the Hazard Mitigation Plan and its recommendations into other
plans is a highly effective and low-cost way to expand their influence. All plan participants will use existing
methods and programs to implement hazard mitigation actions where possible. As previously stated,
mitigation is most successful when it is incorporated into the day-to-day functions and priorities of
government and public service. This plan builds on the momentum developed through previous and
related planning efforts and mitigation programs, and it recommends implementing actions where
possible through these other program mechanisms. These existing mechanisms include the following:
