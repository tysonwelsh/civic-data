31   2.0    Transportation Master Plan (“TMP”) Discussion.
32
33   Vice-Chair Mills reported that Public Works Department Staff would share information about the
34   Transportation Master Plan (“TMP”). Staff had been working with a contractor on the plan details.
35   The City Council had been involved in the process as well and there had been a lot of back and
36   forth. It was now time for the Planning Commission to review what had been done so far.
37   Commissioners would also be able to provide some feedback and direction to Staff. Vice-Chair
38   Mills reiterated that a lot of excellent work had been done on the TMP. He was excited about the
39   presentation and noted that TMP information was included in the Meeting Materials Packet. There
40   were also some relevant attachments and resources that could be reviewed for additional context.
41
42   Staff Engineer, Adam Ginsberg, shared the Cottonwood Heights TMP. He explained that this was
43   something the City has been working on for the last two years. It was noted that a TMP is a
44   unifying document that includes previous plans from the City. This would be the first for
45   Cottonwood Heights and incorporate a lot of the previous plans that had been created, including
46   the most recent 2019 Capital Facilities Plan. In that particular plan, there was a transportation



     Cottonwood Heights Planning Commission Meeting – 07/05/2023                                    1
 1   model that went out for 20 years. The TMP would go out to 2050. It would also include the Mid-
 2   Valley Active Transportation Master Plan and future development plans. Mr. Ginsberg explained
 3   that the model included the Wasatch Front Regional Council (“WFRC”) TAZ model. The TAZ
 4   model was a model that analyzed the population in zones.
 5
 6   There were many goals associated with the Cottonwood Heights TMP, including the following:
 7
 8      •   Improve Cottonwood Heights transportation planning and implementation, prioritizing
 9          efforts based on community-identified mobility goals;
10      •   Identify future projects that will improve pedestrian safety, walkability, and traffic flow;
11      •   Improve existing active transportation network to promote alternative transportation use;
12      •   Consolidate various transportation policy documents under one integrated plan;
13      •   Develop Typical Roadway Cross-Sections for different road classifications;
14      •   Develop a Transportation Model to anticipate roadway level of service up to 2050; and
15      •   Identify solutions to accommodate future growth, road safety, and road classification.
16
17   Mr. Ginsberg reported that several iterations of the TMP were reviewed by the City Council. Staff
18   presented a draft version to the Council in November 2022. Comments were received and it was
19   brought back in January 2023. Another presentation was made in April 2023. Staff was now
20   presenting the final draft of the TMP to the Planning Commission for input and comment. The
21   final draft of the TMP would be adopted by the City Council in the future. During the back and
22   forth with the Council, clarifications were made to the active transportation information.
23
24   Additional information was shared about the modeling data. There was a graph showing the
25   population of Cottonwood Heights over the years based on the census data. The TMP anticipated
26   roadway Level of Service (“LOS”) to 2050 utilizing that data. A lot of the estimated population
27   growth that was expected in the future was the result of the gravel pit development. Mr. Ginsberg
28   further discussed the TAZ model. It used socio-economic data, which meant that it included the
29   people who lived in the specific zones but also considered the impacts of people who came into
30   the City to work and shop. This meant there was both population and employment data.
31   Commutes would show in the traffic counts, but commutes that passed through the City would not
32   show on the TAZ model. The WFRC made the model and it included the entire valley. The
33   different zones influenced one another, so commuter traffic patterns were taken into account.
34
35   Mr. Ginsberg discussed the existing roadway network. Last year, the City updated the
36   classifications for different roadways and there were many types. This included everything from
37   residential to arterial. Urban core and urban arterial were added. Based on the different
38   classifications, the TMP recommended various cross-sections. Fort Union Boulevard has a special
39   cross-section based on the previous Fort Union Corridor Master Plan. Some examples of urban
40   collector two-lane roads included 2300 East, 1700 East, and Greenfield Way. Those were the
41   smaller collector roads that were feeding into the larger arterial roads. Vice-Chair Mills asked
42   about Highland Drive. Mr. Ginsberg confirmed that Highland Drive is a City road. It was shown
43   as an urban core arterial seven-lane and an urban core arterial five-lane. The updated cross-
44   sections were shared. Mr. Ginsberg reported that the two-lane local would be a residential road.
45   Additional examples of two-lane urban collector roads were shared with the Commission.

 1   Mr. Ginsberg presented information about bicycle lanes and explained that they are lanes
 2   designated for bicyclists. They differ from sidewalks because sidewalks function for pedestrians
 3   as well. Bicycle lanes that are located on the road function only for bicyclists. That being said,
 4   there are different types of bicycle lanes, including shared bicycle lanes where the road is shared
 5   between automobiles and bicyclists. There are also dedicated bicycle lanes where there are clear
 6   markings to indicate that only bicycles can utilize those lanes. There had been an effort to include
 7   bicycle lanes in future cross-section designs. One of the challenges was that the roads have already
 8   been built. However, the active transportation program had a lot of options for bicycle lanes,
 9   including trails, cycle tracks, and bicycle lanes on the roads.
10
11   Mr. Ginsberg discussed Fort Union Boulevard and explained that it is a three-lane urban arterial.
12   As for five-lane and seven-lane urban arterials, they will be 1300 East and 3000 East. He noted
13   that a seven-lane urban core arterial example is Highland Drive between Creek Road and the
14   bottom of the hill. Additional information was shared about Fort Union. There are two different
15   cross-sections. The one at the bottom showed a six-foot sidewalk and a six-foot bicycle lane on
16   both sides of the road. That would be a shared-use path with room for both pedestrians and
17   bicyclists. Above that, the urban core arterial Fort Union Boulevard three-lane is from 3000 East
18   to Wasatch Boulevard. That cross-section shows a bicycle lane in the roadway and identified a
19   multi-use path. That was a planned improvement from 3000 East to Wasatch Boulevard on Fort
20   Union Boulevard, which would connect to the Big Cottonwood Canyon Trail. Vice-Chair Mills
21   asked how much of the TMP was connected to the Fort Union Master Plan. Mr. Ginsberg
22   explained that the second cross-section came from the Fort Union Master Plan. Community and
23   Economic Development Director, Michael Johnson, noted that the Fort Union Master Plan was
24   high-level. The TMP is a document that attempts to determine what things will look like in more
25   detail.
26
27   The TMP included an analysis of the roadway and intersection LOS. That data was based on the
28   daily traffic volumes collected. Mr. Ginsberg explained that the LOS represented the congestion
29   and delays on the roadway or intersection. The best LOS was an A and a severely congested LOS
30   was an E or F. Everything up to LOS D was considered acceptable. Once the LOS was an E or
31   an F, there start to be failures on the roadway. Mr. Ginsberg reported that the data was mainly
32   from previous data that had been collected. He noted that Horrocks Engineers collected the data
33   back in 2019, but there was also data that the Utah Department of Transportation (“UDOT”)
34   collected on an annual basis, which had been considered as well.
35
36   For the roadway LOS, there are acceptable volume levels. For the intersections, the time delays
37   are considered. The signalized intersection delay is a factor, which was the seconds per vehicle.
38   35 to 55 seconds would be a LOS D. Mr. Ginsberg explained that the model focuses primarily on
39   the roadways and looked mostly at the acceptable volume levels on the roads.
40
41   Commissioner Chappell wondered if there was any data related to public transit service. For
42   instance, the number of buses that come through in high-traffic areas. She wanted to know if those
43   numbers impacted the LOS ratings. Mr. Ginsberg reported that the volume was considered but he
44   did not know whether there was separate data collected from the Utah Transit Authority (“UTA”).
45   He shared the existing LOS map with the Planning Commission. Green was considered
46   acceptable, with a LOS A, B, or C, and orange was considered acceptable, with a LOS D. An LOS

 5   Commissioner Ebbeler noted that some of the traffic data was from 2019 and 2020. He wondered
 6   if an update had been considered. It might be worthwhile to compare pre-COVID-19 traffic
 7   patterns to post-COVID-19 traffic patterns. Mr. Ginsberg reported that there was a 30% reduction
 8   in traffic during the pandemic but that had largely returned to normal. He did not believe there
 9   were plans to collect new data for the TMP. Commissioner Ebbeler pointed out that some people
10   work in an office, some remote, and some hybrid. Those changes could impact the overall traffic
11   patterns over time. He wanted to know if the model data had considered those factors.
12   Mr. Johnson noted that the WFRC Regional Transportation Plan goes out 30 years in the future
13   but is updated every few years. Regular updates ensure that the trends are accommodated. It
14   would be possible to proactively plan for trends in the General Plan, but in terms of the actual data,
15   it was important for the City to update the TMP on a fairly regular basis.
16
17   Commissioner Mike Smith wondered about extrapolating data from 2020 since that was when the
18   COVID-19 pandemic hit. Mr. Ginsberg clarified that the report was put together in 2020 but the
19   data was collected in 2019. Commissioner Smith did not believe it was possible to determine what
20   life would be like in 2050. He agreed with the projections being made now and adjustments being
21   made every few years. That being said, he anticipated that electric bicycles would continue to
22   become more popular. The bicycle lane issue might become more pressing in the future when
23   more people utilize or want to utilize bicycle lanes. Vice-Chair Mills referenced the piece of land
24   at Hillside Plaza. He was not sure exactly what that would look like in the future. For instance,
25   whether it would be busy with traffic or would be quiet. Mr. Ginsberg explained that the TMP
26   could be updated as those plans became finalized. This was a living document that could
27   incorporate future plans. There was discussion regarding Bus Rapid Transit ("BRT") in the east-
28   west corridors. Mr. Ginsberg noted that the WFRC has a long-range plan that includes transit,
29   roadways, and active transportation. That plan identified a future BRT. Commissioner Steinman
30   believed that having central hubs and a BRT would be beneficial.

32   Mr. Ginsberg reviewed the recommended roadway projects. He explained that the table shown
33   was broken up by phasing as Phase 1 (2023-2032), Phase 2 (2033-2042), and Phase 3 (2033-2050).
34   The location of the project, the type of improvement, and the projected phase of unacceptable LOS
35   were listed for each item. There were two projects with an identified unacceptable LOS, which
36   were 2300 East and Park Center Drive. The recommended improvement for Park Center Drive
37   was to reconfigure the roadway from two lanes to three lanes. As for 2300 East, the
38   recommendation was to improve pedestrian safety and walkability and reconfigure the roadway
39   from two lanes to three lanes. Commissioner Lucy Anderson asked about the 2300 East pedestrian
40   safety and walkability improvements. It was noted that there would likely be a sidewalk installed.
41   Other items from Phase 1 included the Fort Union Boulevard and 2300 East intersection, Highland
42   Frontage and La Cresta Drive intersection, and Highland Drive and Fort Union intersection.
43
44   In Phase 2 of the recommended roadway projects, there were several items listed, including Fort
45   Union Boulevard (300 East to Wasatch Boulevard), Wasatch Boulevard, 2600 East, Danish Road,
46   and 3000 East. All of those are operational projects to improve pedestrian safety, walkability, and

11   Vice-Chair Mills referenced item 3.4 – Fort Union Boulevard (1300 East to 3000 East). He was
12   disappointed to see that item so far down the list. He heard a lot from residents that there was a
13   desire to have more walkability on Fort Union Boulevard. Vice-Chair Mills discussed a potential
14   roundabout at 2300 East and Fort Union Boulevard. He also referenced the property that had been
15   purchased. Mr. Ginsberg explained that it was something that would need to be looked at further.
16   Mr. Johnson reported that the City had funding to do a site-specific Master Plan for that location,
17   which would lead to redevelopment. Part of the scope of that process was looking at ways to make
18   the transportation network around it more conducive to a town center type of environment. Vice-
19   Chair Mills understood that it would depend on the tenant. He was not sure that residents would
20   be supportive of a big box retailer there, but those types of uses generated a lot of taxable income.
21   Additional discussions were had about traffic patterns and the overall LOS for roadways.

23   Commissioner Smith noted that there were spots within the City that had sidewalk gaps. This
24   impacted walkability within Cottonwood Heights. Mr. Johnson explained that all of the funding
25   sources were regional, such as WFRC, UDOT, and Salt Lake County. The projects typically
26   needed to appear on one of their plans and be of some sort of regional significance. Filling gaps
27   in the sidewalks did not typically rise to that level. The City had applied for something similar
28   further down on Creek Road but had not received the funding. That meant that it would need to
29   be added to a local priority list for the City Council to consider. Mr. Johnson reported that work
30   was moving forward on 1700 East where there was an extended sidewalk gap. However, he
31   acknowledged that there were areas like that throughout the City that needed to be addressed. Mr.
32   Ginsberg informed Commissioners that the City was always looking into potential grants.
33
34   Commissioner Chappell appreciated the TMP discussions that had taken place so far. As for the
35   roundabout on 3500 East and Bengal Boulevard, she lives in the area and there is a bus pickup on
36   3500 East and Creek Road. She had previous discussions regarding safer places for bus stops and
37   was told to look for accident records so there would be a case to ask for traffic calming in the area.
38   She wondered if it was possible to downgrade a road. For example, 3500 East could be
39   downgraded to a local road, because it was a low-capacity road and it did not necessarily make
40   sense as a collector. She reiterated that there were existing safety concerns there and wondered
41   whether it was possible to change the road type. Mr. Ginsberg explained that the possibility of
42   downgrading a road had not been explored in the TMP. 3500 East is a unique situation because it
43   is not a high-volume road but has a wide pavement section. He noted that there was a traffic
44   calming process that looked at accident data and traffic volume, but ultimately, everything came
45   down to the different priorities and budgets that were set by the City Council.
