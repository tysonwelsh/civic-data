Item 5.1 was consideration of Resolution 2026-10 Approving an Agreement with Perkins
Engineering, Inc. for Engineering Services. Assistant Chief Engineer, Ben Nelsen, reported that the
Agreement is for mill and overlay design on Highland Drive from Bengal Boulevard to the Starbucks
and from Dan’s to the La Cresta Drive intersection. It does not include the Fort Union Boulevard
intersection. They will also identify any necessary Americans with Disabilities Act (“ADA”)
improvements within the project area.

Mayor Bennion remarked that it also includes looking at additional striping for a third lane.

on Highland Drive, as that study is still in process. As part of the agreement, Perkins Engineering
will evaluate the roadway to determine if it would be feasible to allow the third lane to continue
slightly farther north to maximize the existing road space and mitigate some of the congestion caused
by decreasing from three lanes to two. In response to a question raised by Mayor Bennion,
Mr. Nelsen confirmed that the agreement does not include construction to add a third lane.

Council Member Birrell stated that as she read it, the contract was based on Phases II and III of the
Highland Drive project. Mr. Nelsen clarified that it is a separate project. The scope of this project
includes road maintenance and considering how far the third lane can be extended with striping
changes. It does not include any widening.

        3.3     Blue Sky Program Discussion – Council Member Shawn Newell.

Council Member Newell stated that his goal is to achieve clarity on the Blue Sky and Utah Renewable
Communities (“URC”) programs. From his research, the URC program is mirrored by the Blue Sky
program that already exists through Rocky Mountain Power. He asked why the City would initiate a
program for all residents when they already have the ability to participate without any additional cost
to Cottonwood Heights. He has heard from a lot of residents who want to be part of the program, but
they did not know that the Blue Sky program exists. It has the same parameters, except there is no
cost to the City.

He is also concerned that if residents opt out, it may affect those who stay in the program by increasing
their rate. If everyone is in the program, the highest estimated cost per resident is $48 per year. If
50% of residents opt out, that cost increases to $96 annually. If another 25% opt out, the cost will be
$192 per resident per year. To his understanding, as new renewables come on board, the cost is
calculated by Rocky Mountain Power and added to the Rocky Mountain Power tariff rates paid by
program participants. He does not support subjecting residents to additional costs without being clear
about them. Rather than asking residents to involuntarily take something on that they must voluntarily
opt out of, he believes it would be better to make them aware of the existing program they can opt
into without a concern about escalating rates.

Council Member Newell stated that residents who already have solar panels will have to pay the same
rate unless they opt out. He understands that residents will be notified, but he questioned how many
people would read their bills to find that out. He believes it would put both the City and residents in
a precarious situation. He is also concerned about the additional renewable energies that are being
proposed. For example, Rocky Mountain Power customers will pay for the nuclear plant planned for
northern Utah. He wants residents to know about the Blue-Sky program in addition to the URC
program, and he does not want to be responsible for putting them into one program when another is
available to them right now via opt-in. He will likely opt in because he agrees with the program goals,
but he does not want to put that burden on all residents without complete clarity on how the program.

Mayor Bennion thanked Council Member Newell for the discussion and noted that everyone on the
City Council wants to review all positive and negative aspects of each option. She welcomed URC
representative, Emily Quinton, to the discussion. Ms. Quinton is the Summit County Sustainability
Director. Summit County is part of the URC, along with Cottonwood Heights and 17 other

communities. She has served as the URC Board Secretary and been involved in the process since its

URC is a coalition of 19 communities working to develop the program in partnership with Rocky
Mountain Power. Under the proposed program, participating homes and businesses would have a
new option for where their energy comes from. The clean energy in the program is proposed to come
from both existing clean energy resources and new utility-scale projects built to serve URC customers.
The URC has the potential to be transformative in the development of significant clean energy
resources and was made possible by the Utah Community Renewable Energy Act passed in 2019.
That legislation and subsequently adopted rules defined the program as one where participants are
automatically enrolled with the option to opt out. Ms. Quinton remarked that Cottonwood Heights is
not the first community to express concerns about that aspect of the program.

The URC program will create an additional choice for clean energy. They understand that not all 19
communities will remain in the program, but those 19 communities represent approximately 25% of
Rocky Mountain Power’s electricity sales in Utah.

Ms. Quinton provided an overview of the program.

    •   Upon program launch, customers in participating communities:
            o Remain Rocky Mountain Power customers receiving reliable power.
            o Will be automatically enrolled in the URC program with the choice to opt out. As
                proposed, there will be no cost to opt out initially. After that, there will be a fee to
                terminate participation.
    •   The program cost is anticipated to be less than $4 per month for the average household and
        will be added to the customer’s bill.
    •   The follow additional components were proposed:
            o Qualifying low-income customers will receive a monthly bill credit to offset the
                additional cost to participate. If they choose to leave the program, the termination fee
                will be waived.
            o Rocky Mountain Power will send two notices to each customer to alert them of the
                program before a charge begins. The notices must be separate from the monthly bill.
            o Additional education and outreach to be provided by the URC Communications and
                Low-Income Plan Committees.

After the Community Renewable Energy Act was passed in 2019, communities became eligible to
participate in the program by adopting a renewable energy goal. That requirement had since been
removed from the legislation. Nineteen communities signed a Governance Agreement in 2021. A
Utility Agreement was also drafted outlining the roles and responsibilities that will take effect upon
program approval and launch. The negotiation process between URC and Rocky Mountain Power
was lengthy. URC originally expected to piggyback off of PaciCorp’s systemwide solicitation for
renewable resources, but that was cancelled in 2024, and a new solicitation had to be developed. The
next step is program approval, which is expected at any time, followed by adoption of the program
by participating communities within 90 days of approval.

In response to a question raised by Council Member Newell, Ms. Quinton confirmed that the Board

Newell asked why a household would choose to participate in an involuntary program for which the
cost is uncertain. Ms. Quinton clarified that the cost cannot be determined until the Public Service
Commission approves the program. Their order will specify the initial rate, so that information will
be provided at the start of the 90-day adoption window.

Regarding how URC compares to existing renewable energy options, it is anticipated to cost
approximately 50% less per unit of energy than the Blue Sky program. The Blue-Sky program
provides grants for community projects like the solar panels on the roof of the Cottonwood Heights
City Hall. Summit County also benefited from the program and intends to continue to support it while
pushing for the larger, utility-scale change that URC has the potential for. Rocky Mountain Power
also has the Utah Subscriber Solar program, which had limited enrollment and is not currently open
for subscriptions. That program built one solar plant but has no expansion plans. Residents can also
install their own rooftop solar systems. Depending on their situation, homeowners and businesses
have those different options available to them. Summit County’s interest in URC is based on its
potential to create community-scale renewable energy.

Council Member Newell pointed out that 60% of the cost burden will be placed on businesses.
Cottonwood Heights does not have much commerce, and he was concerned that those costs would be
borne by residents. Ms. Quinton reported that there will be administration and startup costs, but the
primary expenses will be in creating the new renewable energy facilities. Those costs will be
distributed across customers by rate class and on a per-kilowatt-hour basis, not by municipality. To
avoid the worst-case scenario of mass opt-outs, they plan to bring new renewable energy sources
online incrementally. They are negotiating contracts with four potential providers and intend to start
with one resource. That will not be enough to meet the program’s total goal, but it will keep the rate
as low as possible so the program can stabilize during the initial opt-out period. This should prevent
any rate shock.

Mayor Bennion stated that she has heard that the program is flexible, and URC’s goal is to keep the
program cost between $3 and $4. To do so, they may choose not to start a new project until the
program can support it without increasing cost. Salt Lake City has a lot of low-income residents who
might not be in the low-income program. They do not want it to be an expense for those residents.
However, there are environmental costs. If the airshed can be improved through this program, it will
decrease health and other costs that cannot be quantified.

Council Member Newell expressed concern about the unknown issues on top of the many existing
