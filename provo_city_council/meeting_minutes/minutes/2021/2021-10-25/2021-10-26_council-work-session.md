2.      A discussion on percent for the arts programs. (21-098) (0:23:30)

Cliff Strachan, Council Executive Director, presented. Mr. Strachan reviewed the past Council
discussion on this topic. The basic concept of percent for the arts is to provide funding for public
art by setting aside a specific percentage of capital improvement projects to be dedicated to the
arts. Mr. Strachan reviewed several policy decision points for the Council, including formulating
a specific policy and identifying the person or body assigned to review, plan for, and make
recommendations for the program. Mr. Strachan highlighted the process used in several other
Utah cities and shared details of the policies in those municipalities. Mr. Strachan reviewed other
elements that are critical to percent for the arts programs.

Councilor David Harding commented that utility boxes with unique art contribute to the look and
feel of a downtown or other area. He felt the Council should not immediately eliminate the
possibility of a percent for arts for utilities and infrastructure. Mr. Strachan commented on this
suggestion with some insight about how other cities have handled this particular policy decision.
If the Council did not want to pursue a percent for arts program, there are other program formats
which could be used to designate funding for the arts, such as establishing an arts fund, utilizing
RAP tax funds, and other types of donations or grants. In response to a question from Councilor
George Handley, Mr. Strachan illustrated some of the distinctions between a public art fund and
grants. Mr. Strachan summarized by listing some of the possible actions the Council could take.
Mr. Handley was interested in initiating a program like this. Mr. Strachan suggested establishing
the committee in January, once the current year of committee assignments has wrapped up.

Motion:         George Handley moved to create an ad hoc committee to explore and develop a
                recommended policy regarding a percent for arts program, and to defer selection

Councilor Bill Fillmore thanked Mr. Strachan for his presentation and indicated his general
support for public art. Mr. Fillmore was concerned about committing the City by an ordinance to
an arbitrary amount of 1% per year. He referenced some financial constraints which had been
imposed on the State Legislature and he hoped to avoid a similar situation with the City’s
financial commitments. He wondered about a more discretionary approach that could leave
allowance for other budgetary constraints and priorities.

Mayor Michelle Kaufusi liked the idea of a percent for the arts program, but she thought that
additional parameters were needed. She noted that 1% of all CIP funds would be an enormous
sum; she thought the City could formulate an effective program that didn’t have an outsized
budget. Wayne Parker, CAO, echoed Mayor Kaufusi’s comments. He agreed that 1% of CIP
would be out of scale for a city of Provo’s size. He thought doing a review or possibly a public
art master plan would be helpful. Mr. Strachan added in response to Mr. Fillmore’s comments
that those same concerns have come up from many department directors. As the committee
explores the parameters of a percent for the arts program, they could consult department
representatives to work through these related issues.

The Council discussed the timing of the committee formation and selection of the committee
chair and vice chair. Mr. Handley revised his motion to reflect the intended timeline. Mr.
Handley also stated his intent that the committee include department representatives.

Councilor David Harding suggested a simple policy that permits the necessary flexibility. He
suggested 1% of capital improvement costs and allowing the City Administration the ability to
determine use of that 1%. Mr. Harding was also open to limiting the 1% to the costs of buildings,
parks, and streetscapes. Mr. Strachan shared another possible approach of adopting intent
language in the budget or city financial policies.

Councilor Shannon Ellsworth supported the creation of a committee. She thought the committee
should work through the details, include citizen involvement, and determine a scope for use of
the funds. She noted that art can be interpreted broadly or narrowly, and as a recent example,
there was not a lot of citizen engagement in the early stages of planning the water feature. Mr.
Strachan noted that a reality of the situation was that opinions will differ on what constitutes art;
having an Arts Council in place to aid policy and decisionmakers will help.

3.      A presentation from the Kem C. Gardner Institute: "A Time to Build: Physical,
        Human, and Social Capital" (21-094) (1:18:03)

Natalie Gochnour, Director of the Kem C. Gardner Institute, presented. Ms. Gochnour explained
that investment and productivity encourage growth in the economy or a business context. Provo
City is currently dealing with the growth dynamics of Utah County, which could have a negative
impact on the community if certain investments are not made in order to deliver on the promise

of a prosperous community. Ms. Gochnour commented on growth, structural changes, and
decline in institutions. She elaborated on what decline in institutions means, including real
decline in certain institutions as well as decreased or lack of trust in various civic, religious, and
community institutions. Ms. Gochnour reviewed population changes in Utah and highlighted
some different factors. Utah has now reached the rank of a “mid-sized” state. In 2010, Utah was
the 34th largest state and in 2020 it is the 30th largest state. Utah County has grown about 28% in
the last decade, with Wasatch County as the highest growth county in the State with almost 50%
growth. As the County seat, Provo is one of the centers of tremendous urban growth seen in the
area. Councilor David Harding asked about Utah County’s internal growth and Ms. Gochnour
indicated this figure would include county-to-county interflow—it includes all forms of
migration, including from another county, state, or country.

From 2019 to 2021, the country saw decreased jobs and increased unemployment. The opposite
was seen in Utah. Utah County experienced even higher job growth and lower unemployment
than the state average. Ms. Gochnour shared a graph of national infrastructure spending, which
has fallen significantly as a percentage of the GDP. Ms. Gochnour isolated Utah infrastructure
funding; the trends are different, but it has fluctuated over time. Mr. Harding expressed that this
presentation was very helpful and informative. Ms. Gochnour said the presentation was
developed for the business community, but certainly had a lot of application in the public sector.

Ms. Gochnour highlighted some types of structural changes, including behavioral, technological,
demographic, and new paradigm changes. With a lot of structural changes, investment in people
is important. Education is one area of investment in people. Ms. Gochnour shared statistics of
Utah youth mental health and suicide indicators from 2013, 2015, and 2017. Each of the
indicators has increased (i.e., worsened) over time. She thinks investment in people relates well
to the Council’s consideration of the future of the City’s neighborhood program. Ms. Gochnour
defined social capital as a network of trust which allows society to function successfully. The
Joint Economic Committee in Congress has examined about 42 factors to calculate social capital
for each state and Utah has extraordinary levels of social capital in the country. In sum, Utah can
turn the incoming challenges of growth, structural changes, and decline in institutions into
competitive advantages if leaders and policymakers invest in physical, human, and social capital.

During the presentation, Ms. Gochnour referenced the writer Yuval Levin and his book “A Time
to Build: From Family and Community to Congress and the Campus, How Recommitting to Our
Institutions Can Revive the American Dream.” Councilor George Handley said he had read and
enjoyed Levin’s book and Mr. Handley asked Ms. Gochnour why she thought it would look like
for Provo to invest in human and social capital. He noted that the Council is not in the business
of education, but from a municipal government perspective, he wondered what the Council could
do to invest in human and social capital. Ms. Gochnour referenced work by Raj Chetty
(https://www.nber.org/papers/w23001) which showed the effects of neighborhoods on the long-
term outcomes of children. She also referenced the work of David Brooks, who has also written
about using neighborhoods as a building block for creating greater social outcomes. She thought
these sources had great information that could inform the Council’s approach. Councilor
Shannon Ellsworth asked a more subjective question regarding the role of government and use of
the limited resources of a municipality in this sphere. Ms. Gochnour felt that government exists
to help create a more prosperous society for the people. She thought that the way the government

more detail, highlighting different parameters as outlined in the ordinance. Section 9.90.050
outlines the different methods by which a parking area can be designated, including the process
for a resident-initiated application.

5.      An ordinance on towing fees (21-105) (0:53:14 and 2:18:35)

Cliff Strachan, Council Executive Director, presented. Mr. Strachan reviewed the draft ordinance
and highlighted the changes based on the rates in state code. The proposed ordinance would tie
the City rate to a percentage of the State rate, so that the rate would automatically adjust when
the State rate was adjusted each year. Mr. Strachan noted that several Councilors asked for more
information about how other cities have banned patrol towing; Councilor David Shipley said that
he was satisfied with the information sent about the mechanism employed by Salt Lake. Mr.
Shipley felt that Provo’s ordinance made sufficient allowance for an appeal process and a fair
approach. Councilor Bill Fillmore echoed these comments and thanked Brian Jones, City
Attorney, for his very thorough research on the question.

Councilor David Harding commented on the nature of contracts which can allow the owner of
the property to waive the tow fee. Mr. Harding gave a background of patrol towing as it was
tackled by a previous Mayoral Administration under former Mayor John Curtis in 2013. He was
hesitant to make extensive changes to something that was heavily vetted by previous elected
officials. Councilor David Sewell felt this proposed change was not to undo what was done in
2013 but to protect what was done in 2013. He thought that imposing a fixed fee on a business
and never adjusting it for inflation was problematic and introduced potential legal problems for
the City. Wayne Parker, CAO, said Mr. Harding gave a good overview of the towing issues in
2013. He said the City was loath to interfere in the contractual relationships between towing
operators and property owners. The City chose to intervene in other ways, including by requiring
notice (onsite and in apartment contracts) and to try to distinguish between predatory towing by
requiring the default of a call-per-tow and a higher threshold for patrol towing. It is a delicate
balance and it took a long time to work out in 2013. Early enforcement was helpful in setting the

The Council returned to further discussion of this item after the previous items on the agenda

Mr. Harding commented and thanked Brian Jones, City Attorney, for clarifying the definitions of
patrol and predatory towing. Mr. Harding commented on those distinctions; he felt that allowing
patrol towing was necessary to allow private property owners to protect their property interests
and usage. Mr. Parker thought removing the statutory limit on the towing fees might result in
increased types of towing behavior that city officials had tried to limit. A fixed fee incentivized
proper behavior and he thought as long as it is below state maximum (as with a percentage), it
was effective. Mr. Parker elaborated on his concerns with restricting this business type—he
thought it created a slippery slope if the city became involved in controlling contractual
relationships between two private parties. The City tried to create a better environment in which
this business operated by requiring more information to be provided to drivers about the issues
and consequences. The current law also had a mechanism for property owners to relieve the
burden on those who were unjustly treated. If a landlord was too constrained by the policies, it
could push parking onto the street and create a burden for landlords. Mr. Parker thought a
reasonable limit on towing fees probably achieved the right balance. Mr. Handley clarified that
he did not suggest the City control contractual relationships; rather, his concern was about
whether the change was necessary. Mr. Parker thought that question probably merited an
examination of the number and whether the percentage selected struck the balance or whether it
was better to reevaluate on an annual basis. Councilor Travis Hoban asked what the difference
was to the larger fee if the Council moved to the 80% of the state rate. Mr. Strachan replied that
it would result in a fee of $19.40 more with the rate at 80%. Presentation only. This item was
already scheduled for the Council Meeting on October 26, 2021.
