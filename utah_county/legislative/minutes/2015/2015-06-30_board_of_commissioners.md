                                             PUBLIC COMMENTS
American Fork City Councilmember Brad Frost addressed the proposed Mountain Accord land exchange for commercial
ski development in American Fork Canyon. He noted many books have been written about the canyon, and the decision
facing the commissioners will add a chapter in the history for the area. Mr. Frost initially believed the dissenters of
Mountain Accord were a very loud, well-organized minority of the public, but has realized differently over the last few
weeks as concerned citizens reach out to him in favor of preserving American Fork Canyon. Mr. Frost stated all sides
of the issue agree that boundaries have been overstepped, and expressed his hopes that the commissioners will unite in
taking a stand for the community.

Commissioner Ellertson clarified that the Board is aligned in their thinking that decisions for American Fork Canyon
should be made here, in Utah County. Commissioner Lee added they are currently drafting a resolution to be presented
during the next meeting.

Mark Allen, representative of the group “Protect & Preserve American Fork Canyon,” talked about owning American
Fork Canyon Vision (AFCVision). He explained AFCVision has decided to return $50,000 to Snowbird in order to send
a message they will not tolerate corporations acting as legal entities and claiming jurisdiction for land decisions. Mr.
Allen offered to begin a fund with a personal donation of $500 for Commissioner Ellertson to be able to return the 2012
campaign contribution monies from Bob Bonar, Snowbird CEO and President. He named several other local politicians
that could be considered socially connected to Snowbird representatives. Mr. Allen asked the leaders to stand together
and represent their people.

Heather Williamson of Saratoga Springs is concerned about the legislative proposal for a quarter-cent local sales tax for
transportation funding as part of the Fuel Tax Increase House Bill 362. She read a letter urging the commissioners to
vote against placing this sales tax option on the Utah County ballot this upcoming November. Ms. Williamson noted
taxpayers across Utah are already facing a five-cent-per-gallon increase to the motor fuel tax, a $75,000,000 property
tax increase, and additional city and school district property tax increases this Fall. She stated fourteen percent of the
personal income for Utahns go to taxes and fees, and the county has the third highest combined sales and use tax rate
in the state (6.75%). Ms. Williamson commented general sales taxes should not be used to fund transportation, and noted
the unfair advantage to online retailers not having to pay taxes. She mentioned forty percent of the revenues from the
sales tax increase would go towards funding Utah Transit Authority (UTA) projects instead of fixing and maintaining
roads; UTA already receives $33,800,000 in sales tax revenue from Utah County. Ms. Williamson reiterated her plea
to refrain from placing a sales tax increase on the ballot, suggesting the Board ask state lawmakers to instead create a
transparent user-fee model.

Orem resident Sharon Anderson commented on Regular Agenda Item No. 2, expressing concern that additional
information is not readily available to the public regarding the request for a letter in support of military training events.
She stated that while most of the community supports the troops, that does not imply complete agreement with military
objectives or those who issue rules of engagement. Ms. Anderson questioned the true nature of these training events,
specifically the potential involvement of civilians and moving local law enforcement departments to rely on federal
resources. She also asked whether this agenda item is related to the United States Armed Forces ‘Jade Helm’ exercise
taking place from July through September 2015. Ms. Anderson warned against moving the country towards a police
state, and asked the Board to fully investigate issues before endorsing them.

Saratoga Springs resident Becky Pirente opposes development in American Fork Canyon, any military exercises
occurring amongst residents in Utah County, and the proposed tax increase which would make Utah County the highest
taxed region in the state. Ms. Pirente mentioned that if a tax raise is desperately needed to provide for the transportation
needs of the county, it surely was a mistake to divert funds for the recently approved Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit
(BRT) project. She is concerned about giving such a high proportion of transportation revenue to the Utah Transit
Authority (UTA), and would prefer to see the funds spent on local roads.

Pleasant Grove City Mayor Mike Daniels addressed Utah House Bill 362 - Transportation Infrastructure Funding, stating
he supports 60% of the bill: the forty percent designated for local roads, and an additional twenty percent for county
roads. Mayor Daniels questions the remaining 40% for the Utah Transit Authority and Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit
project, and believes the matter should be placed on the November 2015 ballot for a vote by the people. He reminded
the Board that state legislature drafted the bill, hence any comments or concerns should be directed to the legislators.

Vic Devano did not identify his hometown. He spoke of the 1994 realignment of roads near 1200 South in Orem, near
Utah Valley University - the changes were desperately fought by some residents and city and county officials at the time,
but now the interchange serves as one of the most vital traffic developments in Utah County. Mr. Devano attended the
state legislative session this year and was surprised at the lack of media coverage and absence of public representation.
While he does not want the quarter-cent tax imposed, Mr. Devano stated it should be determined by a vote of the people.
He does not support the private handling of transportation.

Lehi City Councilmember Mark Johnson expressed support for the comments made by Pleasant Grove City Mayor Mike
Daniels - Utah House Bill 362 should be put to a public vote. Lehi City is concerned about the transportation issues in
the valley; some of the infrastructure is failing and is in vital need of repair. Citizens should take the opportunity to
research the facts and make an informed decision.

Randi Powell, Lindon City Councilmember, noted that Lindon was one of the first cities to pass a resolution in support
of House Bill 362. The Utah League of Cities and Towns has worked hard to pass this legislation, and Ms. Powell hopes
the citizens recognize it is very difficult for municipalities to obtain funds for transportation infrastructure. Lindon has
55 miles of roads to maintain, and have only been able to pave less than one mile in the last year. Ms. Powell explained
that the best roads are a priority in order to maintain them for the longest period of time and for the best quality. She
asked the Board to make sure the citizens are fully informed before taking a vote, because while paying taxes are a chore
it is also difficult to drive on rough unkempt roads.

Provo resident Lisa Clark is opposed to any tax increase, and would like the Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit (BRT)
project to be placed on a ballot for a vote. Ms. Clark believes 95% of Utah County residents are unaware of BRT, and
is against the large sum of money being spent on an eleven-mile route which only half consists of dedicated lanes. She
stated this is not rapid transit; it is bigger busses with fewer stops on an extremely busy that is already overcrowded. Ms.
Clark is upset about the impending changes to her Cherry Lane neighborhood, stating she is not against public
transportation but there are already busses on that route and Utah County residents have not been given the time to learn
about this project.

Eagle Mountain resident Wendi Baggaley referred to a 2007 Sales and Use Tax amendment to question how many more
times the taxes will go up “just a little bit more.” Utah County has grown significantly in the last eight years, so
additional funding has also come from taxes. Ms. Baggaley was against this transportation tax increase until the Eagle
Mountain Mayor released a statement regarding the need for local road improvement monies, but is still concerned that
the county is accepting unnecessary stipulations in order to receive that funding. She finds the Utah Transit Authority
(UTA) to be financially wasteful and inefficient, especially the six-figure salary for their Chief Executive Officer. Ms.
Baggaley acknowledged that the county has needs but must use the money wisely.

American Fork resident Steve Nau stood to support the Board in taking action against any development in American Fork
Canyon. He asked the commissioners to set an example for the cities and towns, reminding them that Utah County can
be just as powerful and intimidating as Salt Lake County. Mr. Nau thanked the Board for the work completed so far and
encouraged them to continue.

Commissioner Lee mentioned attending an executive meeting of the Mountain Accord group seeking the development
of ski areas in American Fork Canyon; they have acknowledged that Utah County needs to be more involved in the
process. Commissioner Ellertson confirmed having a similar conversation with group representatives in the past week.

Willie Holdman offered to escort the commissioners on a hike in American Fork Canyon. He spoke of visiting Mary
Ellen Gulch with his son, an area proposed for development by Snowbird Resort LLC. Mr. Holdman was impressed by
the beauty and dispersed camping available; if Snowbird acquires the land, all recreation will be fee-based. He read a
portion of a Mountain Accord document which states they will not supercede local authorities, calling them contradictory
to their attempts to exchange Utah County land without involving the authorities. Mr. Holdman declared if the
commissioners go with him to see the canyon, they would also want to save it.

Alpine City Administrator Rich Nelson encourages a public vote on the proposed quarter-cent sales tax increase. Alpine
receives approximately $320,000 for road projects and does not have revenue-generating businesses.

Diane Christensen is a Provo resident who would like to see the quarter-cent sales tax increase put to a vote of the people.
There have been many recent increases in Provo including utility rates, a proposed recreational tax, and the approved
Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) bonding. Ms. Christensen is also concerned that forty percent of the quarter-cent
increase is designated for Utah Transit Authority and BRT when the public was repeatedly told during the process that
BRT will not raise taxes.

Scott Sandstrom expressed that the American Fork Canyon Vision process has been tainted by expressing a focus on
bringing a gondola to the area. Mr. Sandstrom proposed that the Board be involved in starting over with the Mountain
Accord, whether by renaming the group or identifying the stakeholders. The agreement with the Mountainland
Association of Governments has not involved any public input or information.

Christine Ivins, Highland Resident reminded the Board she had suggested at the previous Commission meeting that they
visit American Fork Canyon. She reiterated that request, and thanked the commissioners for their hard work in
preserving the canyon. Utah County resident Eldon A. Neves came forward to echo that appreciation, thanking
Commissioner Lee specifically for attending a meeting about transportation issues and thanking the Board members for
their work.

Springville resident and Utah County Voting Precinct District 11 delegate Cynthia Hallen stated that while a petition
against the resolution for the Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit project had been unsuccessful, the BRT discussion is not
over. Ms. Hallen acknowledged the importance of fixing roads, suggesting the BRT project be cancelled in order to
address road maintenance. She also spoke of the unique beauty and wildlife habitat of Tibble Fork in American Fork
Canyon, asking the Board to prevent any disturbances to the area.

Riley Madsen is a Lehi City resident in favor of preserving American Fork Canyon. Ms. Madsen said, “That canyon has
been my life; my absolute homestead, my escape.” She spoke of camping and off-roading in the Mineral Basin area,
commenting on the passion of the citizens to protect the splendor and asking the commissioners to ‘keep our homes safe.’

Provo resident Linda Roper collected signatures in May and June of this year for the petition against the resolution for
the Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) project. Ms. Roper explained how 95% of the individuals she spoke with were
unaware of BRT and had no idea of the plans for their tax dollars. The quarter-cent sales taxes were set aside for roads
and highways; now the money desperately needed by the cities is being diverted to BRT. Ms. Roper noted previous
declarations that BRT would not cause a tax increase, but that is exactly what is happening. She pointed out that the
public would have loudly voted against BRT if they had been given the chance.

Lehi City Administrator and Saratoga Springs resident Jason Walker expressed Utah House Bill 362 should be put to
a public vote. People do not often have the opportunity to be involved in legislature for local government, and it would
send a message to the politicians to find better solutions if this bill does not pass. Mr. Walker mentioned hailing from
Hawaii and seeing the consequence of low sales taxes there and the lack of transportation infrastructure damaging their
economy. The best option is to educate the people and allow them to participate in the democratic process to make
informed decisions about their taxes.

Stan Harvey, Provo resident, shared a story of his mother-in-law saying “if you put enough good stuff on it, even rocks
taste good.” Mr. Harvey equated this anecdote with the recent acceptance of the Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit (BRT)
project. He suggested the county not get carried away with the promise of benefits which actually hide tax increases.

Orem City resident Claude Richards is running for City Council because he feels obligated to be a voice for the people
that are getting frustrated with tax increases and other changes in government. Mr. Richards noted approving additional
taxes is similar to adding barnacles to a ship; one barnacle does not halt operations, but eventually the entire hull must
be scraped. He spoke about the unfair tax burden upon citizens and suggested the Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit (BRT)
issue be returned to a public vote, questioning the rush to accept federal funds while the nation is bankrupt. Mr. Richards
stated “we are taxed enough.”

Highland City Mayor Mark Thompson had encouraged local legislators to identify additional monies because it is four
times more expensive to lay asphalt now than it was in 1997, when the last gas pump tax increase was approved. Mayor
Thompson supports the proposed quarter-cent sales tax increase and supports the inclusion of the issue on the ballot.
He pointed out that no one wants a cell tower in their backyard, but everyone has a cell phone in their pocket. Additional
revenues are needed for thriving communities, and the infrastructure must be maintained.

Merlin Weekes, Orem resident, is pleased to see people standing to speak about important issues. He appreciates the
discussions surrounding American Fork Canyon, taxes, and the Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit project. Mr. Weekes
reminded the Board they were elected to represent the people, and have a responsibility to educate the public in their
votes. He acknowledged the difficulties the commissioners face.

Lindon City Administrator Adam Cowie lives in Payson and commutes across the county daily for work, so he has seen
first-hand the need for road repair. Mr. Cowie is in favor of the transportation tax, and the issue being placed on a ballot
for public vote.

1.     DISCUSSION REGARDING IMPLEMENTATION OF UTAH HOUSE BILL 362 - TRANSPORTATION
       INFRASTRUCTURE FUNDING
Commissioner Ellertson summarized Utah House Bill 362 as being passed by the legislature to give an opportunity for
public information and to be put before the counties throughout the state as a ballot issue. Utah County has invited the
Utah League of Cities and Towns, as well as Mountainland Association of Governments, to present different aspects of
the proposed bill today.

Utah League of Cities and Towns Director of Government Relations Cameron Diehl came forward with Orem Mayor
Richard Brunst and Santaquin City Councilmember Keith Broadhead. Mr. Diehl noted some cities in Utah County have
passed resolutions supporting the fourth-quarter-cent sales tax, explaining this conversation is about comprehensive
transportation is taking place throughout all 29 counties in Utah. The local option is in recognition that local
governments are struggling to maintain infrastructure with their current resources. Mr. Diehl presented some statistics
on the costs of transportation needs.

Orem Mayor Richard Brunst introduced Orem City Councilmember Margaret Black and Orem City Manager Jamie
Davidson and mentioned that the City Council had voted 6-1 to place House Bill 362 on the ballot. Mayor Brunst noted
the importance of this issue to the cities as represented through the number of mayors and city administrators who made
public comment today. He pointed out Orem’s population has grown forty percent in twenty years, yet the road monies
have decreased by fifty percent. Mayor Brunst stated at least 12 Utah County cities have passed or will pass a resolution
in support of House Bill 362: Alpine, Cedar Fort, Elk Ridge, Fairfield, Highland, Lehi, Lindon, Orem, Payson, Provo,
Santaquin, Saratoga Springs, and Spanish Fork. He also mentioned that 86 cities and towns in 15 counties have passed
or will pass similar resolutions in favor of this first comprehensive transportation bill in the entire state.

Santaquin City Councilmember Keith Broadhead introduced members of City Administration, and expounded upon Orem
Mayor Richard Brunst’s comments. Santaquin City voted 5-0 in favor of the resolution to support House Bill 362.
Councilmember Broadhead provided historical background and statistics for projected growth in Utah County. Mayor
Brunst added that providing roads is a priority concern for Orem residents, encouraging the Board to place the fourth-
quarter-cent sales tax on the 2015 ballot. Andrew Jackson of Mountainland Association of Governments (MAG) then
presented an informative slide show (a copy of which can be found attached to these minutes) emphasizing roads, taxes,
and the benefits of this proposed tax increase.

Utah County Public Works Director Richard Nielson commented on road maintenance costs and gas tax funds,
presenting a slide show to demonstrate deterioration and repair costs (a copy of which can be found attached to these
minutes). Utah Transit Authority’s Steve Meyer summarized the transit improvements which would take place if House
Bill 362 is enacted, including a focus on connectivity, route convenience, and frequency. He mentioned no portion would
be used for the Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) project, explaining BRT is funded through third-quarter-cent sales
tax revenues, which will in turn be reimbursed, with interest, through first-quarter-cent sales tax revenues.

Commissioner Graves excused himself from the meeting at this time. Commissioner Lee summarized Utah House Bill
362 is a new tax increase. Andrew Jackson of Mountainland Association of Governments confirmed that the proposed
fourth-quarter-cent is a new sales tax. Commissioner Lee asked whether Utah County would be the highest sales-taxed
county in the state if no other counties enact House Bill 362. Cameron Diehl answered that Utah County could
hypothetically be the highest if it were the only county to adopt the bill, but all 29 counties are considering approval and
are all within one cent of each other ranging from 5.85 to 6.95 percent. Currently, Utah County is not the highest taxed;
Commissioner Ellertson noted the Wasatch Front communities are higher, and Mr. Diehl confirmed Weber and Salt Lake
Counties both have higher tax rates than Utah County.

Commissioner Lee referred to Regular Agenda Item No. 1 on today’s calendar, commenting on the irony of promoting
local retailers with a proclamation while in the same meeting also considering an increased sales tax which would
essentially drive out those businesses. He also contended that a fourth-quarter-cent sales tax increase is directly tied to
the Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) project in Utah County, seeing as the third-quarter-cent sales tax revenue from
2009 through 2014 had previously been used exclusively for road funding. Commissioner Lee noted the exception of
$2,800,000 spent to study BRT and the debt service used to pay back the Utah Transit Authority (UTA) for roads.
Andrew Jackson of Mountainland Association of Governments countered that the third-quarter-cent is not designated
solely for roads, pointing out it can also be used for airports, highways, and transit.

Commissioner Lee noted previous assurances by those in favor of Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit (BRT): as recently
as eight months ago, proponents had indicated that road funding in Utah County would be sufficient to pay for needed
road projects through the year 2028, and that using third-quarter-cent sales tax revenues to pay for BRT would not harm
current road projects. Now less than a year later he is being presented with graphs and charts claiming the roads are
falling apart, and infrastructure collapse is imminent without a tax increase. Andrew Jackson of Mountainland
Association of Governments commented on the difference between quarter-cent taxes and direct allocations, stating third-
quarter-cent sales tax funds can only be used for new capacity of regional and state road and cannot legally be used for
road maintenance.

Commissioner Lee questioned why state legislators were asked to dismantle the third-quarter-cent silos in 2014, and
Andrew Jackson defined the old requirements: 40 percent of revenues were to be spend on state roads, another 40 percent
on regional roads, and the final 20 percent on rights-of-way. Mr. Jackson stated the funding for regional roads was
insufficient in comparison to state road funding, and removing the silos helped provide the flexibility needed to ensure
higher prioritized regional road projects receive funding before lower prioritized state road projects. Commissioner Lee
summarized the purpose of removing the old silos was to provide flexibility and funding for roads, not other projects.
He acknowledged the need for local road funds but also voiced skepticism over the lack of passion for those monies when
BRT was being presented as a priority over other local road projects between now and the year 2028.

Commissioner Ellertson mentioned this issue would be brought forward for a public vote, reminding Commissioner Lee
that he had previously been adamant about allowing the people to vote on previous issues. Commissioner Lee responded
that requiring a public vote prior to implementation of the fourth-quarter-cent sales tax is the “saving grace” behind this
proposal. He also commented on the hypocrisy of those who claimed less than one year ago funding was sufficient to
pay for both much-needed road projects and the Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit project.

Commissioner Lee suggested Utah House Bill 362 would ideally be returned to the legislature without the Utah Transit
Authority (UTA) portion attached so that road needs can actually be addressed, calling the current proposal an ice cream
cone with “60% good ice cream, but 40% bugs.” Commissioner Lee stated the legislature does not want to enact the bill
statewide because of the political heat. He also commented on the confusing nature of opinion poll questions, arguing
that the public often does not understand that the results are binding. Commissioner Lee noted that some mayors have
focused only on the roads portion of this proposal, but it must be discussed in its entirety when addressed - the forty
percent for city roads, the forty percent for UTA, and the twenty percent for county roads - in order to truly understand
what is being presented. He encouraged the Board to reject this proposal.
