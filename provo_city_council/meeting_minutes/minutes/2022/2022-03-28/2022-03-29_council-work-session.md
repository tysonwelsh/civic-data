1.       A discussion regarding redistricting adjustments to City Council District maps. (22-
         003) (0:09:26)

Karen Tapahe, Community Relations Coordinator, presented. Ms. Tapahe provided an update on
the Open City Hall survey topic. Councilors shared comments on maps included in the final
selection. Several Councilors indicated they did not support the map which resembled the status
quo. The Council discussed the remaining maps to identify maps without broad support to cut
those from the running. There was mixed support for map 34 so the Council moved on to
discussing other maps. Maps 75 and 67 were both eliminated. Many Councilors expressed
similar concerns about how both maps divided areas of the city which had common interests.
Several Councilors also felt that map 75 looked gerrymandered.

eliminated. He made a substitute motion to that effect to move the discussion along.

mitigated some of the issues with district 2, and it has a solid downtown district. He knew there
were some Councilors with concerns about including Grandview with the west side; he was open
to hearing more from Councilors with concerns about this map. Ms. Whipple noted that this
map’s district 1 was closest to the status quo—there were only two precincts that were different
for district 1. Mr. Shipley felt that this map moved in the right direction from the status quo; it
was his least favorite from the west side perspective, but it moved in the right direction. He said
that he had a slight preference for map 113 over this one. With the growth and connectivity
issues facing the west side, he felt it was an area that needed a Councilor who could be hyper-
focused as it is built out and connected into existing city services. Mr. Fillmore did not like map
34 due to the boundaries of district 1 which felt unnatural to him.

2.      A presentation regarding the FY2023 Budget-Council Office. (22-016) (1:41:02)

Melia Dayley, Policy Analyst, presented. Ms. Dayley highlighted several key achievements of
the Council over the past fiscal year, including preservation of open space, redistricting, the
general plan update, and passing a southwest intent statement. The fiscal year 2022 budget made
several restorations to the Council budget, including the internship position, contract services,
and the travel and training budget for attending conferences. Notable requests for the fiscal 2023
budget include upgrades to the new Council Chamber for Zoom meetings, a small inflationary
increase for goods and services, and reclassification of the policy analyst position.

Councilor Travis Hoban felt that given the nature of hybrid meetings moving forward, it was
imperative to have a Zoom room-style facility with the technology to do this easily. Council staff
have had a preliminary discussion with Information Systems about the current plans for the new
building—the decisions about the technology were made before the pandemic, so these needs
had not been anticipated during those early discussions. Councilor Shannon Ellsworth suggested
the Council do this prior to the formal budget process so the changes could be implemented
before the new building is complete. She hoped the Council could vote on this in April. Chair
Hoban indicated that he sensed there was unanimous support for this on the Council. Ms. Dayley
shared additional details on the likely amount for an appropriation. Brian Jones, Council
Attorney, highlighted the process for bringing forward an appropriation. Mr. Hoban also noted
that this building was going to be used for the next 50-70 years—it was important to easily
accommodate resident participation in public meetings as well as easily accommodating hybrid
meetings for future councilors and staff. In response to a discussion on some of the logistics,
Wayne Parker, CAO, said they have not yet received the change order for this request. He also
noted that the Legacy CIP in general fund balance was a potential funding source the Council
could use. Scott Henderson, Project Manager, felt this was a positive development and he noted
the infrastructure to facilitate this was already in place.

Regarding the reclassification of the policy analyst position, Mr. Hoban explained how this
would work—it would not place a limit on the earning potential of a staff member who was at
the high end of the range. John Magness, Interim Executive Director, would be working with
Human Resources on this. Presentation only.

Mr. Peterson reviewed the funding that Downtown Provo, Inc. has received from the city in the
past. DPI received $150,000 in 2011. This later decreased to $125,000 and was down to $75,000
in 2015. He took over as director of DPI six years ago and have been operating with a budget of
$75,000 every year since then. As the offerings and programs run by DPI become more robust,
they are trying to do a lot with very little resources. He formally requested the Council to
increase their funding from $75,000 to $100,000. He noted that part of what makes DPI so
successful is that they are a scrappy program and a non-profit. As a result, an additional $25,000
would go a very long way for DPI compared to other potential uses. He noted this budget would
be used for recurring programs like murals and possibly vinyl wraps for utility boxes.

Councilors were very supportive and thanked Mr. Peterson for the work he does and the impact
DPI has had in revitalizing the downtown area. Presentation only.

presentation showing many Parks and Recreation projects and how these have supported various

and Recreation operations and their vision in more detail. The Provo Recreation Center has been
deemed the most successful recreation center in the nation in a study by an external agency, with
over 2 million visitors annually and 25,000 memberships. Mr. Henderson shared the successes of
the Peaks Ice Arena and the Timpanogos Golf Course.

Customer Service is open Monday through Friday. The many services they offer include:

They are celebrating their 10-year anniversary in May. They have overcome many challenges
and been able to grow into a great operation.

Their primary goal with billing is to distribute utility bills on time with correct information. The
billing division’s seven employees have 102 years of combined experience. Ms. Ercanbrack
outlined details of the billing operations—there are 18 different billing cycles so payments come
in on a regular basis rather than on the same day. They are considering consolidating this a bit,
but it involves two different meter management systems for power and water. It’s a complex
operation and the staff do a great job. She highlighted several recent or in-progress projects
including Neptune 360 (a water metering and billing system) and the recent launch of Time of
Use and SharedSolar rates with the Energy Department. They are also looking into condensing
their billing cycle to avoid weekends and holidays.

The call center has 17 employees and a very detailed training program. Laramie Gonzales,
Business Manager, handles their stats and analytics. He reviewed call center metrics and
highlighted some of the types of data they track in the call center. They answer an average of
91,000-112,000 calls per year; about 75% of calls are utility-related and the other 25% is
anything from zoning, business licensing, parking, to sanitation. In 2020 during the pandemic,
there was an increase of calls up to 111,000. They also implemented a new billing system that
year. People got used to doing business online and call volumes have since decreased slightly.
Mr. Gonzales reviewed details of the Customer Service budget and how this is broken out to the
different operations. Their budget is planned to decrease by about $132,000 due to several
maintenance contracts which are no longer needed. Councilor Rachel Whipple asked about
Information Systems staffing as it relates to Customer Service; IS provides support to Customer
Service. Council Chair Travis Hoban thanked them for the great job they do. Presentation only.

6.      A resolution adopting the Mountainland Pre-Disaster Hazard Mitigation Plan. (22-
        036) (3:28:11)

Chris Blinzinger, Emergency Management Coordinator, presented. In the event of a natural
disaster, the FEMA response is contingent on the hazard mitigations that Provo has in place.

Councilor Bill Fillmore asked for some clarification about some information in the plan; Mr.
Blinzinger indicated that this figure referred to infrastructure and facilities that could be
potentially damaged in a catastrophic event. Mr. Fillmore also asked about failure of Deer Creek
Dam. Mr. Blinzinger explained that they do not have concerns about dam failure—it is well-
constructed and routinely evaluated. The primary concern is if something catastrophic were to
happen upstream of Deer Creek, that Deer Creek is not designed for flood control and that is
what could cause issues. Fire Chief Jim Miguel also explained that there are hundreds of people
from throughout the region who share these concerns and are planning for these potentialities.

Each year there is a complex management strategy employed during spring runoff to mitigate
issues associated with higher river and water flows.

Councilor Rachel Whipple expressed that she is concerned about the hazards presented by
unreinforced masonry building. Mr. Blinzinger highlighted a mitigation program called “Fix the
Bricks,” which was started in Salt Lake City and has gained recognition by federal partners. He
suggested asking the State of Utah to support this program; it involves a combination of funding
sources including grants and insurance money. Having a program at the state level would help
with getting more resources and exposure about the opportunities.

Mr. Blinzinger indicated some of the training he has done with department directors about
emergency operations; when the Council hires a new executive director, he would like to provide
some additional training for them. Mr. Fillmore asked about Provo’s possible liquefaction issues.
Mr. Blinzinger highlighted the geographic and terrain issues that impact Provo. He noted that
mud slides are more of an issue with serious burn scars from wildfires and subsequent
rainstorms; Provo is relatively safe in that regard. Jared Penrod, Engineer, said they have some
monitoring sites on the east benches of the city; there have not been any significant changes in
the last several years. They also have a consultant who works with them on this monitoring.
Public Works does watch for potential problems when reviewing new developments and
retention basins, but in Provo they have not had any major fires. Ms. Whipple asked what types
of factors they evaluate when issuing permits. Mr. Penrod noted the 30% slope prohibition and
restrictions against creating steep slopes. Presentation only. This item is already scheduled for
the Council Meeting on March 29, 2022.
