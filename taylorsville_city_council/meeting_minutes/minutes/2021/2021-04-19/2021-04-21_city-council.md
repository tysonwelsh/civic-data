7.      OTHER MATTERS

     7.1 Resolution No. 21-10 – A Resolution of the City of Taylorsville approving a License
Agreement between the City and Google Fiber Utah, LLC – Mayor Kristie Overson

        Mayor Overson explained that Jacob Brace of Google Fiber was attending the meeting
electronically. Many in the City liked to say that Taylorsville was the city where community
connected. That was never truer than now, as the council had the opportunity to adopt a resolution
approving an agreement that would bring fiber optics to the City. She then pointed out that
Taylorsville would be the first city on the west side of the Salt Lake Valley to bring Google Fiber
to its residents and businesses.

Mr. Brace was very appreciative of the various city representatives he had already been working
with and he was happy to bring a wonderful connectivity resource to the city. It would help those
in Taylorsville connect to healthcare resources, businesses, and education opportunities. He
looked forward to continuing to work with the city on this endeavor.

Councilmember Harker wondered how long it would take for the technology to be available to
residents.

Mr. Brace responded that he hoped to provide the services as soon as possible but permits and
plans needed to be obtained and processes followed. Residents should expect to see work going
on in the right-of-way areas, but it was too early at this point to know when customer service would
begin. Construction of infrastructure should soon be visible.

Councilmember Burgess asked if the service would be in every home. Mayor Overson responded
that it was too early to know, although every home would certainly have the option of having it.

City Attorney Tracy Cowdell had asked the administration to expedite the project and Google had
been able to move things along quickly. He indicated that Google had been fantastic to work with
thus far.

Councilmember Barbieri agreed it was a very exciting proposition and sought a ballpark estimate
of when the service might be available. Mr. Brace responded that he suspected the first customer
would receive service by the fourth quarter of 2021 or the first quarter of 2022. The service would
be available to small businesses as well.

Mr. Cowdell explained that generally, the federal government regulated telecommunication
transmission services and was charged with authorizing cities to create franchise agreements.
There was not yet a provision in federal law for governing broadband since the internet was
unregulated. Therefore, the City had to work creatively with Google to create a license agreement
that did not mirror existing franchise agreement with services like cable television. In exchange
for allowing Google in the right-of-way, it would pay a license fee. More specifically, Google
would pay a 2% license fee to run their service in Taylorsville’s right-of-way. Other providers
had asked the city for money—Google had not. It had confidence in its business model, and he
strongly recommended that the council approve the agreement. He felt Google Fiber was the right
partner for Taylorsville.

Chair Cochran confirmed that unlike what other entities had suggested, Google was not requiring
the City to pay anything and in fact would be paying the City for use of its right-of-way. Mr.
Cowdell agreed that was correct.

        3.2 Preservation Opportunities – David Amott and Alena Franco

        Mr. David Amott of Preservation Utah wished to talk about some of the treasures of
Taylorsville’s history. Preservation Utah was once known as the Utah Heritage Foundation and it
advocated for historic preservation throughout the state. It also sought to promote advocacy in
order to advance preservation.

Preservation Utah had originally come to Taylorsville to work on the old assembly hall which was
currently housing the Al-rasool Islamic Center. More work needed to be done but among other
things, his organization intended to get this building on the National Register of Historic Places
which should make it eligible for state and federal tax credits. Such credits could be used to
preserve the building.

Mr. Amott went on to point out that Taylorsville had a rich stock of older buildings and he had
included a list of such buildings with his presentation information. There were Certified Local
Government (CLG) programs which were run by the National Park Service. These programs
involved the city donating $5,000 per year which was matched by another $5,000 grant given by
the Utah Historic Preservation office. Funds were then available for nominating buildings, adding
them to the historic registry, etc. He felt there were economic and ecological benefits, tax credits
and a resulting sense of pride in one’s community. Put plainly, the money that the City put into
the program would come back to it. He proposed that the City work with Preservation Utah to
bring the CLG program to Taylorsville.

Councilmember Harker asked if the grant funds could be used on the museum. Mr. Amott
responded that first the building would need to be placed on the national register. The Assembly
building was currently eligible since it was already on the register. In response to another inquiry
from Councilmember Harker, Mr. Amott explained that since Taylorsville already had a historic
preservation committee as well as a preservation ordinance, the City merely needed to designate
the funds in the budget. Ten thousand dollars every two years could result in a $10,000 grant. The
next grant cycle was in February 2022.

Councilmember Barbieri asked if Preservation Utah could help write the grants. Mr. Amott
responded that it could but explained that the applications were quite simple.

At the request of Councilmember Harker, Susan Yadeskie of Taylorsville’s Historic Preservation
Committee approached the podium and explained how important it was for us to know who we
are, where we came from and where we are going. There was strength and purpose in preserving
our identity. She felt the committee could accomplish a great deal with $5,000 per year. They
were a volunteer committee, and their hearts were committed to preserving Taylorsville’s history.

        5.1 Ordinance No. 21-03 – An Ordinance of the City of Taylorsville approving a
        General Plan Map Amendment from Business Park to Medium-Density Use for 24-
        30 acres of property at 4202 S Riverboat Road – Jim Spung

      Jim Spung explained that he would make a single presentation regarding ordinances 21-03
and 21-04 although the items would need to be voted on separately. The items were a General
Plan Map Amendment and a Rezone for the same property, which was in the Sorenson Research
Park, adjacent to the Jordan River. When the General Plan was originally adopted, the City had
designated the area as a business park. The owner of the currently vacant property had been in
contact with Ivory Development about potentially building residential units as well as a mixed-use

commercial development on the site. Their initial concept plan included 142 townhome units, two
commercial buildings each with a 20,000 square-foot footprint, a stand-alone fitness/daycare
center with a playground and multi-purpose field. The application was originally submitted in
early 2020 but had evolved significantly since then and was no longer in line with the City’s current
General Plan Map or Zoning Map. Moreover, the developer had notified the city staff that they
no longer wished to have a commercial component to their project. As for the request to rezone
the property, the applicant hoped to rezone the land from Industrial to Mixed-Use. Mr. Spung
indicated that both the Staff and the Planning Commission recommended denial of both updated
applications. Despite the negative recommendations, the applicant still wished to address the
Council to see if it would be interested in an all-residential project on the site in question.

Councilmember Armstrong asked what staff’s vision was for the property. Mr. Spung responded
that business park zoning would be the most appropriate. Councilmember Armstrong stated that
the planning commission existed for a reason and he was interested to hear what their concerns
had been. Mr. Spung indicated that the Commission had expressed interest in a site-specific
development (SSD) which was quite involved and required much more work by the applicant than
did a more typical zoning designation. It was riskier for the applicant to do a great deal of work
without knowing if the application would be approved.

City Administrator John Taylor pointed out that regarding the City’s vision of the property, it was
one of the few spots left for recreation along the Jordan River. Rezoning the property would affect
what could be done in the future. It was important to get the zoning right. At this point, the
applicant was only interested in building residential units. The Jordan River Commission had a
variety of ideas, none of which had been finalized, largely due to the number of different entities
involved.

Councilmember Barbieri explained that a site-specific zone was a great opportunity for the city to
do something unique and creative that might not fall within the parameters of traditional zoning.
Summit Vista was one such example. She suspected that the planning commission was interested
in an SSD.

Tracy Cowdell, City Attorney, explained that city administration had been having ongoing
dialogue with the property owner and much had changed over time. The applicant had asked to
have their project presented to the City Council, which was their right. The Council could agree
with the applicant’s plan or concur with staff and the planning commission.

Councilmember Burgess asked that the Council think long-term. He hoped the property would
remain as it currently was.

Mr. Spung agreed that the City should be thinking 20-40 years down the road. The property had
some physical constraints regarding the ability for emergency vehicles to access the area and also
a high liquefaction rate, the fact that it was in a flood zone and regulatory flood way, and finally,
utility access was very challenging. He indicated that SSD was not appropriate in this case because
the applicant was no longer proposing a mixed-use project.

    6. FINANCIAL MATTERS

        6.1      Resolution No. 21-14- A Resolution of the City of Taylorsville approving an
                 interlocal agreement between the City of Taylorsville and Salt Lake County
                 on behalf of the Salt Lake County Clerk Elections Division for 2021 Municipal
                 Election Services - Tracy Cowdell

       Tracy Cowdell explained that as Salt Lake County contracted with more and more
municipalities and special service districts to provide election services, the contracts had become
increasingly complicated. The contract included a “not to exceed” cost which typically was on the
high side of what would eventually be billed. It was staff’s recommendation to approve the
contract to allow the County to provide election services for 2021. It contemplated both a primary
and a general election.

Councilmember Harker inquired about Ranked Choice Voting. Mr. Cowdell explained that the
contract did not require a specific type of election. Any city that wished to utilize Ranked Choice
Voting would need to notify the County and the Lieutenant Governor’s Office by May 10.
Councilmember Harker felt that RCV was worth discussing. Mr. Taylor pointed out that Salt Lake
County had made it clear that any city choosing to hold an RCV election would be completely
responsible for marketing the new election type and educating the public. He also pointed out that
ballots could be mixed with some RCV and some traditional elections on them. He recommended
a traditional election. He felt that a change to RCV should happen at the county or state level so
that everyone was doing the same thing.

MOTION:          Councilmember Harker moved to approve Resolution No. 21-14, approving
                 an interlocal agreement between the City of Taylorsville and Salt Lake County
                 for election services. The motion was seconded by Councilmember Barbieri.
