adopt the 2025 Moderate Income Housing Plan as Part of the West Valley City General

Written documentation previously provided to the City Council included
information as follows:

       State code (subsection (2)(a)(iii) of Section 10-9a-403) requires cities to
       include a moderate income housing element or plan in their general plans.
       The moderate income housing element must include an implementation
       plan with a timeline for implementing selected strategies from State code.
       The City’s last update to our Moderate Income Housing Plan was adopted
       in 2022 using 2020 U.S. Census Bureau data, which was the most recent
       Census data available at the time.

       The proposed changes to the City’s Moderate Income Housing Plan were
       made primarily for two reasons. First, staff updated the analysis of moderate
       income housing within the City and other municipalities within the County
       utilizing 2023 Census data to reflect significant increases in home values,
       rents, and mortgage rates since 2020. The analysis also now includes a more
       comprehensive view of all income restricted housing units and not just tax
       credit units. Second, staff updated the implementation plan to reflect
       anticipated actions over the next five years. Since cities are required to
       provide annual reports to the State on progress made, an up-to-date
       implementation plan is needed. The implementation plan also no longer
       includes a strategy dealing with the City’s Housing Authority as this entity
       will become part of the County’s Housing Authority called Housing
       Connect in July of this year.

Mayor Lang asked why there were 9 strategies listed instead of the 5 that required.
Steve replied that the State is responsible for reviewing and certifying compliance.
In previous instances, the State has accepted some submissions while rejecting
others. To address this, extra measures are taken as a form of insurance to ensure
compliance even if one or two items are not counted. He emphasized the
importance of demonstrating to the legislature that the City is acting as a
responsible partner in addressing housing issues—not only meeting minimum
requirements, but exceeding them.


Councilmember Harmon asked for an example on Strategy F. Steve explained that
the City has been proactive in demonstrating compliance by highlighting projects
already completed. He noted that the State has so far allowed some flexibility in
recognizing these efforts and hopes this continues. The City's strategy includes

             residential use—and showing how projects are now progressing through the site

     Jim Welch, Finance Director, discussed proposed Ordinance 25-11 that would

                     -8-


amend the Budget of West Valley City for the Fiscal Year Beginning July 1, 2024 and
Ending June 30, 2025 to Reflect Changes in the Budget from Increased Revenues and
Authorize the Disbursement of Funds.

Written documentation previously provided to the City Council included
information as follows:

       State Statute Title 10, Chapter 6, Utah Code Annotated 1953, as amended,
       allows the City of West Valley to amend its budget during the year. The
       West Valley City holds public hearings on budget amendments on a quarterly
       basis each fiscal year.

Jim indicated that Councilmember Whetstone had asked several questions via email
regarding the budget opening. He addressed those as follows:

1. Storm Water Fund – What are the implications of converting the Storm Water
   Fund from an enterprise fund to a special revenue fund? How would this affect
   long-term financial reporting, fee structures, and capital planning?

   Jim clarified that the Stormwater Fund was initially categorized as a special
   revenue fund, which is purely an accounting classification and does not impact
   the fund's operations. When the city incurred debt for a special project and
   designated Stormwater revenues to repay that debt, the fund had to be
   reclassified as an enterprise fund. Despite this change, the city continues to
   maintain separate financial and operational records for the Stormwater Fund.
   The reclassification primarily serves accounting purposes by consolidating the
   fund’s assets with the city’s for overall financial reporting, while daily
   operations and asset management remain unchanged.

2. WVA Foundation Transfer – Why is $2.75M being transferred from the
   WVA Foundation Fund to the General Fund balance? The agreement
   presented last December outlined a transfer of $320K by year-end, and
   another $320K by April.

   Jim reported that when the West Valley Arts Foundation separated from the
   City and became an independent entity, it carried an accumulated operational
   deficit. As of the end of last year, that deficit was estimated at $2.1–2.2
   million; the current estimate is approximately $2.7 million, which includes
   two payments made to help sustain the foundation’s operations. Since the
   foundation is now fully independent, the city is transferring funds from the
   general fund to eliminate the $2.7 million deficit. This one-time transfer
MINUTES OF COUNCIL STUDY MEETING – MAY 27, 2025
                     -9-


   settles the City’s financial obligation to the foundation. Without this action,
   any continued deficits would have increased the amount owed, while
   surpluses would have reduced it. This transfer is a required accounting step to
   close out the city's involvement.

3. RDA Transfers to General Fund – Why are RDA funds being used to cover
   operations in General Fund departments such as Planning and Neighborhood
   Services? (Reference #18)

   Jim explained that the Redevelopment Agency (RDA) benefits from various
   services provided by the City’s general fund, including administration, planning
   and zoning, accounting, human resources, building inspections, and plan
   reviews. It would not be cost-effective for the RDA to hire separate staff for
   these functions. Instead, the City provides these services, and it is legally
   permissible for the RDA to reimburse the city for the associated costs. These
   reimbursements are reflected as transfers. This practice is consistent with how
   the City handles similar arrangements with other funds, such as stormwater and
   ambulance services, ensuring each entity pays its fair share of operational costs.


4. Fitness Center and Golf Course Operations – Regarding Items #7, #14, and
   #18: with the General Fund transfers and increased utility costs, are these
   programs still budget-neutral, or are they generating additional costs?

   Jim noted that the Fitness Center is not budget neutral and has required a City
   subsidy since it began operations. Currently, the City subsidizes it by
   approximately $1.6 million annually, though this amount can vary. Despite
   operating with a business-like approach and maintaining competitive market
   rates, the Fitness Center still requires public funding to continue offering
   services to the community. Regarding the city’s golf courses, Jim explained
   they are now operating close to break-even, and the debt service on both
   courses has been paid off. However, each course has an accumulated negative
   cash balance of about $2 million. This deficit has built up over the years due
   to the need for city subsidies to cover expenses when sufficient cash was not
   available, even though the courses now show positive net assets. Since assets
   like land and equipment cannot be used to pay expenses, the City must
   effectively loan money to cover the shortfall. He also addressed budget-
   related transfers, such as funding for essential maintenance like new sand
   filters for the pools (critical for summer operations) and resolving irrigation
   issues at the golf courses. Rather than worsening the courses' negative equity
   by charging these expenses directly to them, the decision was made to use
MINUTES OF COUNCIL STUDY MEETING – MAY 27, 2025
                     -10-


   general fund transfers to cover the costs. These transfers were discussed and
   approved as part of the budget process.

   Councilmember Whetstone inquired about funding sources for large capital
   expenditures at the golf courses, specifically referencing recent work on
   bridges. He sought clarification on whether such expenses were paid from the
   general fund and noted that while day-to-day operations of the golf courses
   appear to be self-sustaining, he questioned how major capital projects are
   funded.

   Jim confirmed that the golf courses are close to breaking even operationally,
   typically within a few hundred thousand dollars of balance by year-end. While
   that margin may seem large, it is relatively minor compared to total operational
   costs. However, he clarified that the courses are not fully self-sustaining,
   particularly when it comes to infrastructure needs. Large capital expenditures,
   such as bridge repairs, are not covered by the golf course budget. Some of those
   repairs were funded this year through the City’s Capital Improvement Fund via
   transferred funds. Looking ahead, similar projects recommended in the
   upcoming budget will also need to be funded through external sources, as the
   golf courses do not have the financial capacity to support such capital expenses
   themselves.


5. Arena Operations – For Item #26: With a $1.5M increase in operating costs
   due to a higher volume of events, is the arena currently self-sustaining or
   relying on city subsidy?

   Jim reported that the Maverick Center is currently operating without a city
   subsidy and has had positive financial results in recent years. He explained the
   budget appropriation process, noting that every dollar spent must be
   authorized by the city. For example, if the Maverick Center was initially
   authorized to spend $3 million but requires $4 million due to additional events
   that also generate extra revenue, the city must approve the additional $1
   million. Currently, it is estimated that the Maverick Center will need an extra
   $1.5 million to cover new events for the remainder of the year. Importantly,
   each of these events is expected to be profitable. Jim likened this process to
   purchasing inventory to enable more sales, highlighting that increasing the
   spending authorization is necessary to support increased revenue
   opportunities.
