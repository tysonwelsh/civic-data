2.1. Wasatch South Hills Master Development Agreement Amendment Discussion - Blake Thomas,
Community Development Director
Community Development Director Blake Thomas noted John Lindsley was in attendance to answer any
questions. Director Thomas briefly summarized the presentation he had given at the last Council meeting.
During that meeting, a question arose regarding whether the proposed plan aligned with the 2008 General Plan,
per the original Master Development Agreement (MDA). He presented several slides which reviewed the subject
property and highlighted the key points of the proposed plan. The owner originally wanted to have additional
units associated with Real Salt Lake; however, this request has since been removed. The property was over 600
acres, and the proposed plan would create about 190 acres of open space, all of which was undeveloped property
on the hillside. The plan included a regional park and a multi-family area outlined in the original Rosecrest
MDA. The proposed commercial use had increased by about 17.6 acres. The medium- and low-density housing
had decreased in acreage. Regarding high-density housing, Director Thomas explained the 2008 General Plan
allowed for 9.8 acres of high-density and 43.5 acres of mixed-use. The density allowed for mixed use was up to
30 units per acre. The current plan had a lower density than the Rosecrest Plan.

Mayor Watts asked how many acres of the original MDA were mixed-use, and if that mixed-use included a
commercial component. Director Thomas said that he had spoken with the planning group about the mixed-
use element, and their idea of mixed-use in 2008 was different from mixed-use today. City Planner Michael
Maloy added that when the east area General Plan was prepared and adopted by the Council, it allowed for a
range of density units up to 30 units per acre. When they adopted the mixed-use regulations, it was 10-15 units
per acre. The Council had originally anticipated a higher density in the General Plan, but when they actually
developed the zoning tools, the density was reduced. In this area, they would focus on retail and retail services.
They also anticipated some office and possibly a hotel. The increased commercial space and subsequential
decrease in residential was in line with the overall objectives of Herriman City.

Councilmember Smith asked where the low-density housing had been in the original plan. Director Thomas
identified the areas. There was some discussion regarding the original and newly proposed location for low-
density. The lots proposed now were all an acre in size or larger, but there were fewer of them than the original
plan.

Director Thomas referenced the last slide in the presentation and stated they had determined the minimum and

with the number of units proposed in 2017, they found the number of planned units fell in line with the General

2.2. Discussion of a resolution adding fees related to small cell wireless facilities and Public
Improvement District applications to the Herriman City Fee Schedule - Chase Andrizzi, City Attorney
City Attorney Chase Andrizzi stated the Council had taken some actions in the last several months which
required an update to the Master Fee Schedule, specifically the adoption of the Master License Agreement for
Small Sale Wireless Facilities, and the adoption of a policy regarding public infrastructure agreements. The
Master License Agreements dictated that small cell wireless facilities had certain fees that were dictated by statute,
including a $100 application fee for each facility, a $250 installation fee for facilities located on an existing utility
pole in a right-of-way, or a $1,000 installation fee for facilities located on an existing utility pole outside of the
right-of-way. These fees needed to be added to the Fee Schedule. Attorney Andrizzi then addressed fees for
public infrastructure agreements. There was a $100 fee due at the time that a letter of intent was submitted, and
a $1,000 fee for the submission of a governing document for the Council to review.

Councilmember Ohrn asked how these fees were determined, and Attorney Andrizzi explained that they were
determined by state statute.

2.4. Discussion pertaining to an Interlocal Agreement with Salt Lake County regarding a Storm Water
Management Outreach program - Jonathan Bowers, City Engineer
City Engineer Jonathan Bowers explained the City was responsible for certain items regarding storm water
management, and some of those overlapped with County responsibilities. Herriman City and Salt Lake County
had entered into an agreement in 2017 to share the cost of public improvement for storm water management.
The agreement focused on cost, and now the State has requested that the County better differentiate the actual
tasks and responsibilities of the County and the City.

Councilmember Ohrn asked if the City would be able to handle these costs on their own in order to maintain
their autonomy. Engineer Bowers replied it was in the City’s benefit to enter into the agreement. The City could
take credit for the actions of the Storm Water Coalition, which was run by the County. He noted he had not
done a cost benefit analysis of what the repercussions would be if the City broke away from the agreement. The
agreement had a termination clause, which stated that the City would have to inform the County of their intent
to leave the agreement within 30 days. Councilmember Ohrn wanted to see if it would be cost effective to sever
from the County on this. The cost of their membership was $5,300 annually. Councilmember Shields agreed
with Councilmember Ohrn and said that it was important to consider alternative options.

Engineer Bowers summarized his presentation, which outlined the City’s responsibilities, which primarily
consisted of paperwork and administrative duties.

2.5. Discussion pertaining to bonding options for Water infrastructure - Alan Rae, Finance Director
Finance Director Alan Rae said there was nothing new to present regarding this issue. The item had been added
to the agenda to answer any questions from the Council and get direction. In this case, the City could do nothing,
or they could refund the two bonds.
October 14, 2020 – City Council Minutes                                                         Page 5 of 12
Mayor Watts asked if this was considered separate from other bonding conducted, and Director Rae answered
affirmatively. He explained the Water Fund and the Storm Drain Fund were set up to be sustained by user fees
only. They could not take money from the Water Fund for any other use, but could take money out of the
General Fund for the Water Fund. He said the only collateral for this fund would be the user fees, but it also
qualified for impact fees, if the Council wanted to go in that direction.

In response to a question from Mayor Watts, Director Rae said they were no prevented from going for a General
Use bond, but he didn’t recommend that they apply for it. They were rated AA+ for the water governmental
funds and had a slightly higher rating for the enterprise funds. The funds were also accounted for in different
ways, and water governmental funds were supposed to carry their own costs.

Councilmember Ohrn asked if there were other options for funding, and Director Rae replied Public Works
Director Justun Edward had explored other cost options with developers, but had not found an option that was
more cost-effective than the bonds. Director Rae added the Council could choose to levy a tax for water, but
that did not seem like the best option either. The City could simply wait and accumulate the money. If they
didn’t do any other projects, they could have the money in six or seven years. If they continued to do projects
as they have been, the money would be accumulated in about 15 years.

Director Rae said the largest water tank cost $14.5 million. They could opt to build smaller tanks, but they
wouldn’t want to bond for those. Currently, there was not enough revenue to build tanks in the foreseeable
future without bonds. He had proposed a bond because he felt that the City often came at project from behind
and had to find developers to fund projects. He felt that it would be better to obtain the money themselves and
then go through the normal bidding process. When he reached out to Zions Bank regarding a bond, they
proposed 2.1-percent, although that was subject to change. If the City took the bond, staff would write a
parameters resolution which would stipulate that they would not consider anything that exceeded a certain
amount of interest and principal. Then, they would work towards the underwriting to ensure that they could
stay within those parameters and they would examine if the market could sustain what they were trying to do.
The process would take 60 to 90 days.

Mayor Watts clarified they would potentially have better control on cost by going with the bond process. The
City had far less control over the reimbursement process. Director Rae agreed and noted that with this situation,
it would be difficult to get reimbursement due to the number of landowners involved.

Mayor Watts then asked what the City’s obligations were in terms of continued development. It would take
several years to save the money for the project, and he wondered how that fell in line with the City’s plan to
maintain water infrastructure. Public Works Director Justun Edwards replied there were some projects that
were not pertinent to development, and some projects were aimed to help with the growing demands on
Herriman City as the population increased. The main obligation of the City was the water line at the end of the
City, which fed into Creek Ridge. Another priority was the East Herriman Water System, including the
installation of several water tanks and pipes. Many developers were anxious to begin developing, but they
couldn’t do that without the water system in place. He noted that some improvement projects were currently
under construction in the Juniper Canyon water system. Those would be funded by impact fees. The City could
October 14, 2020 – City Council Minutes                                                            Page 6 of 12
tie the project into the bond, since the interest rate was so low. This would free up some of those impact fees
for other projects. The City also needed to install a new pump station and upgrade an existing pump station.
Both of those pumps would help resolve some of the infrastructure issues they were facing.

Mayor Watts asked if there was a maximum amount of time that they could hold on to the funds before they
were required to spend them. Director Rae stated they would have to spend 10-percent of the bond in the first
six months, and the full amount should be spent within three years. The money did not have to spent in any
particular order. Mayor Watts said he was not opposed to going for the bond, but he wanted to ensure they
prioritize the needs of the residents before the needs of developers. He believed the bond would sustain itself.
Councilmember Ohrn commented the interest would continue to accrue from the moment they took possession
of the funds, so it was important to lay the pipes as soon as possible. Director Rae noted the funds would go
into a Public Treasurers Investment Trust, which would collect interest. The interest rate on the trust was
between 1.2-percent and 1.7-percent, so there wouldn’t be a significant difference between what the City would
earn and what they would pay in interest.

There was a brief discussion regarding a potential refinance. Director Rae said it would benefit them to move
forward. It was 6.8-percent at present value on the refinance, and it was generally best practice to consider
refinance above three percent. The two bonds were at the point where they could be refunded. He explained
there had been something called “advanced refunding,” which they had done several years ago, in which they
had gone out for bond and put it in trust, and they had been 3-4 years out from paying it back. As of two years
ago, advanced refunding was no longer an available option. Since advanced refunding had been removed,
Director Rae had to watch the bonds to see when they became available. City had gone out for bond in
December 2015, and it had been the lowest rate in the bond market in 100 years. The last five years the bond
market had not reacted to the Federal Bank, and bonds were considered to be more secure than other financial
options for people who wanted to save their money.

Councilmember Shields asked if some of the priorities on the list would help produce additional revenue, and
Director Edwards said they would not necessarily increase revenue, but would increase the system efficiencies
as the City expanded. Councilmember Shields asked if it would be wise for the City to apply for a larger sum of
money, since the interest rates were historically low. He also asked if there were any projects that could be put
off a while longer. Director Rae said the challenge they were facing was that they needed to do improvements
before money could be collected, as water user fees accumulated over time.

Councilmember Shields thought they might save money if they worked on more projects now while the interest
rates were so low. Mayor Watts agreed and said that maintenance was always more effective than repairs or
replacement. They would save the residents’ money in the long run if they looked ahead to future needs.
Councilmember Shields said if they had the opportunity to get ahead of future needs now, they should take it.
Councilmember Smith supported looking ahead, but he didn’t want to delay the process of moving on this
current project. He suggested that they begin drafting a parameters resolution. Director Rae said staff could
come back to the Council with several options.

    7.1. Public Hearing and consideration of an amendment to the 2020-2021 Herriman City Budget -
         Alan Rae, Finance Director
Finance Director Alan Rae stated the City had received their first disbursement of funds from the CARES Act,
and some of those funds had been spend. However, he felt like it was time for an official budget amendment
to make sure they were on the right track. So far, they had received $2,461,000 and as part of their contract with
Salt Lake County; 60-percent was to support local businesses and 40-percent was for COVID remediation.

$1,590,000 of the $2,461,000 was to support local businesses, and some of that was spent on the Shop Around

Assistant City Manager Tami Moody noted Herriman City had been recognized for the Shop Around program.

efforts included increased sanitation, plexiglass installation, PPE for all departments and some businesses, and
PPE kits in police vehicles. They were also able to fund additional laptops, since the court had been conducted
virtually. Plexiglass had also been installed at the courthouse to prepare for the upcoming election.

Assistant City Manager Moody said there were some concerns with connectivity in the City building, and staff
was looking at ways to increase service. They were also exploring an enhanced microphone system, a ventilation
system in the community room, and a third and final round of the Shop Around program. For the Shop Around,
staff would like the Council to consider an amount of $100-150 per household, since 60-percent of the funds
needed to go toward economic development. Councilmember Ohrn asked how much had been spend on the
first two round. Assistant City Manager Moody said they had received a lot of positive feedback from the
residents and businesses, and she thought the higher amount would incentivize people to try new businesses.
Mayor Watts was supportive of doing as much as they could to help residents, especially with Christmas coming

    10.1. Discussion and appointment of an Interim City Manager - Chase Andrizzi, City Attorney
City Attorney Chase Andrizzi explained Wendy Thomas had been filling the position of Interim City Manager.
It was his recommendation to use the open meeting to discuss general qualifications and achievements that they
wanted in a candidate before staff moved forward into a closed session to discuss specific individuals. The hiring
of a City Manager could be a lengthy process.

Councilmember Shields stated they needed to decide what they wanted to do as far as a permanent City Manager
and how they would go through the process. He felt finding a City Manager was a complex issue, and it would
be in the City’s best interest to take their time in finding the right individual. Attorney Andrizzi stated the Council
did not need to make a decision tonight, but they should work toward a final decision within the next month.

Councilmember Smith suggested putting this item on a future agenda for a detailed discussion. He wanted input
from the City Attorney and staff, as well as information on what other cities were doing. Councilmember Shields
added that the agenda should also include a discussion on the hiring process. Attorney Andrizzi said they could
hold a special Council Meeting, if necessary.

Interim City Manager Wendy Thomas suggested they wait until after the new year to post the position. She
didn’t believe they would receive many applications during the holiday season.

Councilmember Smith thought it was appropriate to discuss expectations of a City Manager and to clearly
articulate the Council’s expectations. Everyone who had the potential for this position already had a grasp of
what was expected, but they also needed to be able to maintain the culture of the workplace and keep things
moving forward. They should also be able to address items as they come up, and not wait to pass them on to
the permanent replacement. Councilmember Shields agreed and added he was not against an outside hire. A
new person may take longer to get into the swing of things, but a fresh perspective was not necessarily a bad
thing. Mayor Watts was against an outside hire for the Interim City Manager. Councilmember Shields said he
wasn’t necessarily advocating an outside hire, but there could be some benefits to it. Councilmember Ohrn
didn’t think they should close off the opportunity to an outside hire, as long as the person was qualified and had
the experience.

Mayor Watts thought communication was very important and that an increased level of communication was
necessary during this time. Councilmember Shields asked what level of communication he expected from the
Interim City Manager. Mayor Watts replied he would like quick bullet point updates daily. Councilmember
Smith commented that he didn’t want to micromanage or require daily updates if they were unnecessary.
Councilmember Ohrn agreed. Mayor Watts replied that his intention was to set an expectation.

Councilmember Shields moved to appoint Wendy Thomas as Interim City Manager. Councilmember Ohrn seconded the motion,
