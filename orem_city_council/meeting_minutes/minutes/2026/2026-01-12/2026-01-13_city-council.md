ULCT (Utah League of Cities and Towns) Legislative Update Discussion Presenter:
Cameron Diehl, ULCT Executive Director

Mr. Diehl, Executive Director of the Utah League of Cities and Towns (ULCT), detailed
a fundamental shift in how the League engages with the state legislature. Moving away from its
former reputation as the "League of No," the ULCT now operates under the principles of respect,
collaboration, and outcomes. Mr. Diehl emphasized that collaboration means "co-laboring" with
the state to find common ground rather than just resisting change. A major strategic initiative in
this vein is the "Conflict Competence" curriculum, developed in partnership with the University
of Utah Law School. This program trains local officials to move past political friction by
identifying core interests, particularly in the contentious area of land use, and is designed to
remain non-partisan and accessible to leaders across the political spectrum.

The legislative landscape for 2026 features several high-stakes bills, most notably HB
184 (Local Land Use Revisions). This bill introduces the concept of "preferred land use
regulations," which would categorize certain housing types—specifically "starter homes" and
accessory dwelling units (ADUs)—as permitted uses regardless of existing local zoning rules. A
starter home is defined as a single-family unit sold to an initial owner-occupier at or below the
county's average price with a two-year deed restriction. The bill creates a strict 30-day review
window for cities; if a city does not proactively deny a "preferred" request within that timeframe,

Open Meeting Act and Ethics Training Presenter: Steve Earl, City Attorney

City Attorney Earl conducted the mandatory annual training, emphasizing that the
underlying premise of the Open and Public Meetings Act (OPMA) is the public's right to witness
and participate in the transparent conduct of government business. Under Utah Code § 52-4-101,
a "meeting" is legally defined as a convening of a quorum (at least four members of the Orem
City Council) for the express purpose of deliberating, receiving public comment, or taking action
on matters within their authority. This includes regular sessions, work sessions, and retreats.
However, the Act excludes casual social gatherings or chance encounters, such as a Christmas
party or meeting at a local restaurant, provided that council members do not discuss city

business. To ensure transparency, all public meetings require a minimum of 24-hour notice—a
responsibility managed by the City Recorder—which must include the date, time, location, and a
specific agenda. The Council is legally prohibited from taking final action on any item not
explicitly listed on the agenda with "reasonable specificity." While the Council may respond to
comments on unlisted topics during "Personal Appearances," they must direct staff to schedule
those items for future meetings if action is required.

State law mandates that both written minutes and a recording be maintained for all open
meetings. The minutes must capture the substance of all matters discussed, the names of
non-council members providing testimony, and a record of every vote. While Orem typically
provides both audio and video via YouTube, an exception exists for site visits or "traveling tours"
where no votes are taken, as these do not require a recording. Addressing the nuances of modern
communication, Attorney Earl advised that while the OPMA does not explicitly prohibit texting
or emailing during meetings, such behavior is widely considered contrary to the spirit of open

~ government. Furthermore, any digital communication between council members or staff
regarding city business during a meeting is classified as a public record under the Government
Records Access and Management Act (GRAMA) and is subject to subpoena or public request.
Group emails for information dissemination are permitted outside of meetings, but they should
not be used to conduct hidden debates or "pre-judge" an applicant’s request before a public
hearing.

Closed meetings are permitted only under strict legal criteria and require a two-thirds
majority vote (at least five members) to initiate. The most common reasons for closing a meeting
include discussing the character or professional competence of an individual (personnel matters),
strategy sessions regarding pending or imminent litigation, and deliberations regarding the
purchase or sale of real property. Despite the private nature of these sessions, a recording is still
required unless the sole topic is an individual’s character or health, in which case the Mayor must
sign a sworn statement. Crucially, no final action—including the passing of ordinances,
resolutions, or contracts—can ever occur within a closed session; all votes must be cast in an
open, noticed meeting. While there are no direct criminal penalties under the OPMA for
disclosing what was said in a closed room, Attorney Earl warned that such disclosures might
violate GRAMA’s protected record status. However, it is a Class B misdemeanor for the Council
to hold a closed meeting for a subject not authorized by law.

The training concluded with an overview of the Utah Municipal Officers and Employees
Ethics Act, which sets rigorous standards to prevent the misuse of public office for personal gain.
Council members are required to disclose any conflict of interest and recuse themselves from
deliberating or voting if a decision would have a material effect on their direct financial interest
or that ofa relative (spouse, child, or sibling). Recusal is not required for casual acquaintances or
general neighborhood proximity, but members were encouraged to seek legal counsel if they feel
a "personal alert" regarding a potential conflict. Regarding gifts, the law prohibits accepting
anything of substantial value that could improperly influence a public duty. While there is a legal
exception for occasional gifts valued at $50 or less, the City Attorney recommended a "best

Emergency Preparedness Discussion Presenter: Heath Stevenson, Emergency Manager and
Marc Sanderson, Fire Chief

Mr. Stevenson, Orem City Emergency Manager, accompanied by Fire Chief Marc
Sanderson, provided an in-depth briefing on the city's emergency preparedness framework. The
entire operation is anchored by the "LIP" acronym: Life Safety, Incident Stabilization, and
Property/Environmental Conservation. Stevenson explained that every decision made during a
crisis—from the tactical responses of police and fire to the high-level policy determinations of

the Mayor and Council—must be priori tized in that specilic order. Chier Sanderson added a
crucial departmental perspective, noting that the city is shifting its view of "first responders" to
include Public Works and other essential departments, rather than just police and fire. This
integrated approach ensures that the city acts as a single, cohesive unit when addressing threats
to the community's safety or environment.

The city’s emergency response is organized into a four-tiered bubble system designed to
prevent communication breakdowns. At the ground level is Incident Command, where tactical
personnel handle the "boots on the ground" response. Above them is the Emergency Operations
Center (EOC), located in a centralized facility at City Hall, which provides logistical support and
resource coordination when field resources become overwhelmed. The third and fourth tiers
consist of the Executive Staff and the Mayor and Council, who operate at a "30,000-foot level."
In this structure, the City Manager serves as the vital liaison, translating the EOC’s situational
data into actionable information for elected officials. Chief Sanderson emphasized that the
primary goal of this hierarchy is to provide the Mayor and Council with enough detailed
intelligence to maintain public confidence and succeed in media briefings, ensuring that the
"political judgment" and "policy clarity" required during a disaster are based on real-time facts.

A significant portion of the training addressed the legal and financial thresholds for
declaring a local emergency. Mr. Stevenson clarified that a disaster declaration is generally
triggered when Orem City’s internal resources—personnel, equipment, or funding—are maxed
out. While Orem has the capability to "wing it" through large-scale incidents like microbursts or
protests, a formal declaration (such as the 18-month declaration during the COVID-19
pandemic) is necessary to unlock county, state, and federal aid through FEMA. Mr. Stevenson
noted that these declarations often funnel down from the Governor’s office, which monitors
emerging threats and advises local municipalities when state-level resources or financial
reimbursements are becoming available. This process is highly dependent on rigorous
documentation; Mr. Stevenson warned that without a clear paper trail of decisions and
expenditures, FEMA will deny reimbursement requests.

To mitigate risks before they occur, the city utilizes a rigorous Mass Gathering Permit
process for any event exceeding 1,000 people for more than two hours. For major city events like
Oremfest, Mr. Stevenson develops a 30-to-35-page Incident Action Plan (IAP) that outlines
medical responder requirements, security objectives, and "See Something, Say Something"
protocols. The city also acts as a regulatory body for outside organizations, such as the
University Mall or Colonial Days, requiring them to fulfill Utah County Health Department
standards before Orem Risk Management signs off on the site. This preventive planning ensures
that even during non-emergency celebrations, the city has a pre-determined medical and safety
footprint ready to activate if an incident occurs.

Orem’s preparedness extends beyond City Hall through the Everbridge emergency
notification system and the annual Orem City Drill. Mr. Stevenson urged the Council to act as
ambassadors for Everbridge, as the system allows the city to "ping" cell phones with life-saving
information during localized crises. The annual drill is a landmark success for Orem, utilizing a

block captain program and ham radio volunteers to engage residents across [8 of the city’s 21”
geographic areas. While many cities struggle to achieve a 30% participation rate in emergency
exercises, Orem has seen engagement from over 45,000 residents. Additionally, the city has
recently boosted its neighborhood preparedness courses by partnering with Provo City, a
collaboration that has seen attendance jump from 15-30 participants to as many as 120, fostering
a regional culture of self-reliance and readiness.

Stevenson concluded by providing a checklist for the Council to use during a real-world
activation. Rather than questioning tactical field decisions, the Council is encouraged to focus on
three high-level questions: What is the current risk to life and property? What decisions require
political judgment? What policy clarity does the EOC need? By focusing on governing the
response while allowing responders to execute it, the Council ensures the city remains stabilized.
Council Member Mecham noted that tragedies, when handled with this level of organized
expertise, serve as "community-building events" that bring residents closer together. The session
ended with a commitment to conduct "mock activations" in the new EOC to ensure that every
city employee and elected official is comfortable in their role before a true disaster strikes.

Water Emergency Repair Recognition Presentation Presenter:Chris Tschirki, Public Works
Director and Reed Price, Assistant Public Works Director and Quinn Fenton, Water Manager

Orem Public Works Director Tschirki, Assistant Director Price, and Water Manager
Fenton provided a detailed report to the City Council regarding a significant infrastructure crisis
that occurred during the first week of December.

The crisis began on a Thursday afternoon at 3:00 PM—yjust as crews were finishing their
scheduled shifts—with an unrelenting series of water main breaks west of I-15. What was
initially expected to be a standard repair evolved into a 30-to-40-hour marathon for several
employees. In total, crews battled nine challenging breaks in rapid succession. The primary cause
was identified as severe corrosion in ductile iron pipes installed in the early 1990s. The system’s
fragility was exposed when a tiny leak in a control valve caused a minor 15-pound pressure
increase; this small shift was enough to trigger a "domino effect" of failures across the weakened

Mr. Fenton described the physical and emotional toll on the workers, who had to

managed a massive cleanup effort to remove mud and debris from neighborhood streets to ensure

To prevent future "piece-meal" repairs in this corridor, the Public Works department is

sheer scale of Orem’s 500-mile network means that infrastructure often "identifies itself" through
these types of failures.
