30   2) Conflict Resolution Training
31
32   Dr. Danya Rumore began with a recap of last month’s work session discussion. She briefly
33   reviewed the basics of conflict, interests vs. positions, and effective listening. She outlined the
34   day’s discussion.
35
36   She began by having the group break into smaller pods to discuss the listening self-reflection
37   exercise assigned last month.
38
39   Rumore framed the day’s conversation by reemphasizing that “conflict just is” in planning and
40   government. She explained that she was there to help, but urged those collected to use their own
41   discernment while remaining open minded enough to give her strategies a try.
42
43   Rumore introduced the concept of effective framing. Framing is the lens through which one
44   views the world. Thoughts and situations are always being framed by individuals subconsciously.
45   Frames inform the narratives people hold about the world, situations, other people, and
46   themselves, and define what is seen as possible and impossible. She encouraged a shift from
47   disempowering frames to empowering frames.
48

49   Rumore introduced the term ‘probletunity,’ meaning that in every problem there is an
50   opportunity. She used skiing as an example. When skiing, one’s path is determined by what is
51   being focused on. Skiers need to focus on the paths they want to take, rather than the obstacles
52   they want to avoid.
53
54   There are four principles of effective framing that Rumore shared with the commission:
55
56   Principle 1: Focus on the probletunities, not the people. Do not personalize problems, as that
57   integrates ego into the situation rather than working towards resolution. Conflict is always co-
58   created and not any one individual’s fault.
59
60   Principle 2: Focus on interest, not positions. Framing solutions around interests helps get to the
61   core of issues.
62
63   Principle 3: Focus on assets, not deficits. This frames and defines people in a positive way, rather
64   than a negative way.
65
66   Principle 4: Be responsive, not reactive. This allows individuals to be drivers of action rather
67   than passive observers. A key part of this is understanding and communicating one’s feelings
68   rather than insisting on acting on emotions. Prioritizing agency over feelings allows more to be
69   accomplished.
70
71   Rumore also advised using tentative language as a way to implement effective framing.
72   Language that acknowledges one’s personal perspective makes one feel more open and inviting
73   to others. Additionally, it is generally good practice to ask questions rather than make statements.
74   She emphasized that effective framing is an intentional choice people make in order to become
75   more effective problem solvers. It doesn’t come easily.
76
77   Commissioner Eyre shared his worries that framing can be utilized to force a specific way of
78   thinking about a problem and impose it on other people. Rumore explained that it is a possibility,
79   but that framing is already occurring subconsciously. Her goal is to make the commission aware
80   of the way they frame things to make them conscious of how they are using them in
81   conversation.
82
83   Rumore introduced five different “modes” of problem-solving (avoidance, competition,
84   accommodation, compromise, and collaboration) and discussed how they are all informed by
85   how concerned one is with one’s own outcomes, as well as with the outcomes of others.
86   Collaboration results when there is a high level of concern for the outcomes of all parties.
87   Collaboration (derived from the word co-labor) leads to making differences productive and
88   jointly-created value. It is a pragmatic approach based on interest-based negotiation.
89
90   The fact that conflict is seen as a problem is part of the reason collaboration is so difficult.
91   Rumore explained that many conflicts are made worse by unchecked dysregulation. She
92   encouraged those present to recognize their own dysregulation and deal with it internally before
93   attempting to problem solve. She explained that implementing much of the information will take
94   time and will occur in a cycle of having a moment of self-awareness, recognizing skills that
95   would help in a situation, make different choices based on those skills, and integrating them into
96   our way of being and habits, which will make it easier to gain that self-awareness.

 98   The Commission separated into different groups for an exercise meant to help them apply key-
 99   concepts to a real-world example. Rumore asked those gathered to think about SHAR and
100   identify 1) who some of the key parties are 2) what positions those parties are advocating for 3)
101   what the parties’ underlying interests may be 4) how the issue was framed and how it could have
102   been framed more effectively 5) how well the parties effectively listened to each other and 6)
103   how they would approach the situation differently after having been through this training.
104
105   After the exercise, Rumore opened the floor to questions, observations insights, and takeaways.
106
107   Commissioner Lockhart asked how to best balance collaboration with boundaries. Rumore
108   explained that boundaries are internal controls put in place to protect our own needs. You need to
109   have a good grasp on your needs to set good boundaries. She also discussed the difference
110   between a boundary set to keep one safe vs. a boundary set to keep one comfortable.
111
112   Commissioner Everett asked if it was fair to define a boundary as a position that supports an
113   interest. Rumore explained that a position is a demand, whereas a boundary is internal. Focusing
114   too much on positionality can create threat where there previously wasn’t any. Brittney Harris
115   shared that positions are based off of interested, but that investigating those interests can lead to
116   common ground desires and needs.
