    2.2. Discussion Regarding Adoption Process for Public Infrastructure District
         Governing Documents for the Panorama and Wasatch-South Hills Master-
         Planned Developments – Blake Thomas, Community Development Director
Community Development Director Blake Thomas began by giving a brief overview of the PID,
explaining that it had been established by the legislature to allow developers to set up a taxing
district to generate revenue, which would help them finance public infrastructure
improvements through the municipal bond market. He emphasized that the improvements
November 13, 2024 – City Council Minutes                                         Page 3 of 24

funded by the PID must be public, and all property owners within the taxing area must
consent to be included in the district.

He explained that the proposed PID For the Panorama and Wasatch-South Hills project area
covered about 1,100 acres, with an estimated $55 million in infrastructure costs. The
developers were proposing a 5-mill tax levy to fund the project, leaving the door open for
additional fees or assessments. The governing document was designed to allow for multiple
amendments, enabling the developers to expand the PID as needed. Director Thomas
mentioned that the governing document would be essentially the same for both PIDs, with
only the names of the areas changed. He pointed out some areas that were not fully met
according to the PID policy. These included requirements for more detailed financial
information, such as a pro forma and financial plan, a breakdown of infrastructure costs, and
a phasing plan for the development. He suggested referencing the Master Development
Agreement (MDA) for details on phasing and densities, as these were already outlined in the
MDA but were not included in the governing document itself. He also recommended including
a maintenance agreement for areas managed by homeowners' associations (HOAs),
especially for public improvements that would be maintained by the HOA.

Another issue raised during the discussion was the potential for mismanagement or wasteful
use of public funds, particularly regarding management fees and other soft costs that could
be charged to the PID. The Council expressed concerns that such fees could enrich
developers at the expense of the public. Director Thomas emphasized that the PID should
focus solely on funding public infrastructure, not additional administrative or management
costs.

The Council considered whether the PID's governing document should include a detailed list
of infrastructure projects, as well as information about the phasing of those projects. It was
suggested that a map and phasing timeline would be helpful for better understanding how the
infrastructure would be developed over time. Director Thomas agreed to provide these
additional materials for the next council meeting for consideration. The Council also
expressed some uncertainty about how future expansions of the PID would affect the mill
levy rate. It was anticipated that the mill levy would remain consistent across the entire
district, the actual amount paid by individual property owners would vary based on the value
of their properties.

The council discussed their oversight role in the PID process, particularly in ensuring that
the tax revenue generated by the PID was used appropriately. The relayed their duty was to
protect the public interest and avoid any potential misuse of funds. Council consensus
concluded that language to control costs and prevent misuse of tax revenue should be
included in the governing document. They also agreed to move forward with the process,

incorporating the feedback provided, and to revisit the PID proposal at the next council
meeting.

    2.3. Discuss Financial Impact of Hiring Additional Police Officers – Kyle Maurer,
          Director of Finance and Administrative Services
Finance Director Kyle Maurer began by discussing the financial aspects of hiring additional
police officers, focusing on the potential costs and the financial impact of such a decision. He
mentioned that several key individuals were present, including Chief Carr, Deputy Chief
Stromberg, and Commander Adams, to help navigate the discussion. Director Maurer first
referred to the tax increase approved in August 2023, which had been allocated for specific
purposes: police vehicle replacements, hiring additional officers, and funding for a mental
health specialist. He explained that the tax increase had been structured with restrictions,
meaning that only certain uses were allowed for the funds. Specifically, 8.7% of the tax
increase was directed towards vehicle replacements, 2.4% (around $192,000) for hiring
officers to meet demand, and 1.1% (around $88,220) for the mental health specialist position.

Director Maurer then provided an overview of the financial situation for the current and
upcoming fiscal years, stating that approximately $1.4 million had been collected for vehicle
replacements, $385,000 for new officers, and $176,000 for the mental health specialist. He
clarified that these figures represented funds collected for two years' worth of tax increases,
despite the tax revenue for the second year still being in the process of collection. The funds
were set aside in separate accounts to preserve them for their designated purposes. He
further explained the total salary burden for a new officer, which, including benefits and
retirement costs, amounted to approximately $155,000 annually. Additionally, the operating
costs (such as equipment, training, and subscriptions) would be around $25,000, and a new
police vehicle would cost approximately $82,500. Thus, the total first-year cost for hiring a
new officer would be about $262,000. However, he noted that fuel costs were not included
in this calculation because the department's new vehicles were more fuel-efficient, and the
current fuel budget was sufficient to cover these expenses for now.

Police Chief Troy Carr followed up by discussing the shifting priorities and financial
challenges faced by the police department. He mentioned that the previous focus had been
on how to address "peaking times" and how to meet increasing demands for police services.
However, with the rise in more complex criminal activities, Police Chief Carr explained that
the department’s main concern had shifted towards addressing these escalating issues. He
highlighted the accelerated rate of complex crimes, noting that the department currently
lacked a specific focus on this area, unlike many other departments that had specialized units
for such activities. Police Chief Carr emphasized that while the department had been lucky
in the past, they could no longer rely on that luck, and the need for additional resources had
become critical. He also mentioned the implementation of AI technology in the department,
which had significantly increased efficiency, particularly in report writing. While the AI
November 13, 2024 – City Council Minutes                                           Page 5 of 24

system required an annual expense of $50,000, it had freed up more than 50% of officers'
time that was previously spent on writing reports, thereby allowing them to focus more on
patrol duties. However, he pointed out that despite these efficiencies, the department was
still facing a significant increase in call volume, which compounded the challenge of meeting
staffing demands.

Councilmember Jared Henderson suggested that the Council should plan to discuss the
broader budget and staffing plan at a future meeting. He advocated for a transparent
conversation with the public regarding the purpose and allocation of funds, particularly when
it came to hiring additional officers. Councilmember Henderson also highlighted that the City
needed to address the long-term issue of inflation and rising operational costs, which had not
been fully accounted for in the initial tax increase. He pointed out that while the tax increase
was intended to fund specific expenses, the city needed to build a comprehensive plan to
manage both current and future budgetary challenges, particularly when considering
inflationary pressures.

Councilmember Teddy Hodges expressed support for using the funds from the previous tax
increase to hire at least one new officer, as this was the original purpose of the tax hike. He
felt that the funds should be used as intended, and he did not see any issue with moving
forward with this allocation. Councilmember Hodges agreed that it might be necessary to
consider additional officers if the budget allowed for it. He acknowledged that the situation
might be different from earlier projections, but felt confident that the funds were available
and should be utilized to meet the city's current needs.

Councilmember Steve Shields reflected on the progress made in understanding and
projecting police costs. He praised the team for their work in identifying the long-term
financial needs of the police department, particularly with the help of data analytics and
projections. Councilmember Shields acknowledged that police costs would continue to rise
each year and emphasized the importance of planning ahead to manage these costs. He also
recognized that the City’s previous approach had been more reactive, and now, with the new
data and projections, they could be more proactive in addressing the growing financial
demands of the police department. He reiterated that the City had the tools to manage these
costs effectively, as long as they continued to be forward-looking and data-driven in their
approach.

   2.4. Update and Design Alternatives for 6000 West Roadway Widening Phase 1 –
        Bryce Terry, City Engineer
City Engineer Bryce Terry began by providing background on the area, noting that Phase 1
had a budget of $3.5 million, which was set aside for both design and construction. He
explained that the funds would come from various sources, including traffic, water, and storm
drain impact fees, and that not all of the design funds might be used, potentially rolling over
November 13, 2024 – City Council Minutes                                         Page 6 of 24

into construction funds. He emphasized that although Phase 1 was confirmed in the budget,
Phase 2, which included the middle portion of the road, had not been fully funded yet, and its
timing could change depending on available revenue. City Manager Nathan Cherpeski
clarified that Phase 2 was part of the Capital Improvement Plan for fiscal year 2027 but could
be altered based on shifting priorities.

Engineer Terry moved on to the design options for Phase 1, asking for feedback on three main
questions: the cross-section or width of the road, the treatment of the park strips, and the
management of overhead power lines. He outlined three options for the roadway cross-
section. Option 1, his recommendation, proposed a 5-foot park strip on each side of the road,
with a sidewalk placed behind it. He explained that this option reduced conflicts with private
irrigation systems and fences, which could be problematic. He added that while this design
offered a better environment for trees, it would slightly reduce their growth potential due to
the smaller park strip. Option 2 followed the City's standard with a 4-foot park strip on each
side. While this design minimized conflicts, it reduced the buffer for trees, which could have
negative impacts on aesthetics. Option 3 eliminated the park strips entirely, placing the
sidewalk directly next to the curb. This design would require mountable curbs, potentially
reducing the safety of pedestrians, especially due to the lack of buffer between the sidewalk
and the road. Engineer Terry acknowledged that Option 3 had received negative feedback
from residents who felt that the mountable curbs created aesthetic and safety issues.

Engineer Terry further explained that Option 1 would reduce the number of property owners
whose land would need to be acquired, cutting down the need for right-of-way purchases.
However, he clarified that not all properties would be fully avoided. While Option 1 would
result in fewer property acquisitions, it wouldn’t completely eliminate the need to negotiate
with some landowners. He added that the difference in costs between Option 1 and Option 2
was minimal, but Option 1 would provide better long-term benefits by minimizing conflicts
with private irrigation systems and fences.

The discussion then shifted to the park strips and how they should be landscaped and
maintained. Engineer Terry presented several options. Option A, which had been used on
Herriman Main Street, featured alternating stamped concrete with planter beds containing
bushes and trees. Engineer Terry mentioned that this approach was not only visually
appealing but also cost-effective to maintain. Option B followed the City’s standard with rock
mulch covering the park strips, with at least 50% plant coverage and a tree every 30 feet. He
emphasized that Option A was easier and cheaper to maintain than Option B, and it had
received positive feedback from the public.

Next, Engineer Terry presented three options related to the maintenance and landscaping of
the park strips, each involving varying degrees of city and property owner responsibility.
Option C involved the City installing the park strips but providing a stipend or credit for
November 13, 2024 – City Council Minutes                                         Page 7 of 24

property owners to landscape the strip as they saw fit. Option D also had the city install the
park strips, but without offering a stipend, leaving the property owners to decide whether to
landscape the area in the future. Option E was a hybrid, where the City would install gravel
or rock mulch but would expect property owners to handle the maintenance and landscaping.
Bryce explained that for Options C, D, and E, irrigation sleeves would be installed beneath
the sidewalks, allowing future connections to irrigation systems without requiring the
sidewalk to be cut up.

Engineer Terry briefly addressed the issue of maintaining water-efficient landscaping, noting
that some areas of the project had already been built out under older guidelines, which had
led to a mix of landscaping types. The team had decided not to go back and retrofit the older
landscaping areas to meet modern water efficiency standards, though the project would still
adhere to these standards in new sections.

Engineer Terry then turned to the issue of overhead power lines. He noted that the power
lines along 6000 West were currently above ground, and while the City could ask Rocky
Mountain Power to move them for free within the City’s right-of-way, undergrounding the
lines would be very costly—over a million dollars. Given the high cost, the project team
recommended keeping the power lines above ground, as they had done on Main Street.
Engineer Terry explained that although undergrounding would be ideal, it wasn’t feasible
within the current budget.

Finally, Engineer Terry provided a cost matrix for the project, outlining the implications of
each design choice. He reaffirmed that Option 1A for the cross-section and keeping the
overhead power lines would be the most feasible option given the budget constraints. He
emphasized that undergrounding the power lines would stretch the project’s budget beyond
its limits and could make it impossible to fund. Council consensus chose to move forward
with Option 1A, as recommended by staff.

transitioning to market-rate, age-targeted housing that remains attractive without the
regulatory confines of age-restricted projects.

elaborated on the complexities of fulfilling the senior-only requirement and conveyed
confidence in the viability of market-rate, age-targeted housing. Mr. Mackey shared insights

with federal housing restrictions. He assured that Ivory Homes was committed to
November 13, 2024 – City Council Minutes                                          Page 14 of 24

collaborating with the City to meet the updated landscaping requirements and ensure
compliance with newly enacted water conservation standards.

    9.7. Amending Title 11 (Residential Treatment Facilities) to allow the City Manager to
         act as the Hearing Officer for certain reasonable accommodation request – Todd
         Sheeran, City Attorney
City Attorney Todd Sheeran City Attorney Todd Sheeran explained this amendment would
allow the City Manager to make certain decisions on accommodation requests, such as for
treatment facilities or sober living homes, rather than requiring a hearing officer in all cases.
He stated this would save time and money for simple requests while still allowing more
complex cases to go through the full hearing process. Attorney Sheeran elaborated that there
are typically some requests that do not require the intricate legal process typically associated
with a hearing officer. He shared an example of a situation where a resident might request
privacy fencing because their child is vulnerable to outdoor dangers, like a dog bite. In such
straightforward cases, involving a hearing officer could be unnecessarily cumbersome.
Therefore, the proposed amendment focuses on practicality, reserving the hearing officer
process for those cases that indeed necessitate that level of detail and complexity.
