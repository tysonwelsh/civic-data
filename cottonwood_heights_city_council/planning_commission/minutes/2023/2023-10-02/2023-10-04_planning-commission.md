25   1.0    Form-Based Code Update.
26
27   Community and Development Director, Michael Johnson, introduced the Form-Based Code
28   update, which describes progress over the last several months. Mark Morris, Principal Landscape
29   Architect/Founder of VODA Landscape + Design presented the Form-Based Code update and
30   stated the current draft is 90 pages. The Form-Based Code is being applied to three areas within
31   the City, all of which are on Fort Union Boulevard. The intention is for it to apply to areas that
32   are already commercial. The Code is made up of Form Districts which are similar to zones and
33   driven by the intensity of development and the context of where the area is defined. The first
34   District is specific to the Union Park Center with mixed-use, office, and commercial. It will be
35   developed as primarily mixed-use. As currently written, the minimum height will be three stories
36   with a maximum of eight.
37
38   The second Form District is specific to the Town Center Area. The intention is to focus on how
39   this area develops. It was noted that it is not a site plan or specific development. Mr. Morris
40   reported that the Hillside Plaza corner is one part of the Town Center and the Code will apply to
41   the entire Town Center area. The hope was that the Hillside Plaza Development would catalyze
42   additional change as private property redevelops.
43
44   The Third Form District is specific to Fort Union Boulevard and contains larger commercial
45   development along Fort Union. The intensity is less than in the other two Districts and will
46   encourage more intensity of development in commercial areas.

 2   Mr. Morris next described the Residential Transition District. It is typical of a Form-Based Code
 3   and is used to identify areas that make sense to buffer commercial development from the
 4   surrounding single-family neighborhoods. These areas are most often completely developed.
 5   When redevelopment occurs, the Residential Form-Based Code will be applied to any
 6   development change. Boundaries were discussed. Mr. Morris reported that Mixed-Use and Form-
 7   Based Code are complimentary parallels and it makes the most sense to start with those areas.
 8   Mr. Johnson reported that the idea behind Form-Based Code is to determine what works in a
 9   Mixed-Use Zone, such as Canyon Center, versus what works in a Mixed-Use Zone on smaller
10   parcels.
11
12   Commissioner Mills referenced the Cottonwood Mall property and the planned underground
13   structure. He asked if VODA has found anything structurally that would impact the effective
14   grade, even if from the street level. Mr. Morris explained that Form-Based Code does not consider
15   underground portions and is based on the finished grade. It, however, treats ground floor heights
16   separate from upper floor heights, specifically with commercial development. He reported that
17   VODA Landscape + Design has developed a prototype where they have selected a sample parcel
18   that addresses the main parameters of the Form-Based Code. The focus is on the form of
19   development where uses can come and go. The intent is to create something that is infinitely
20   reusable. The prototype goes through site-specific parameters including the following:
21
22          1.      Building location;
23
24          2.      Building footprint; and
25
26          3.      The layout of the parcel.
27
28   Mr. Morris next identified which districts have minimum and maximum heights with ground floor
29   and upper floor height being considered separately. As with any other development, the amount
30   of parking that will fit on the site will drive the determined height. Building height is considered
31   the articulation of a façade. Parking requirements are still a work in progress but are not vastly
32   different from the current requirements. He noted that Form-Based Code strives to put a maximum
33   on parking to allow the applicant to determine how much parking is needed, which makes it more
34   market-driven. Shared parking options may also be considered. Chair Mills believed that most
35   challenges will not be with those patronizing the building but with building employees. The City
36   is faced with the complicating factor that if they want a gathering area, especially in the Town
37   Center, parking must be supplied. Creative solutions were recommended. Mr. Johnson
38   commented that a similar issue took place with the northern Gravel Pit Development where shared
39   parking was implemented with discussion of alternate options. He acknowledged that parking is
40   important and felt that reasonable numbers provide flexibility. Transit-oriented options were
41   discussed.
42

44   with street trees, furnishings, and landscaping to improve the public realm beyond the
45   responsibility of the development. Use of the areas is the first and the last question where there
46   they are giving flexibility to consider multiple use directions. This also considers ground floor

23   2.0    Pickleball Court Ordinance Introduction.
24
25   Chair Mills introduced the Pickleball Court Ordinance discussion and stated that several months
26   earlier there were strong concerns voiced by residents of the community. This matter comes to
27   the Planning Commission by way of the City Council. Mr. Johnson reported that a moratorium
28   was placed on outdoor residential sports courts at a recent City Council Meeting. Concern was
29   expressed with any sports court that is greater than 500 square feet in size. There has been an
30   increase in pickleball courts being built and neighbors expressing concerns over lighting and noise.
31   The moratorium was issued to specify that no residential sports courts be constructed from the date
32   of the Ordinance for a maximum of six months. As a result, the City has a maximum of six months
33   to adopt regulations or the moratorium lifts. What is proposed is an Ordinance in response to that
34   moratorium and issues with noise and lighting. Because the City Council’s direction was to act
35   quickly, the proposed Ordinance focuses particularly on pickleball courts. Staff will return with
36   more general regulations with an immediate emphasis on pickleball courts.
37
38   Mr. Johnson reported that the City has defined General Sports and Indoor Pickleball Courts to
39   clarify that the two are different than the current regulations for outdoor pickleball courts. The
40   intent is to address pickleball courts that are in or adjacent to residential zoning or residential uses
41   to mitigate unique impacts with noise, lighting, and fencing. He stated that the best way to mitigate
42   noise is distance. Requirements may be added for hours of play and specific surfacing, paddles,
43   and equipment but may be difficult to enforce. He reviewed a study that shows that pickleball
44   courts that are 150 to 200 feet from a property line can reach 65 to 70 decibels. This is a widespread
45   issue and the USA Pickleball Association is presently looking to update its standards to specifically
46   address noise impacts. Staff has proposed that a Building Permit be required, that an applicant

 1   include a site plan showing the distance to the property line, and provide fencing details. He
 2   reported that there are no current requirements in most areas of the City and only areas with
 3   groundwater or sensitive lands issues have an impervious surface requirement. Enforcement issues

 5   would now fit the definition of a pickleball court. If it is being used in a manner that does not
 6   comply with the adopted standards, it becomes enforceable.

 8   Staff was proposing a 150-foot setback from property lines to mitigate noise impacts. A parcel-
 9   by-parcel analysis had not been completed. Mr. Johnson stated that this would make it difficult to
10   fit a pickleball court on any single-family residential property in the City. He reported that there
11   are current standards for lighting, fencing, and a current Noise Ordinance, which he did not expect
12   to be modified. The most important matter for consideration was the proposed setback. He
13   reported that the setback may be reduced by one-half with the completion of a qualified noise study
14   to show how the sound will be buffered. Noise levels may not exceed 65 decibels at the property
15   line with the additional mitigation.
16
17   Commissioner Anderson asked if there should be additional clarifying language to specify that
18   they are not responsible for noise. Commissioner Shelton expressed frustration with the potential
19   for the proposed setback to eliminate the construction of tennis courts, pickleball courts, basketball

26   complaint was filed with the Salt Lake County Health Department.
27
28   Commissioner Shelton commented that the primary concern is noise. He was concerned when a
29   use is singled out rather than the specific offense. So far, discussion has focused on the issue of
30   noise. He felt there were multiple ways to mitigate the issue including the use of different
31   equipment. He felt strongly that the City is missing the point if noise is what they are trying to
32   regulate.
33
34   The comment was made that from a use standpoint, the City should look at ways to alleviate noise
35   and address it as a Code Enforcement issue. It was suggested that the focus be on the noise itself
36   rather than from a land use standpoint. Land use primarily detracts from property rights. The
37   comment was made that this issue has more to do with a lack of respect for neighbors and hours
38   of use and should be addressed as noise compliance with the Health Department. The use is
39   difficult to restrict and there was uncertainty as to whether it is within the City’s purview to restrict.
40   It was emphasized that the issue should be considered from a noise standpoint rather than a land
41   use issue. The proposed Ordinance would ban any outdoor activity and use while they should
42   instead encourage residents to spend more time outside.

44   Commissioner Anderson did not disagree with the concerns raised but did not think it was helpful

40          3.1       Project GPA-23-001 – A Public Hearing and Possible Action on an Updated
41                    City-Wide Transportation Master Plan.
42
43   Senior City Engineer, Adam Ginsberg, presented the Staff Report and stated that the purpose of a
44   Transportation Master Plan is to identify the projects that are eligible for impact fees. In 2019, the
45   City completed a 10-year Transportation Master Plan and based on that study, no impact fees were
46   identified. The 2023 Plan will serve as a unifying document to consolidate the previous



     Cottonwood Heights Planning Commission Meeting – 10/04/2023                                         6
 1   Transportation Master Plans including the Wasatch Boulevard Gravel Pit Area Master Plan, the
 2   Fort Union Master Plan, and the Mid-Valley Active Transportation Plan. The Plan objectives
 3   included the following:
 4
 5      •   Enhance neighborhood connectivity and pedestrian safety;
 6
 7      •   Improve active transportation networks to promote use;
 8
 9      •   Provide safe and efficient traffic movement; and
10
11      •   Support regional coordination and cooperation
12
13   Roadway Cross Sections were identified. The four primary classifications include the following:
14
15      •   Freeway/Expressway;
16      •   Urban Core Arterial;
17      •   Urban Arterial;
18      •   Urban Collector; and
19      •   Local Streets.
20
21   A map of the existing roadway network was displayed. Cottonwood Heights separates its active
22   transportation facilities into three categories:
23
24      •   Level 1 Protection offers the most protection. These facilities are separated by grade,
25          physical barriers such as bollards and parked vehicles, and other elements that separate the
26          bicyclists and vehicles.
27
28      •   Level 2 Protection includes a road with striping that designates a bike lane. This can
29          sometimes take the form of a typical bike lane, shoulder space for bicyclists, or a buffered
30          bike lane with increased space between bicyclists and vehicles.
31
32      •   Level 3 Protection includes roads that are shared between bicyclists and vehicles. These
33          roads are sometimes marked with road striping or a sign.
34
35   Mr. Ginsberg reported that Active Transportation Projects are intended to provide a list of
36   improvements that are needed to form the Backbone Network. Prioritizing the development of the
37   Backbone Network will benefit regional connectivity and create a multi-jurisdictionally connected
38   Active Transportation System. The existing Active Transportation Plan was discussed. The model
39   used to develop the Transportation Master Plan is based on the Wasatch Front Regional Council’s
40   (“WFRC”) TAS Model, which was developed for the entire Salt Lake County Region and includes
41   the employment and residential population for different areas. The transportation model uses
42   socioeconomic data that includes both the future demand on the roadway from the existing
43   population and growth in the valley.
44
45   Level of Service was next discussed. Mr. Ginsberg reported that the Level of Service is the process
46   of measuring the delay on a road based on the total number of cars in one day. Level of Service


     Cottonwood Heights Planning Commission Meeting – 10/04/2023                                      7
 1   ranges from ‘A’ which involves a free flow of traffic where users are virtually unimpeded by other
 2   vehicles on the roadway, to ‘F’ where traffic exceeds the operating capacity of the roadway. A
 3   road map rendering was displayed. Mr. Ginsberg explained that the lanes being recommended on
 4   2300 East, 2600 East, and 1700 East will expand from two to three lanes or through the addition
 5   of a dual left turn or center lane. He emphasized that widening the road for additional lanes is not
 6   recommended for any of the projects. The majority of projects will attempt to address pedestrian
 7   safety and walkability. The Plan encourages pedestrian safety and walkability along Wasatch
 8   Boulevard with a multi-use trail. The roadway will also be reconfigured from two to three lanes.
 9
10   Commissioner Anderson asked how projects are prioritized. Mr. Ginsberg responded that the
11   projects are from the 2019 Capital Facilities Master Plan and priorities are determined when
12   identifying the needs of the City. A question was raised regarding parking in a buffered bike lane.
13   Mr. Ginsberg commented that the City Code does not prohibit parking in bike lanes. It was his
14   understanding that buffered bike lanes may include a bike lane with parking. A cross-section
15   rendering was reviewed. Chair Mills wondered if there were any solutions other than completely
16   isolating the bike lane, to prevent cars from being in the car lane. Mr. Ginsberg stated that a
17   shared bike lane is not recommended on a road with speeds greater than 35 MPH and is the reason
18   the buffered bike lane was proposed. It was noted that traffic calming could also be implemented.
19   Mr. Johnson reported that from a planning standpoint, level of service and traffic flow do not
20   always correspond to speed. Design elements can mitigate speed while still maintaining a good
21   level of service to allow vehicles to move efficiently but at safer speeds. The critical element is to
22   ensure that all of the plans are compatible while achieving different goals.
23
24   Commissioner Ebbeler referenced the Streetscape diagram and stated that in other countries,
25   vehicle and bicycle parking has been switched so that cars provide a physical buffer for bicycles.
26   He supported a separation between the two. Mr. Ginsberg commented that the diagram for Level 2
27   comes from a company that has a configuration with car parking on the inside of the road. This
28   design would include a landscape buffer and some sort of curb. He confirmed that placing parking
29   for vehicles on the inside of the lane could be considered.

31   Chair Mills indicated that he is an avid cyclist and while riding, he has had vehicles drive very
32   close to him. He appreciated any efforts to increase safety. Chair Mills encouraged Staff to obtain
33   Strava data to ensure that they understand the movement of bicycles and pedestrians. Buffering
34   issues were discussed.

11                  Use Permit Request for a Detached Accessory Dwelling Unit (“ADU”) in the
12                  Rear Yard of 1787 East 7200 South. Detached ADUs are Allowed as
13                  Conditional Uses in the R-1, RR-1, and F-1 Zones in Cottonwood Heights.
14
15   Associate Planner, Ian Harris, presented the Staff Report and identified the property owner as Ilse
16   Wilson. The subject property is in an R-1-8 Single-Family Zone where detached ADUs are a
17   Conditional Use. The proposed ADU is 10’ x 16’, approximately 13 feet in height, and will be
18   affixed to a permanent foundation. The proposed parking will be provided in the subject property’s
19   existing driveway, which exceeds 100 feet in length. A property rendering was displayed. The
20   ADU is to be located 20 feet from the rear property line, and eight feet from the side property line,

22   Chapters 19.75 and 19.84 of the City Code with the requirement of continually meeting those
23   provisions. There is landscaping and fencing to mitigate the visual impacts of both the parking
24   areas and the ADU entrances on the neighbors. Staff recommended approval subject to the
25   findings and conditions listed in the Staff Report.
