Determination 2020-01, pursuant to Utah Code Annotated 52-4-207(4)

I, Mayor Troy K. Walker, do hereby determine conducting an electronic meeting of the Draper
City Council with an anchor location presents a substantial risk to the health and safety of those
who may be present at the anchor location and hereby authorize the Draper City Council to conduct
electronic meetings without an anchor location.

The foregoing determination is based on the following facts:
   1. Federal, state, and local leaders, including the Draper City Mayor and City Council, have
       all recognized a global pandemic caused by the spread of the COVID-19 virus.
   2. COVID-19 cases continue to fluctuate at high levels and hospitalizations in Salt Lake
       County and Draper continue at high rates, both of which pose a risk of overburdening the
       local health care system.
   3. It is difficult, if not impossible, to predict the number of attendees at any meeting and to
       manage issues regarding social distancing in order to comply with State Phased Guidelines.
   4. COVID-19 poses a continuing and immediate threat to the health, safety, and welfare of
       Draper City residents.
   5. The City has the technological capability to provide means by which the public may hear,
       or view and hear, the open portions of the meeting and to participate in public hearings.
   6. The original of this written determination is on file in the City Recorder's office.

Resident were able to participate through the options outlined below:
   1. Listen through the Draper City website - https://www.draper.ut.us/95/Agendas-Minutes
   2. Email comments to public.comment@draper.ut.us by 5 PM on the day of the meeting.
       These become part of the public record.
   3. Those wishing to speak during the Public Hearing portions of the meeting, send a request
       for the Zoom meeting ID to public.comment@draper.ut.us by noon, August 3, 2020.

1.0   Discussion: Flag Lots – Todd Draper

1.1   Todd Draper, Planning Department Staff, provided an update on the draft of a new flag lot
      ordinance that staff would like to take forward to go through the public process,a review
      by the Planning Commission, and a second review by the City Council. He provided an
      overview of key points that were added to the draft ordinance: (1) Staff updated the purpose
      statement for brevity and to better meet state regulations; (2) There was an addition for a
      preference for development of private lanes over the development of flag lots; (3) Flag lots
      were limited to lot splits and smaller subdivisions; (4) Flag lots were limited to non-
      classified streets; (5) Added specifications to the lot size and design were added. The width
      of the staff was increased to 30 feet with the minimum flag size increased to 1.25 times the
      underlying zone. Staff left in place the 15,000 square foot minimum for the flag area; (7)
      Requirements for the pavement of driveways, landscape screening, and control of drainage
      water were also added; (8) There was an increase in the main structure setbacks to be
      between 25 and 30 feet to make them a uniform depth on all sides; (9) Staff reduced the
      main structure height; (10) There was a reduction in lot coverage; (11) There were also
      increases on the setback distances for accessor structures larger than 200 square feet. After
      outlining these changes, Mr. Draper said that City staff were available for questions.

1.2   Councilmember F. Lowry asked about the reduction in building height. Todd Draper,
      Planning Department Staff, said that the building height was reduced from 35 feet to 30
      feet. Councilmember F. Lowry also asked about the changes to the setbacks. He wanted
      to confirm that this change did not limit the square footage of the structure, but the amount
      that a developer could encroach upon a right of way. Todd Draper confirmed that was
      correct.

2.0   Presentation: Draper City Rebranding Project – Britnee Johnston

2.1   Britnee Johnston, Director of Communications, provided an overview of Draper City’s
      rebranding project including the project goals, phasing, and timeline.

      To begin, Ms. Johnston addressed the question of why rebrand?

      For Draper City, the primary motivation for rebranding was to create consistency across
      departments. She displayed the various logos of organizations and services associated with
      Draper City which demonstrated a wide variety of visual identities. She noted their
      different looks and that this created confusion because one would not know that they came
      from a single entity: Draper City. She emphasized that creating unity across the logos
      enhanced the voice, power, and trust of the brand among residents and employees.

      The second motivation for Draper City’s rebrand was because the City was the home of
      one of Utah’s largest development projects. Ms. Johnston explained that when the prison
      site moved all eyes, local and national, would be on Draper City. She noted that when the
      Point started recruiting businesses and residents to the area, the City would play a big part

in the process with joint communications and marketing efforts. Currently, because Draper
City’s branding and logo were almost 20 years old, they were not of the same quality as
the Point who just created their brand. As a result, communications would look
disconnected.

Britnee Johnston then transitioned into describing the project phases: (1) Logo and
branding guidelines, (2) New logo design templates, (3) Website redesign, (4) Logo and
website launch, and (5) Physical items updated. The staff was currently in the logo and
branding guidelines phase. Ms. Johnston informed the Council that the City had already
hired a design consultant who had worked on some design markups. Staff had engaged
with department heads and received their feedback to refine the markups. As a result, the
staff wanted to involve the City Council to get their feedback on which logos they felt best
represented Draper City. She said that she did not include the logos in the presentation but
would email them to the Council the following morning so that each councilmember could
look at the logos individually.

Ms. Johnston then outlined the rest of the phases. She explained that during phase two,
new logo design templates, instructions would be given to creating continuity between
email signatures, letterhead and business cards, employee ID cards, PowerPoint templates,
etc. Then in stage three, website redesign, the webpage would incorporate the new brand,
feature a new navigation menu, and potentially have a new URL draperutah.gov. The use
of .ut.us was an older website indicator. Ms. Johnston said that now it is more common to
see cities with a .gov address. In the best-case scenario, this would take four months with
a target completion goal of the end of 2020. Once the website was complete, Ms. Johnston
proposed that the City launch the logo and website together as stage four. Following this,
staff would have to take a phased approach to update the logos on physical items like
exterior building signs, the City’s 2,139 street signs, parks and trailhead signs, and the
Welcome to Draper signs. Because of the number of street signs, Ms. Johnston suggested
just doing a sticker overlay as opposed to replace all of the signs to reduce costs.

Ms. Johnston, returned to the current phase of the re-branding. She explained that staff
had provided their consult with some existing research the City had to help guide their
design inspiration. The first of these resources was a 2020 employee survey about what
they would like to see in the new rebranding and logo. 62 respondents described Draper
with the top five words: trails, family, beautiful, parks, and clean. Many of the respondents
wanted to continue to see mountains and trees in the logo. Staff also provided the
consultant with the transcripts of the key stakeholder interviews they conducted in 2020.
These interviews represented 26 key stakeholders such as city officials and department
heads. There was agreement among these individuals that consistency was needed across
the logos. Also, the 2020 well-being and 2019 general resident surveys were provided.

The design consultant conducted a city analysis of neighboring cities to get a sense of their
current branding and logos. These logos were displayed to the Council. Ms. Johnston
noted that cities tend to use blues and greens. She described that Lehi City utilizes their
cityscape, Bluffdale the first letter of their city name, Riverton their city hall dome, where
Cottonwood Heights, West Jordan, and Sandy all featured mountains. Also, the staff

      a little bit of information on the text amendment since there had been little discussion since

      Ms. Jastremsky reminded the Council that the question that had arisen about what the
      proper amount of access points was for ingress and egress for multiple-family projects and
      what features did those access points need to have (emergency only, full access, etc.). As
      a solution, the text amendment would clarify how many vehicular access points were
      required and the type of access required. She explained that the text amendment would be
      broken down based on the type of development: single-family units, two-family units,
      townhome unit developments, and multi-family unit developments other than townhome
      dwellings. For all dwelling types, an access point is defined as:

             A roadway access into a subdivision or development that provides unrestricted
             access to residents, the public, and emergency services. This definition does not
             prohibit a gated community entrance that is designed to service residents, guests,
             and the general public.

      The text amendment proposed the following access point requirements for each dwelling
      type:
             a. Single-family units would be required to have one access point for every 30
                 units. Private rights-of-way could serve up to 20 single-family units. The street
                 width standards of private rights-of-way would be widened to ensure a 20-foot
                 minimum asphalt width.
             b. Two-family units and townhome unit developments would have one access
                 point required for every 30 units.
             c. Multi-family unit developments, other than townhome unit dwellings would
                 require having one access point for projects with up to 100 dwelling units.

2.0         Public Hearing: Ordinance #1447, amending portions of Chapters 9-8 and 9-11 of the
            Draper City Municipal Code as it pertains to the Commercial Zoning Districts. A
            city-initiated text amendment to create a new zoning district called Commercial
            Buffer Zone (CBU). The proposed zone will be located in Chapter 9-11 (Commercial
            Zones) of the Draper City Municipal Code. Other related sections of Title 9 may also
            be amended as part of this proposal.

2.1         Maryann Pickering, a city planner, explained that the item was a city-initiated request to
            recreate the new zoning district. She said that there had been recent concerns about
            commercial development that was located near residential zones that could have potentially
            negative impacts on those residential areas. The Commercial Buffer Zone (CBU) was
            intended to be a lower density commercial zone. She emphasized that staff were not
            planning on rezoning any properties at this time. Any changes to the zone would be
            applicant driven. The proposed zone would be located in chapter 9-11 of the Draper City
            Municipal Code (DCMU) allowing it to be located anywhere in the City if the applicant
            chooses to use this zone. The uses and standards for the zone were based loosely on the
            Town Center (TC) zone. The uses for the CBU would be less intensive than the TC zone,
            but both permitted and conditional uses were included in the chart. The development
            standards for the CBU zone would be similar to the TC zone in that the parking would be
            required to be located behind buildings to give them a street presence and small-town feel,
            all service elements would be screened, lighting and signs would have to comply with
            DCMC, and the maximum building height would be two stories or 30 feet.

            Design standards were also included in the zone. Properties within the proximity of the
            Town Center Zone, if re-zoned to CBU would have to comply with the Town Center zone
            standards. Conversely, if an applicant were to choose to use this zone near Harmon’s or
            across the freeway, they would comply with the standard commercial zone standards.

            Finally, the staff included redevelopment and historic review. Ms. Pickering said that there
            might be a property that has a historic home on it that the applicant would like to rezone to
            the CBU. However, it was important for the City to preserve that building. Therefore, the
            Planning Commission would be able to approve non-conformities to the home regarding
            height and setback requirements. All properties listed on National, State, or local registries
            would need to be reviewed by the Historic Preservation Commission.
