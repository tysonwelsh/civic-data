1.       A presentation regarding the 2020 4th Quarter Financial Report. (20-137) (0:11:34)

John Borget, Administrative Services Director, presented. He noted that this particular report
was not audited, as the audit was currently underway for the past fiscal year. Mr. Borget shared
revenue updates from the final quarter of Fiscal Year 2020. He highlighted the challenges posed
by the coronavirus pandemic and how this has impacted revenues over the last several months.
Sales tax revenue has been strong as the recovery has been felt in the local economy, coming in
above the previous year’s sales tax revenue, as well as higher than the budget projections. Fiscal
year 2022 will be the third year with the Recreation, Arts, and Parks tax in place. Mr. Borget
reviewed other revenue categories, noting that property tax and vehicle fees came in strong. Mr.
Borget reviewed the total appropriations, which he felt reflected good management on the part of
the Council and Mayor. Mr. Borget reviewed the library and golf course revenues, noting the
impacts of the pandemic to these facilities’ operations. Mr. Borget also reviewed the utility
revenues in detail. Several funds’ revenues were up due to the related utility rate increases
associated with capital projects for those functions at the city. Council Chair George Handley
thanked Mr. Borget for the presentation. Presentation only.

3.      A discussion regarding proposed amendments from the Sign Committee to Provo
        City Code regarding electronic sign hold times and permitted locations. (20-101)
        (0:55:24)

Hannah Salzl, Policy Analyst, and Javin Weaver, Planner, presented. Ms. Salzl reviewed the
logic and rationale behind the proposals. She reviewed the policy considerations with different-
length hold times, to which areas of the city they would apply, and responded to questions and
feedback from Councilors. Ms. Salzl first outlined the competing interests and goals of the
committee with regard to these hold time standards; primary purposes included promoting safety
and preserving community aesthetics, as well as facilitating business-friendly conditions. The
current code uses the phrase “churn” but hold time was more the industry standard, so these
changes would align Provo’s code with this more widely used term. Ms. Salzl outlined the
proposed changes to hold times, noting that multi-tenant signs in the SC3 zone representing 20 or
more tenants would have a slightly shorter hold time than the proposed standard, to facilitate
wayfinding and ease of identifying a business in a large complex. Electronic signs were
prohibited in residential and agricultural zones and the downtown area had its own standalone
sign ordinance and regulations. Otherwise, long hold times were permitted anywhere except
agricultural and residential areas, and short hold times were designated in certain areas only.

In response to a question from Councilor Shannon Ellsworth about how the standards would
apply in a corridor containing both residential and commercial properties, Ms. Salzl explained
that signs would be allowed for the commercial properties, and not for residential properties.
This raised a point of discussion which the Council discussed in more detail, specifically as
regarded sections of north University Avenue and south State Street. Councilor David Sewell,
who served on the committee, elaborated on the intent of the ordinance to regulate external
characteristics. He noted that the committee was wary of banning digital signs completely
(besides in the downtown area due to historic design standards), lest such a ban not prove legally
defensible. Ms. Ellsworth asked about the distinctions between residential zones versus areas;
Mr. Sewell noted that the current intent was to leave the code as it was, in specifying a listed set
of agricultural and residential zones in which electronic signs were not permitted.

6.       A presentation regarding the Central Corridor Transit Study's findings. (20-133)
         (2:02:55)

Mary DeLaMare-Schaefer, UTA Regional General Manager, and Claire Woodman, senior
planner at Parametrix, presented. Ms. Woodman shared an update on the Central Corridor
Transit Study (CCTS), which examined the 850 bus on State Street through Utah County from
Provo to Lehi. A driving tenet of this project was to evaluate high-capacity transit improvements
from Lehi to Provo, preferred alternative for transit (alignment and mode), and to have a
transparent and collaborative process with the cities involved. The study resulted in a desired
route and it would utilize a bus rapid transit system with a similar technology as UVX.

Ms. DeLaMare-Schaefer responded to a concern from Councilor George Handley about large
population areas and access to transit and shared some of UTA’s philosophies on the subject.
Ms. Woodman noted that the CCTS was not intended to solve every transit problem in the
County, but that UTA would continue to explore more options for service. She noted some of the
land use planning resources available for cities to consider so that they can support transit
infrastructure as a cohesive part of their land use and transportation planning. Presentation only.

     Councilor David Shipley was interested in reducing confusion that could arise from
        having different mandates in effect from the state and the city. He suggested that the
        Council examine in the coming weeks whether there was any confusing guidance.
