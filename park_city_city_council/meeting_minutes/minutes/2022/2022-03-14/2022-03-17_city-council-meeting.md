14   Community Housing Update and Policy Discussion:
15   Housing Assessment: Jason Glidden, Rhoda Stauffer and Elyse Kats, Affordable
16   Housing Department, presented this item. Stauffer explained the 2021 Housing
17   Assessment was recently finalized. She indicated 86% of the workforce commuted to
18   Park City. She displayed a chart of the work sectors and noted the average annual
19   wage for the top three sectors was under $40,000. She discussed the housing inventory
20   in the City limits and noted it was made up of 30% primary and 70% secondary
21   residences.
22
23   In response to Council Member Gerber's question on job numbers versus commuters,
24   Stauffer stated there were 14,000 jobs in town and 12,700 people commuted to town for
25   those jobs. Council Member Toly asked if the assessment considered seasonal jobs, J1
26   employees, or was it only year-round jobs. Glidden explained the State tracked the jobs
27   and he thought that included seasonal jobs. Council Member Toly wondered if the
28   statistics allowed for the dwellings that had more than the optimal occupancy.
29
30   Housing Goals: Stauffer reviewed the current goals staff worked on based on the 2016
31   Housing Assessment: a housing goal of 800 units by 2026, including 80 new units per
32   year to maintain 15% workforce in City limits, and to focus development within City
33   limits. She indicated the City hoped to build a complete community, and that included
34   housing for every stage of one's life: long-term rentals, multi-income developments,
35   options for protected community members, and the support of small businesses in the
36   City. She stated there was a question on whether the average median income (AMI)
37   was an accurate calculation in determining affordable housing. She defined AMI versus
38   workforce wage (WFW), which was based on who worked in the City. The WFW median
39   wage in the City was much lower than the AMI. She asked that Council clarify what
40   income calculation should be considered in the City. She proposed affordable housing
41   be equal or less than 100% of WFW and attainable housing to be up to 150% of AMI.

38   defined a moratorium as a zoning regulation adopted for a limited time and noted the
39   reason for a moratorium had to have a justification. It would be adopted by Council and
40   was designed to give Council the ability to act quickly. It was designed for emergency
41   situations and it could only be in place for six months. Council Member Gerber asked
42   what would be considered an emergency situation that would require a moratorium.

13   Alex Roy, Senior Transportation Planner, presented this item and indicated the project
14   goals were to improve connectivity, engage the community, be a “Best in Class” transit
15   system and incorporate tactical, relevant innovation. The Short-Range Transit Plan
16   (STRP) was a plan for the next five years. He stated the separation of High Valley
17   Transit from Park City Transit initiated staff to rethink how to deliver service within town.
18   Throughout the process, there were multiple levels of public outreach with stakeholders,
19   including a community survey, open houses and establishing an advisory committee.
20
21   Roy noted survey results showed interest in increasing bus frequency, having more
22   direct routes, and improving connectivity between neighborhoods and downtown.
23   Stakeholders also requested microtransit. Several alternatives were presented for
24   transit including bus rapid transit, bus stop improvements, express routes, local buses,
25   microtransit, park and rides and specialty transit.
26
27   Roy presented three alternatives for new systems. Alternative One would cover new
28   areas and would include microtransit, adding a bus route to Quinn’s Junction Park and
29   Ride, and streamlining bus service by combining routes; Alternative Two would offer
30   fast, direct service, including high frequency and direct routes to key destinations,
31   microtransit, and corridor investment to help improve bus speeds and reliability; and
32   Alternative Three would minimize transfers, including links to important community
33   destinations without bus transfers, microtransit, and connecting bus routes to more
34   areas around Quinn’s Junction. He indicated the next step was to continue to seek
35   public input. He hoped to roll out the preferred alternative and stated it would be

               Rent to Own Program
• City purchases the home
• Renter contributes 1-2% of purchase price as
  “initial home savings fund”
• Each month a percentage of the rent goes
  into “home savings fund”
• After three years, renter has enough money
  for down payment
               Shared Equity Program
• Shared equity programs require a one-time investment into
  each property by the municipality
• In return for the subsidy, the buyer agrees to share any home
  price appreciation at the time of resale with the entity
  providing the subsidy, which helps preserve affordability for
  subsequent homebuyers.
• Shared equity programs are also used by private companies
  (Pacaso) to encourage second home ownership

                         UTAH STATE CODE
      10-9A-504 TEMPORARY LAND USE REGULATION
(1)(a) A municipal legislative body may, without prior consideration of or recommendation from the planning commission,
enact an ordinance establishing a temporary land use regulation for any part or all of the area within the municipality if:
         (i) the legislative body makes a finding of compelling, countervailing public interest; or
         (ii) the area is unregulated.
(b) A temporary land use regulation under Subsection (1)(a) may prohibit or regulate the erection, construction,
reconstruction, or alteration of any building or structure or any subdivision approval.
(c) A temporary land use regulation under Subsection (1)(a) may not impose an impact fee or other financial requirement on
building or development.
(2) The municipal legislative body shall establish a period of limited effect for the ordinance not to exceed six months.
(3)(a) A municipal legislative body may, without prior planning commission consideration or recommendation, enact an
ordinance establishing a temporary land use regulation prohibiting construction, subdivision approval, and other development
activities within an area that is the subject of an Environmental Impact Statement or a Major Investment Study examining the
area as a proposed highway or transportation corridor.
(b) A regulation under Subsection (3)(a):
         (i) may not exceed six months in duration;
         (ii) may be renewed, if requested by the Transportation Commission created under Section 72-1-301, for up to two
         additional six-month periods by ordinance enacted before the expiration of the previous regulation; and
         (iii) notwithstanding Subsections (3)(b)(i) and (ii), is effective only as long as the Environmental Impact Statement or
         Major Investment Study is in progress.
    WHAT IS A MORATORIUM
          A temporary land use regulation or a “moratorium”
                      is a mechanism by which a
ZONING REGULATION IS ADOPTED FOR A LIMITED PERIOD OF TIME
   without having to go through the normal process of public hearings
                      *Utah Code § 10-9a-504
      WHO CAN ENACT A
       MORATORIUM?
                   LOCAL GOVERNMENTS
                    are authorized under the
         Land Use Development and Management Acts
              to enact temporary zoning regulations
and must identify the justification for the temporary regulation,
 the area it affects, and the effective dates for the regulation
HOW IS A MORATORIUM
     ADOPTED?
                  CITY COUNCIL
 ADOPTS A TEMPORARY LAND USE ORDINANCE

  The notice provisions normally applicable to zoning
  ordinances do not apply, and prior consideration by a
  planning commission is not required.

         IN EFFECT?
                 FOR NO MORE THAN SIX MONTHS (180 days)
            The local government must establish the effective period
                             when it adopts the regulation.

                  Utah Code does not provide for an automatic extension
       A temporary regulation prohibiting development in a proposed highway corridor
may be extended for two additional six-month periods (for a total effective period of 18 months),
              but only if requested by the State’s Transportation Commission.
