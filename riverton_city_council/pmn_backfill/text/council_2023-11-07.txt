         Update on Justice Court

Judge Christopher Bown provided an update on the Justice Court, beginning with the
introduction of the new clerk, Christine Carlisle. He said that Ms. Carlisle brings valuable
experience, having worked at the Third District Court and the Federal Drug Court level. He
expressed satisfaction with the performance of the prosecutors and defense counsel since the
passing of Craig. The court holds regular court sessions on Tuesdays, focusing on criminal
cases, while Fridays are flexible for additional matters. The continued use of WebEx for remote
proceedings has proved beneficial in providing access to justice for those who cannot physically
attend. He shared statistics related to case dispositions, emphasizing the importance of timely
resolution. The report included benchmarks for criminal, civil, and traffic cases, with a focus on
meeting goals within specific timeframes.

Councilmember Pierucci asked Judge Bown if he has the resources necessary to see justice
served in a timely manner. In response, Judge Bown shared insights into the challenges of
enforcing timely case resolutions, particularly in traffic cases. The issuance of warrants and
subsequent suspension letters from the Driver's License Division were mentioned as tools to
encourage appearance in court. He acknowledged the limitations of these measures and
considered exploring potential solutions for better enforcement.

Councilmember McCay asked what the most common cases are that are in the Justice Court.
Judge Bown said that the majority of cases fall under the category of traffic offenses, including
speeding, driver's license violations, and lack of insurance. In terms of criminal cases, thefts and
possession of controlled substances were noted as the most prevalent. During the summer
months, code violations, particularly related to weeds, tend to increase. Overall, traffic cases,
especially involving juveniles, constituted a significant portion of the court's caseload.

Councilmember Wells asked about the disposition numbers going down. In response, Judge
Bown mentioned that while he observed a decrease in the number of cases disposed, he
couldn't pinpoint any significant factors contributing to this trend.

Judge Bown said they had achieved an 88% disposal rate for criminal cases, 100% for small
claims, and 95% for traffic cases in the reported period. Over the past three years, only 18
appeals occurred. These included five in small claims, 10 in criminal cases, and three in traffic
cases. Considering the substantial caseload of over 6,650 cases during that period, the appeal
rate remained relatively low. He also presented statistics on the court's average case numbers,

average increased to 212 cases, indicating increased caseload activity.

         Artificial Turf Discussion

Mr. Lethbridge discussed code amendments, particularly focusing on artificial turf regulations.
The concern over artificial turf was prompted by increasing inquiries from businesses and
residents about the permissibility of installing artificial turf and under what conditions. He
presented a summarized version of proposed code amendments, referencing examples from
other cities. Key points included specifications on appearance, such as pile height and color, an
eight-year manufacturer warranty, and addressing issues like bunching and sub-basin
compaction.

Mr. Lethbridge emphasized the need for standards to maintain a natural appearance, outlining
restrictions on usage in drainage ponds and maintenance requirements. Notably, artificial turf
lacks the regenerative qualities of natural grass, leading to discussions about responsibility for
repair costs. The main question posed to the Council was the permissible locations for artificial
turf—whether limited to commercial applications or allowed in residential areas under certain
conditions.

Mr. Lethbridge suggested a potential limit of 50% of the total landscaped area for residential
lots, excluding recreation areas like soccer fields and playgrounds. He expressed confidence in
the ability to establish controls through ordinance implementation, assuring a visually appealing
outcome. Additionally, he addressed concerns about a potential widespread shift to artificial turf,
highlighting the higher cost and shorter lifespan compared to traditional sod.

Mayor Pro Tem McDougal expressed two concerns. The first concern raised was about the
environmental effects of artificial turf, particularly regarding the materials used and their impact
on the environment. The second question posed related to the impact on stormwater
management, acknowledging that artificial turf would not contribute to water conservation in the
same way as natural grass.

Councilmember Pierucci emphasized a commitment to personal property rights, acknowledging
the complexity when those rights impact others' lives. Concerns were raised regarding the use
Page | 6 Minutes – City Council Meeting
November 7, 2023




of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), also known as Forever chemicals, in artificial
turf. He highlighted the presence of PFAS not only in the substrate but also in the blades of
grass. Citing EPA statements about the health risks of PFAS in water, the speaker expressed
worries about potential negative impacts on the community's health and safety, particularly in
relation to water quality.

Councilmember Pierucci also asked about the potential effects of artificial turf on stormwater
management. Acknowledging a lack of expertise, he expressed a desire for information from
experts. Specifically, concerns were raised about the possibility of increased runoff and the
potential heightened risk of flooding with a greater prevalence of artificial turf in the community.

Councilmember Wells shared insights based on having artificial turf in his backyard for
approximately nine years. He said there is considerable maintenance required to keep artificial
turf looking good, emphasizing the importance of the underlying sand base and the need for
professional assistance in maintaining its appearance.

Councilmember McCay raised a question regarding the proposed 50% limit for artificial turf in
the front yard and whether this percentage includes sidewalks and driveways or if it pertains
only to the landscaped area.

Councilmember Buroker provided a personal perspective on the artificial turf debate, citing two
neighboring homes—one with 100% artificial turf and another with a gravel front. She praised
the home with artificial for its aesthetic appeal and well-maintained appearance, and said the
gravel-fronted home was in serious disrepair. She attributed the difference to personal pride in
property ownership and the commitment to maintenance, irrespective of the landscaping
material used. Councilmember Buroker echoed support for reconsidering the 50% limit,
proposing a potential increase to 60%.

Mr. Lethbridge offered insights on the environmental and safety aspects of artificial turf,
addressing concerns raised during the discussion. Regarding turf dots, he clarified that they are
specific to high-traffic sports fields and are not used in residential or commercial applications.
While manufacturers claim the product's environmental safety, he acknowledged the existence
of chemicals in the turf and suggested that as long as it is well-maintained, the risk of leaching is
minimal. He said he would gather more specific data on the environmental impact.

On drainage considerations, Mr. Lethbridge noted that flooding is not expected to be a
significant issue for residential properties, especially when the proper grading and sub-base are
in place. For commercial applications, drainage plans would need to be in accordance with code
language. The permeable nature of artificial turf was highlighted, emphasizing its ability to
accommodate water flow when installed correctly.

Regarding the percentage limit, Mr. Lethbridge highlighted the flexibility observed in other cities'
approaches, such as allowing artificial turf only where landscaping isn't required. He mentioned
that there is no specific magic number and that the decision may hinge on aesthetic
preferences.

In terms of safety, Mr. Lethbridge clarified that, at this point, there is no explicit allowance for
artificial turf in the City code. Even if the Council decides against allowing it, he proposed a text
amendment to clarify the City's stance. He also expressed a willingness to provide more specific
information on drainage concerns and involve engineering staff in the discussion.
Mayor Pro Tem McDougal emphasized the need for a more comprehensive discussion and

thorough exploration of the environmental impact of artificial turf before delving into specifics like
percentage limits. He shared concerns about potential difficulties in addressing issues once
residents have invested substantial amounts of money in artificial turf installations. He
advocated for taking the time to gather robust data and information, seeking perspectives
beyond installers and narrowing down specific product types if the decision moves in the
direction of allowing artificial turf.

Councilmembers asked the staff to seek opinions from the Division of Water Quality and other
experts to provide feedback about artificial turf.

         Commercial Projects

Mr. Lethbridge briefly provided an overview of ongoing and upcoming commercial projects in the
community. His presentation started by noting construction at Holy Trinity Lutheran Church,
located a bit south on Redwood Road. The church is adding a new building to accommodate
their growing needs, particularly for their sanctuary and daycare/preschool operations. While
facing some cost adjustments, the project has progressed well and is considered a positive
investment for the community. Moving east to west along Redwood Road, the speaker
highlighted a retail center under construction near the High School on 12600 South. Though
specific tenants had not been identified at the time of the presentation, the development was
seen as a positive contribution to the area's growth.

Mr. Lethbridge said there was commercial development near the intersection of Bangerter
Highway and 12600 South. He said the flex office warehouse on the corner, which initially
raised some skepticism, has turned out well. Mr. Lethbridge commended the planning staff, led
by Tim Prestwich and Lisa Halversen, for their collaboration with the developer to enhance
architectural standards, contributing to the project's visual appeal. Construction progress has
been swift, and the development is expected to occupy the entire property along the corner.

Councilmember Pierucci asked if developers have been incorporated into part of the City’s
economic development plan to proactively attract tenants. Mr. Lethbridge said yes.

Mr. Lethbridge shifted the focus to an area around 13400 South and Bangerter Highway,
highlighting ongoing commercial development as part of the SLR project. The development,
referred to as Old Liberty Way, involves frontage pads that are in the final stages of approval
before recording. He noted the diversity of tenants, including popular food establishments like
Raising Cane's, Freddy's Steakburgers, and Apollo Burger, along with additional coffee shops
and another Quick Quack. He expressed optimism about the development, emphasizing its
significance and the role it will play in supporting the City's goals, including improved
infrastructure and commercial diversity.

Mr. Lethbridge continued to provide updates on various development projects in the City. There
is a residential development just east of Costco, consisting of rental properties, that has been
successful and beneficial for the surrounding commercial areas. Mountain View Village, while
nearly built out, continues to attract new businesses, including Shake Shack, Panera, and Soul
Foods. The development has adapted to market changes by dividing office space to
accommodate smaller users, contributing positively to the City's tax base and community
offerings.

View involving Edge Homes. Edge Homes acquired a property with a medical office and car
