5. PUBLIC HEARING, CONSIDERATION, AND RECOMMENDATION on a
Proposed Text Amendment to Bluffdale City Code Section 7.040.030 to Adopt a List
of Street Trees Permitted within or Abutting Power Corridors, and to Sections
11.150.050, _11.160.290, and_12.80.060, Noting that Trees may also be Subject to
Spacing Requirements of Utility Providers (Application 2025-38). City of Bluffdale,
Applicant. Ellen Oakman, Staff Presenter.
Associate City Planner, Ellen Oakman, presented the Staff Report and stated that the Text
Amendments were requested by Rocky Mountain Power to prevent large trees from being planted
near transmission and distribution power lines and protect future electrical infrastructure. The
changes would affect both street trees and trees planted near power lines on commercial and
residential properties.
Rocky Mountain Power provided the City with a guide regarding appropriate distances from power
lines for different-sized trees. Low-growing trees that reach less than 25 feet in height when
mature ones can be adjacent to overhead power lines. Medium trees that reach 25 to 35 feet in
height must be a minimum of 25 feet away from overhead power lines. Large trees that reach over
35 feet in height must be a minimum of 50 feet away from overhead power lines.
Language was also added to indicate that other public utility companies, such as sewer, telecom,
and gas providers may have additional landscaping or tree requirements near utility equipment or
corridors. For example, Dominion Energy does not allow trees to be more than 15 feet tall along
power lines. CenturyLink requires trees and shrubs to be trimmed or cleared if they are in the way
of communication cables.
Ms. Oakman reported that only seven species from the existing list of approved street trees were
allowed by Rocky Mountain Power. Subsection 7.40.030(D) lists the seven approved species:
e Amur maple;
e Cockspur;
e Eastern redbud;
e English hawthorn;

6. Work Session to Review the Draft Integrated Water and Land Use Element of the

General Plan as Required by SB-110 (2022) and SB-76 (2023). Caitlyn Tubbs and

Daniel Tracer, Staff Presenters.
Ms. Tubbs presented a video on the purpose of the Integrated Water and Land Use Element. The
City’s 2021 Water Conservation Plan addressed a majority of the items required by State Code,
and the draft would address remaining items, such as consultations with the City’s water provider
and the Utah Department of Water Resources. The Planning Commission would make a
recommendation to the City Council at its December 3, 2025, meeting.
The Integrated Water and Land Use Element begins with an introduction outlining the passage of
SB-76 and its associated requirements, as well as the City’s existing Water Conservation Element
and water system. The City currently has approximately 5,400 culinary water connections spread
across residential, commercial, industrial, and institutional users. A map of the water system’s
pressure zones is included.
The City’s water demand has grown an average of 5% or approximately 60,000 gallons per year.
Assistant City Engineer, Dan Tracer, reported that conservation efforts during the 2021/2022
drought were very effective in reducing water usage. However, that was followed by good
snowpack years and decreased conservation efforts, which resulted in increased usage. The graph
included in the report shows that significant savings can be realized when the public embraces
conservation efforts.
Ms. Tubbs reported that City Staff met with representatives from the Jordan Valley Water
Conservancy District (““JVWCD”) in February 2025 to review future development plans and
anticipated water needs. The JVWCD indicated that they have available water to supply all

In response to a follow-up question from Commissioner Flynn, Mr. Tracer clarified that the

Mr. Tracer reported that Bluffdale uses approximately 1.1 billion gallons of drinking water each

year. There are approximately 5,400 connections, of which approximately 5,200 are residential

users that account for 75% of water usage. Institutional users include schools, churches, parks,

and the Utah Data Center, each of which consumes approximately 2 million gallons of culinary

water per year.

The most effective direction for water conservation is landscaping because it has the greatest

demand. Inside residential use is only 10,000 to 20,000 gallons per year, but residential users

consume approximately 110,000 gallons per year to irrigate the outside of their homes.

Ms. Tubbs stated that landscaped open space is the largest consumer of culinary water. The City

Council adopted the Parks, Open Space, Recreation, and Trails Master Plan in September 2025,

which indicated that Bluffdale currently has 92 acres of City-maintained parks, not including

regional parks and trailheads. The City will need to develop an additional 12 acres of parks by

2050 to maintain its existing level of service. The plan recommended that the City implement

some non-traditional park features to maximize use of the area while reducing the irrigable area,

similar to what was done in Day Ranch Park, which has waterwise landscaping with a walkway

and seating areas. It is active open space, but its water demand is significantly reduced.

In response to a question raised by Vice-Chair Swanson, Ms. Tubbs stated that she would obtain

information regarding the lifespan of the artificial turf at Day Ranch Park. Commissioner Flynn

asked if more artificial turf could be incorporated without raising costs. Mr. Tracer stated that the

JVWCD and Utah Division of Water Resources’ position is that plants provide many benefits.

They are pretty to look at, and they aid in stormwater infiltration by absorbing water or allowing
; it to seep slowly into the ground. Artificial turf seals off and heats the ground, and as a result, it

kills off all microbial life and creates a dead zone below it. It also does not provide the same

cooling effect as vegetation. The JVWCD’s position is that plantings, trees, and grass should be

encouraged in a waterwise way to ensure that there is enough water for everyone. Their approach

is to encourage less grass, more native plants, and trees that require less water.

Vice-Chair Swanson stated that natural grass surfaces are necessary for many youth athletic
programs, but low-water-use areas need to be made inviting and usable as well. Mr. Tracer
clarified that artificial turf can be used by the City, but it is not a requirement and is not eligible
for the “Flip Your Strip” rebates.
Ms. Tubbs reviewed Bluffdale’s water conservation goals as outlined in the draft document. Table
4-2 reports on the status of each of the 2021 plan goals and identifies next steps. Several of the
goals have been met, and the next steps are ongoing education and outreach. An incomplete goal
is to encourage metering of private secondary water connections, which would help residents
understand how much water is used to irrigate their landscaping.
Commissioner Flynn asked how many homes do not have metered secondary water connections.
Mr. Tracer reported that the City did not have that information. The goal is to encourage metered
connections, but the City cannot require meters on systems it does not own and maintain.
However, it is becoming a problem. In Spring 2025, the Utah Lake District Canal Board asked for
his assistance in determining why their water was running dry near West Jordan. Fifty percent of
the water used by Bluffdale’s pressurized irrigation system is unaccounted for. Many connections
were not recorded when they were installed.
Commissioner Flynn suggested that a survey be conducted to determine where the connections are
and noted that newer homes have metered connections and are billed for their usage. Mr. Tracer
confirmed that many connections are not being billed, and the City does not even know where they
are located. The pipes are buried and cannot be easily traced. Commissioner Flynn asked if a
company with ground-penetrating radar could be retained to find the connections. Mr. Tracer
stated that Staff is trying to do as much as they can in-house to be good stewards of City resources.
They are speaking with as many residents as possible to locate connections. When areas of the
system are isolated, they often receive phone calls from residents who did not know they were
connected to it. They are currently focusing on the area around 3200 West. When the new pump
station was installed, they were able to identify 50 homes on the line. Commissioner Flynn noted
that paying to identify the connections would pay off in the long term because those residents
would begin paying for secondary water.

The final goal Staff requested feedback on was to encourage conservation through high water rates.
for customers who use more than 50,000 gallons monthly. Mr. Tracer stated that Tier 3 users
consume between 50,000 and 100,000 gallons, and Tier 4 users consume 100,000 gallons or more
per month. Increasing rates for those tiers would financially incentivize conservation. More
aggressive tiers could already be implemented in severe or extreme drought conditions, but the
City had the option to set higher rates for high water users.
In response to a question raised by Vice-Chair Swanson, Mr. Tracer reported that the City adopted
the drought rates approximately two years previously. Tier 1 users of less than 12,000 gallons per
month would not be affected. Tier 2 users of 12,000 to 15,000 gallons would see a slight increase.
Tier 3 and Tier 4 rates would increase more drastically. The rates are per property and not
normalized by property size. Tier 1 reflects average interior water demand.
Commissioner Luker asked how the City would utilize any additional income from increased rates
and if it could be utilized for water conservation efforts. Mr. Tracer stated that the Planning
Commission could suggest that the City Council consider allocating the funds directly to
conservation efforts. The Commission agreed that this item should be discussed in further detail.

Ms. Tubbs reported that the City consulted with the Utah Division of Water Resources as required

Is Planning Commission Training.
Ms. Tubbs reported that Planning Commissioners are required to complete four hours of training
per year consisting of one hour on general powers and duties, and three hours on assorted land
uses. Attendance at 12 meetings within one calendar year counts as one training hour.
Commissioners must report their training hours to Staff so that proper records can be maintained.
Anyone who needs to complete additional training was encouraged to visit the Training Zone on
the Land Use Academy of Utah website at www.luau.utah.gov. Commissioners could request in-
person training or watch training videos from the online training catalog. The Planning
Commission then reviewed videos on Conditional Use Permits (“CUP”).
Ms. Tubbs stated that cities are not required to have conditional uses in their zoning code.
Bluffdale still allows some conditional uses, but City Code has been revised over time to tailor
uses to their respective zones and ensure that projects that are not a good fit for a zone do not have
to be approved as conditional uses. The Planning Commission had previously expressed interest
in reviewing the Land Use Tables to determine if identified uses are appropriate for each zone.
Ms. Tubbs noted that hospitals are only allowed as a conditional use due to their high anticipated
impacts. However, any conditions imposed on a CUP must already be adopted in City Code, so

Vice-Chair Swanson asked if allowed conditional uses were mapped to the applicable standards.

Mr. Crowell stated that when Bluffdale was incorporated almost 50 years ago, it did not have
developed site planning or engineering standards. Many different standards have been developed
since that time. The State Legislature acted in response to real-time events regarding conditional
uses. For example, rural county commissioners could issue CUPs for any use, including those not
listed in the Land Use Table.
Vice-Chair Swanson asked if the proposed carwash near Smith’s Grocery would have been a
conditional use. Mr. Crowell stated that the zone was a good example of how negative impacts
were addressed through legislative action by setting specific standards for the zone. A carwash
would be a permitted use north of Smith’s, but not in other areas, and it would not be allowed as a
conditional use. Quick Quack Car Wash had submitted a preapplication, and the zone standards
were now so specific that it would primarily be an administrative decision.
Mr. Crowell used the example of hospitals and indicated that any conditional use the City does not
want in a zone should be removed from the Land Use Table. If an applicant wishes to pursue that
use, they can apply for a Text Amendment and convince the City Council of the merits of allowing
it in a zone or suggest a Development Agreement.
Commissioner Flynn asked if the City could prohibit any use or if that was restricted by the State.
Mr. Crowell stated that there are some preemptions. Internal accessory dwelling units can only be
prohibited in very limited circumstances. Charter schools cannot be prohibited. For example,
Summit Academy on 14400 South could not be prohibited, but the City could speak with them
about traffic. Federal and state activities cannot be prohibited. Certain conditions cannot be
imposed on single-family homes unless they are part of a Development Agreement.
