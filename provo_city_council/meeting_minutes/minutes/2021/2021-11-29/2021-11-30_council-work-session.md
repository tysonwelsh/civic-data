 1.      A discussion regarding the appointment of City Constable. (21-111) (0:13:16)

John Borget, Administrative Services Director, presented. Provo City had been working with
Bob Connor as the city constable with All-Pro Security since the Justice Court was opened in
2007. Mr. Connor was reappointed in 2017 to serve another four-year term. The appointment
expired in July 2021, but they have continued to provide the service until a new appointment
could be made.


Provo City Municipal Council Work Meeting Draft – November 30, 2021                                  Page 1 of 12
Mr. Borget said 2019 changes to Utah State Code 17-25a moved the responsibility of appointing
a constable to the legislative body of a city. The legislative body would establish a nominating
commission consisting of one member of the city legislative governing body, one judge, the city
attorney, the chief of police, and one private citizen. The entire commission would review each
applicant’s credentials and make a recommendation to the Council. Constables were now
appointed for a term of six years instead of four years and could serve more than one term if
reappointed by the appointing body.

Mr. Borget said that, since the code also allowed a currently appointed constable to be
reappointed for a six-year term without establishing a nominating commission, the council could
just reappoint Mr. Connor. Mr. Borget provided a letter from Justice Court Judge Romney and
ReAnnun Newton (Justice Court Administrator) asking the council to reappoint Bob Connor as
the city constable for a six-year term.

Mr. Borget said there were only two vendors in the state that provided the services that we need,
including transports, Justice Court security, and warrant service. One was All Pro Security and
the other was Wasatch Constables out of Ogden. He noted that All Pro Security had not raised
their rates since 2007. They notified us that their rates would increase from $26/hour per deputy
to $32/hour per deputy beginning January 1, 2022.

In response to a question from Councilor Ellsworth, Brian Jones (City Attorney) replied that the
law used to say “the City” would appoint a constable. In 2019, the law was amended to state the
“legislative governing body of the City” would make the decision. He pointed out that the code
states the legislative governing body could establish a nominating commission or they could
reappoint the current constable for successive terms.

Councilor Ellsworth felt it was a foregone conclusion about who the constable should be;
however, she wanted the council to have more oversight. They should establish a nominating
commission to oversee the appointment. In six years, the next council could decide what they
wanted to do.

Chair Sewell and Councilor Handley were supportive of moving forward with a reappointment
of Bob Connor.

Councilor Ellsworth wanted to continue this item since they only had four council members
present. She made the following motion:

Motion:         Councilor Ellsworth made a motion to continue this item. The motion was
                seconded by Councilor Hoban.

Mr. Jones said this issue did not need to be discussed in a public meeting; however, a resolution
reappointing a constable would need to be approved in a public council meeting – usually the
evening meeting. The decision to establish a nominating commission did not need to happen in a
public meeting. If four or more councilors submitted a written request to establish a commission,
and recommended commission members, it would begin the process without coming back to a
public meeting.

 2.      A presentation from the Housing Committee regarding an ordinance change for
         ADU’s. (21-112) (0:35:10)

John Magness, Council Policy Analyst, presented. He said the Housing Committee had come to
a point where some decisions needed to be made about accessory dwelling units (ADU’s). The
following four items were issues that the committee did not have consensus on.

Internal Connection
        Do we want to require an internal connection with the primary dwelling unit? The law
        passed in 2019 (HB82) took away our ability to enforce that requirement because it did
        not allow us to make requirements to the interior of the building. Councilor Harding, after
        a conversation with the sponsor of HB82, said the legislature would possibly amend
        HB82 in the next legislative session to allow us to make that requirement.

Occupancy Levels
      The committee needed direction on was the occupancy level should be. Currently, Provo
      City allowed two unrelated adults in the A-overlay. The S-overlay allowed two unrelated
      adults with the ability to obtain a special permit allowing four if they met certain
      requirements. HB82 said you could not create an ordinance with an occupancy level of
      less than three unrelated adults. He said our legal department felt we could keep our
      occupancy level at two in the A-overlay because our ordinance predated HB82. In
      response to a question from Councilor Ellsworth, Mr. Jones stated that the RC zone
      allowed ADU’s and was not covered by an overlay so it would allow three unrelated
      adults.

Allowed areas of the City
      The Housing Committee agreed that ADU’s should be allowed in all R1 (One-Family
      Residential) zones, the RC (Residential Conservation) zones, the A (Accessory
      Apartment) overlay zones, and the A1 (Agriculture) zones. The Planning Commission
      approved the proposed ordinance, which included all these zones.

Removal of the A and S Overlays
     There was consensus to remove the A and S overlays in all zones to make it less
     confusing and just have one occupancy level.

Council members shared their concerns and thoughts about the four items.
    Councilor Ellsworth hoped to make the ordinance simple and easy to live by. We should
      not require an internal connection because it would not be wise to create a law that was
      not enforceable. The ordinance could be updated if the legislature changed the law. We

       needed a rationale for determining which areas of the city the ADU’s should be allowed.
       She preferred to have them allowed more broadly in the city because it incentivized home
       ownership and expanded private property rights. She would be in favor of allowing
       ADU’s in the R1.1 zone if they did not have the A and S overlay.
    Mr. Jones said that Councilor Harding had expressed concern that if we do not require an

       legislature would amend HB82, and allow us to require an internal connection, why not

    Councilor Hoban said he liked having an internal connection in the home. An ADU with

    Councilor Handley liked the idea of requiring an internal connection. He did not want to

    Councilor Fillmore was against expanding ADU’s broadly throughout the city. It was

    Mr. Jones said the only reason to have the A and S overlay was for occupancy levels. If

       That way, they could eliminate the overlays regardless of whether or not ADU’s were
       allowed citywide. He said if the Council did not come to a consensus on these issues, we
       could not enforce any ADU rules currently on the books.

 4.      A discussion regarding the Provo River Delta Restoration Agreement. (21-083)
         (2:05:43)

Wayne Parker, Provo City CAO, presented. For the last several years, city staff and various
federal and state partners have been working on a series of agreements related to the Provo River
Delta Restoration Project. The project was part of the June Sucker Recovery Program. It was
needed in order to continue receiving federal funding for water development programs in the
central part of the state.

Provo City’s primary concerns with the project have been related to the proximity of the restored
delta to the Provo Airport. The success of the June Sucker program could increase the risk of
bird strikes at the airport. They have been working with consultants to make sure the delta would
not lend itself to a significant water habitat for large waterfowl that would be a hazard to a jet
engine. There was also a concern about long-term maintenance. If the slopes were not
maintained, we could change the nature of the habitat.


Provo City worked with federal partners to come up with agreements to address the level of
security for design, operation, and maintenance. The parties involved in the development of the
agreements included the Utah Reclamation and Mitigation Commission (part of the US
Department of the Interior), Central Utah Water Conservancy District (CUWCD), Utah County,
the Utah Department of Natural Resources (DNR), the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources
(DWR), the United States Department of Agriculture Wildlife Services (USDA), Provo City, and
our respective legal counsels, consultants, and others.

Several agreements will come back to council for final approval. This first agreement, the
Wildlife Hazard Mitigation Agreement, established the large framework around all the other
agreements. There may be a few tweaks to the agreement before it was presented as an action
agenda item in two weeks.

Mr. Parker reviewed some of the highlights of the agreement.
    Defined the process for minimizing waterfowl issues at the airport.
    Provided an agreement between Provo City and USDA to jointly fund a wildlife
       biologist. This would be a federal position, but the biologist would be housed at the
       Provo Airport. The biologist would monitor the status of the delta restoration area. They
       would ensure the harmless hazing of waterfowl near the runways and make sure we were
       following all the ground rules.
    Provided an agreement with the CUWCD to provide funding if the project was out of
       budget and federal and state partners did not come to the table. If required, a non-lapsing
       appropriation of $100,000 could be drawn on for minor work at the delta. The fund



Provo City Municipal Council Work Meeting Draft – November 30, 2021                     Page 6 of 12
          would be replenished if used. This was a significant concession on the part of the
          CUWCD.
         Created a working group, a small group of stakeholders, that would work together on an
          ongoing basis. As the biologist defined work that needed to be done at the delta, the
          working group could study and approve it.
         Created a super group, one level up from the working group, to look at the bigger picture
          and resolve any conflicts within the working group.
         Adopt, by reference, a Wildlife Hazard Mitigation Plan.
         Adopt, by reference, all necessary permits between the city, state, and federal partners to
          allow reciprocal access to common areas.

In response to questions from councilors, Mr. Parker said we were already managing bird strike
risks at the airport by using hazing strategies (loud noises) to get birds to move off runways.
There would not be a lot of incremental changes at the airport. The Salt Lake Airport had a
similar arrangement to manage wetlands near the airport. The fundamental philosophies on both
sides needed to meet in the middle. The airport wanted to protect human life while other
agencies were concerned about environmental issues. Provo City was among the last to approve
this agreement so all partners in the state have already bought into the language. Presentation
only. Will be placed on the action agenda for the December 14, 2021 regular council meeting.

 5.        A discussion regarding the public input process for the consideration of a potential
           UVX Station on 900 East. (21-115) (2:26:45)

Dixon Holmes, Assistant CAO, presented. When the Utah Valley Express (UVX) project
(formerly BRT) began, there was a planned stop on 900 East, just south of the BYU Creamery.
Due to quite a bit of opposition from the neighborhood, the stop on 900 East was eliminated and
two stops (one south of campus and the other by the MTC) were built. At that time there was not
a commitment or agreement that a stop on 900 East would never happen. About a year ago,
Provo City staff started receiving emails requesting a UVX station on 900 East between Campus
Lane and Birch Lane. The City had not taken an official position of support or opposition to this
request. We agreed to commence a process of providing information and receiving feedback
from the public. The administration was not asking for a final decision to be made at this time.
The request before the council was to determine whether we should continue to move forward.

The Mayor’s Office, in conjunction with the Utah Transit Authority, Mountainlands Association
of Government, and additional Provo City staff recently hosted two public open houses for
gathering public input on the question and interest in a potential UVX station on 900 East,
approximately between Campus Lane and Birch Lane. These events took place on October 13th
and 20th at the Wasatch Elementary School and multi-purpose room at the Provo City
Recreation Center, respectively, each for about an hour. Concurrent to this time frame a poll was
open on Open City Hall web portal of the Provo City Municipal Council web page.

Open City Hall, while not statistically significant, was a way to gauge public opinion. Responses
were eight to one in favor of a station on 900 E. Surveys taken at the open houses were five to
one in favor.

 6.      An ordinance amending Provo City code to comply with current state law
         regarding fireworks sale, distribution, and discharge of Class C consumer
         fireworks. (21-113) (2:46:31)

Lynn Schofield, Provo City Fire Marshall, presented a report on the 2021 fireworks season.

Provo City Municipal Council Work Meeting Draft – November 30, 2021                       Page 8 of 12
During this year's extreme drought, city officials sought creative ways to allow individuals to
still use fireworks but in a safer, controlled way. As such, the 2021 firework season was a
success for the city. To set the city up for success again regarding firework use and to align city
code with current state statute, this ordinance is proposed to make some changes about Discharge
Restricted Areas and revisions to match state laws.

Prior to 2011, fireworks were classified as “safe and sane” and could not go more than 10 feet
off the ground. In 2011, the Utah State Legislature allowed consumers to purchase and use aerial
cakes that discharged as high as 125 feet off the ground. They allowed a dangerous new firework
but did not allow the city to enact ordinances that were more restrictive. The city chose to
establish clearly identifiable restricted discharge areas and grant access to five parks to allow
shooting fireworks in the restricted areas. The fire department actively patrolled all restricted
areas during the open fireworks windows.

Because of the extreme drought in the 2021 fireworks season, there were many calls to ban
fireworks. We did not think it was legal for cities to ban shooting off fireworks or even selling
fireworks. Many firework vendors were mom and pop organizations, or clubs using the sales to
supplement income or pay for activities. We felt it would harm our citizens to ban sales. Instead,
the fire chief, mayor, CAO, city attorney, and fire marshal made the following recommendations:
     Increase the number of parks available for citizens to use their consumer fireworks from
         five to thirteen.
     Engage a social media campaign to encourage the residents to go to the parks.
     Aggressively enforce the Discharge Restricted Areas.
     Provide additional staffing of brush trucks.
     Trust our citizens to do the right thing.

Going forward, the proposed ordinance would be amended to continue allowing fireworks in the
thirteen parks, bring the city code more in line with Utah Code Section 53.7(2) and Section 11.3,
make minor adjustments in the discharge restricted areas and increase the amount of liability
from $1 million to $3 million.

In response to comments from councilors, Chief Miguel replied that the goal was to move
fireworks out of neighborhoods and into areas that were safer and irrigated. He did not think it
increased the use, just consolidated them. The legislature had not taken any serious action toward
nuisance concerns. Noise and air pollution calls will continue to be a problem, but the more we
move them out of neighborhoods to parks, those calls will be reduced. Presentation only – this
item will be placed on the action agenda for the December 14, 2021 regular council meeting.

 8.      A presentation regarding how elected officials and community leaders can
         strengthen the impact of girls and women in Provo. (21-117) (3:22:03)

As part of the Provo City Council's ongoing discussions on diversity and inclusion efforts
throughout the city, this presentation provides a look into best practices for various stakeholders
interested in supporting and empowering Utah girls and women.

Dr. Susan R. Madsen, Inaugural Karen Haight Huntsman Endowed Professor of Leadership Jon
M. Huntsman School of Business, Utah State University, presented. Dr. Madsen had worked to
strengthen the impact of women and girls globally for more than 20 years. For the past 13 years,
Dr. Madsen had worked specifically in Utah on the Utah Women & Leadership Project.

The mission of the Utah Women & Leadership Project was to strengthen the impact of Utah girls
and women by producing relevant research, creating valuable resources, and convening training
and events to ignite growth and change for all Utahns. Everyone (including individuals, teams,
organizations, communities, governments, and societies) benefitted by strengthening girls and
women. Dr. Madsen said the reason we need more women leaders in Utah was to:
    Improve Financial Performance
    Strengthen the Organizational Climate
           o When both men and women were leading and had a voice, there was decreased
               turnover, higher employee satisfaction, more women hired for the pipeline,
               smaller gender pay gap, and increased productivity.
    Leverage Talent
           o The study showed that women ask different questions, bring different
               experiences, were sensitive to nonverbal cues, and had different leadership styles.
               Women were more inclined to fund education, healthcare, and social programs.
    Increase Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) & Reputation
    Enhance Innovation & Collective Intelligence

Dr. Madsen said this was a wicked, very complex problem. If it were simple, it would not matter.
Research had shown that it helped to have both men and women on teams. A study, using teams
of five men and other teams with four men and one women, showed that teams with one women
outperformed the rest of the teams. We should seek win-win solutions and have equal
representation on teams and in organizations.

So what can we do to strengthen the impact of women and girls in Utah?
