34   4. Presentation and Discussion of Attainable Housing
35      The Mayor presented a proposal for an attainable housing overlay zone that would provide
36      incentives for developers to build smaller, more affordable homes. He emphasized the
37      importance of providing attainable, starter home options for the community, drawing on his
38      own experience growing up in a small, 900 square foot home. He felt this type of housing was
39      currently missing in Lehi and could give young families an opportunity to build equity. The key
40      elements of the proposal include: Offering a 50% density bonus for developers who build homes
41      1,200 square feet or less on 6,000 square foot lots. This is intended to offset the lower profit
42      margins on smaller, more affordable units. The city would work with engineering to ensure the
43      increased density does not exceed infrastructure capacity. Requiring the homes to be deed-
44      restricted for owner-occupancy to prevent them from becoming investment properties. The
45      Mayor suggested keeping the eligibility open rather than restricting it to certain professions.
46      Adopting a new sub-local street standard with a 30-foot road and mountable curbs to reduce
47      costs. Allowing the home owner to be responsible for sweat equity items like garages and

48      landscaping, rather than requiring them upfront from the developer. The goal is to create a
49      simplified, incentive-based process to facilitate the development of attainable, starter homes
50      that attainable to a wider range of residents. The Mayor was a strong proponent of the attainable
51      housing concept, seeing it as an important tool to create more affordable homeownership
52      opportunities for Lehi residents.
53
54      The discussion covered topics like density bonuses, infrastructure impacts, deed restrictions,
55      and engaging the community. Overall, the presentation explored ways the city could facilitate
56      the development of more attainable housing through zoning, incentives, and partnerships.
57
58      The Council members engaged in a discussion, raising various questions and concerns about
59      the attainable housing proposal. Key topics included the potential impact on rental prices, the
60      need to balance different housing types, and the importance of not exceeding infrastructure
61      capacity. There were discussions around the mechanics of the density bonus. Councilors
62      Stallings and Condie questioned whether a bonus density approach was necessary or if a new
63      zoning designation would be more appropriate. Also discussed was how the overlay would
64      allow developers to participate in the affordable housing program voluntarily through a
65      development agreement. There were discussions about the need for deed restrictions to ensure
66      owner-occupancy and flexibility for future home expansions. There were concerns raised about
67      ensuring fair distribution of density among multiple property owners and the potential for one
68      owner to benefit disproportionately.
69
70      Cameron Diehl, Executive Director of the Utah League of Cities and Towns, provided insights
71      during the discussion. He explained that the state had created several new tools last year to help
72      address the infrastructure challenges that have been hindering housing development. Diehl
73      noted that the league had brought data to the governor and legislative leadership showing the
74      large number of planned housing units that were stuck due to infrastructure issues. Diehl
75      expressed that the concept of a density bonus in exchange for affordable, owner-occupied
76      housing has generated significant interest among city leaders across the state. He indicated that
77      many Mayors he has spoken with are open to exploring this type of approach, as they are all
78      concerned about the future of homeownership in Utah. He emphasized the importance of cities
79      working together to find solutions, rather than just reacting to state proposals. He suggested that
80      Lehi's attainable housing model could be replicated in other communities if the mechanics can
81      be worked out to balance the needs of both cities and developers.
82
83      Councilor Newall expressed support for the overall direction of the attainable housing proposal,
84      stating that it addressed many of the concerns she had with the previous affordable housing
85      plans. Regarding the bonus density approach, Newall emphasized the importance of ensuring
86      the density bonus is tied to the requirement to build the smaller, more affordable home sizes.
87      She felt this was key to actually achieving the goal of creating attainable housing, rather than
88      just allowing developers to maximize profits on larger homes. Newall also highlighted the need
89      to carefully consider community amenities and architectural differentiation, even in these
90      smaller, more affordable developments. She wanted to make sure livability and aesthetics were
91      not sacrificed in the pursuit of affordability. Overall, Newall was generally supportive of the
92      overlay zone concept and bonus density incentives, as long as they were structured in a way

 93      that truly delivered attainable, owner-occupied housing options for the community. She wanted
 94      to ensure the details were well-crafted to meet the city's goals.
 95
 96      Councilor Stallings was generally supportive of the direction. She emphasized the importance
 97      of protecting the city's existing affordable housing stock, not just focusing on new construction.
 98      Stallings questioned whether the bonus density incentive was truly necessary, suggesting the
 99      city could simply create a new zoning designation that allows the smaller, more affordable home
100      sizes without the added density. She was concerned about the city potentially overstepping by
101      offering the bonus. At the same time, Stallings acknowledged the need to be very thoughtful
102      about where this type of attainable housing overlay is applied, to ensure it fits well with the
103      surrounding neighborhoods and does not create undue burdens on infrastructure or traffic.
104      Overall, Stallings was open to the attainable housing concept, but wanted to ensure the city's
105      role was appropriate and that the implementation was carefully considered to benefit the entire
106      community.
107
108      Councilor Condie expressed support for the overall direction of the attainable housing proposal,
109      but raised some important considerations. Specifically, he would like to know the impact this
110      may have on rental prices. Regarding the bonus density approach, Condie understood the
111      rationale, but was concerned about the potential for developers to request exceptions and try to
112      apply the overlay in areas that may not be appropriate. He emphasized the need for careful
113      analysis to ensure the increased density can be supported by the existing infrastructure.
114
115      Councilor Hancock raised several considerations about the details of the attainable housing
116      overlay. He was concerned about validating the assumption that smaller home sizes would
117      automatically result in reduced infrastructure demands, noting that overall household size and
118      fixture units may not decrease as much as expected. Hancock suggested the Council should
119      consider putting limits on how much the density can be increased, especially in areas currently
120      zoned for larger lots, to avoid dramatic changes without proper analysis. He liked the idea of
121      providing pre-designed expansion options to help make the smaller attainable homes more
122      livable long-term. Hancock also noted the importance of ensuring adequate community
123      amenities, even in these higher density developments, to maintain a good quality of life. Overall,
124      Hancock was supportive of the general direction but wanted to ensure the specifics were
125      carefully crafted to address infrastructure, density, and livability concerns.
126
127      Paige Albrecht expressed general support for the direction of the attainable housing initiative,
128      stating that she felt it was heading in a good direction. She appreciated the plan to have the
129      overlay on the general plan, as it would make the opportunity known to property owners upfront.
130      Albrecht raised a question about whether there should be limits on how much the zoning could
131      be changed, particularly if a developer wanted to apply the overlay to an area currently zoned
132      for larger, half-acre lots. She was concerned about dramatic changes to the zoning without
133      proper analysis. Regarding the potential for home occupations and nuisances in the smaller,
134      denser attainable housing areas, Albrecht suggested the Council may need to review the
135      permitted uses and consider any necessary adjustments to the city's nuisance ordinances.
136      Overall, Albrecht was supportive of the attainable housing concept, but wanted to ensure the
137      implementation details were carefully considered to maintain quality of life and community
138      character, especially in areas where the overlay would allow for significant density increases.

140   3. Discussion of the Central School District including appointments made by the Interlocal
141      Board to the Utah County Redistricting Advisory Committee and Naming of the new
142      school district.
143      The Council had an extensive discussion about the process for naming the new school district.
144      Mayor Johnson expressed concern that the naming process often becomes an emotional and
145      contentious issue that can distract from more important priorities. Heather Newall emphasized
146      the need for a temporary name other than "Central," as she felt it would be problematic for
147      school board candidates to have to campaign under a placeholder name. Newall advocated for
148      involving the community through surveys and a naming committee led by the Alpine School
149      District. She believed this would help build ownership and avoid negative discussions about the
150      name during the election. Michelle Stallings argued against the city taking the lead on naming,
151      stating that it should be reserved for the elected school board members. She was concerned
152      about the city spending money on the process and potentially not being reimbursed. Stallings
153      felt that the new board should handle the final decision on naming. Councilor Condie suggested
154      sticking with "Central" and allowing the new school board to go through a more inclusive
155      process of gathering public input and selecting the final name. He felt this would provide an
156      important community engagement opportunity for the elected board members. Paige Albrecht
157      agreed that the new board should have the opportunity to name the district, but was open to the
158      Interlocal Board providing a temporary placeholder name to avoid the issue becoming a focus
159      during the election. Paul Hancock stated that if the Council was just going to use a temporary
160      placeholder name, then he thought they should stick with "Central." Hancock used an example
161      to illustrate how a placeholder name, even if not intended to be permanent, can become
162      entrenched and very difficult for the new school board to change later on, so he suggested they
163      simply use "Central" as the placeholder rather than trying to come up with something more
164      creative.
