STUDY SESSION

Bonanza Flat Conservation Easement and Adaptive Management Plan History
and Overview:
Wendy Fisher, Utah Open Lands (UOL) Executive Director, reviewed the history of
Bonanza Flat. When acquired, Bonanza Flat was not a pristine landscape, but a parcel
in need of restoration. In 2014, Guardsman’s Pass was paved and talks about
management and conservation of the land began. Members of UOL met with the
Brighton Community Council and assessed the impact and mitigation efforts that would
be needed to restore and manage the land moving forward.

After Bonanza was purchased in 2017, it went through an analysis and planning stage
and two 12-member stakeholder committees were appointed. One was a jurisdictional
stakeholder committee made up with representatives from Wasatch County, the Forest
Service and Wasatch Mountain State Park, and the other was a technical stakeholder
committee that included members from non-profits that had been involved, Snyderville
Basin, and the Recreation District. Each committee had a total of six meetings and
provided feedback to UOL.

Fisher reviewed restoration efforts, including trash piles being removed, parking
conditions being remedied, and motorized vehicles used in the jurisdictional wetlands.
In 2019, additional considerations were analyzed, including safety corridors, inholdings,
leases, trail reroute, and infrastructure. The following year, adoptions, conservation
easements, baseline documentation, and an adaptive management plan were all
considered. Implementation of trailhead and waste management, trail realignments and
sustainable design, parking enforcement, and restoration were all underway as well.
Bonanza was now in the implementation phase. There were two inholdings on the
property and UOL was able to raise funds to acquire both.

Council Member Toly inquired as to what the implementation phase meant if it was
ongoing or if there was another phase after implementation. Fisher replied that the
implementation phase was ongoing. She stated the adaptive management program
ranged from forest health to restoration, to understanding capacity and degrees of use
and that it would be ongoing since the human and wildlife population was not stagnant.

Fisher stated that Bonanza Flat was a $38 million purchase and the City put up a $25
million bond, which meant there was a $13 million gap to fill. That gap was paid for by
11 non-profits, three cities, three counties, and over 3,500 individual donations. She
stated Bonanza Flat today went from 1,340 acres to 1,550 acres, in part to encompass
Girl Scout cabins that were on land they didn’t own, but rather leasing from United Park
City Mines. Fisher stated that a resource inventory was executed the first year the
property was owned, which looked at the current and historic uses of the land. They
also hired Mindy Wheeler to complete an assessment of the invasive species as well as
a vegetative ecologist, who discovered three rare species that grow on Bonanza Flat.

Fisher presented the adaptive management plan and stated the conservation easement
was a document that set the intention for the property and would be used as the
principal guideline to drive the parameters of the adaptive management plan.

Fisher displayed a topographic map that illustrated Bonanza Flat and neighboring
areas, like Talisker. She further stated there was a fair amount of topographic
fragmentation on the property due to various hiking trails that already existed prior to the
purchase in 2017.

Discuss Empire Creek Education and Outreach Plan:
Clint McAffee, Public Utilities Manager, presented this item and stated about 50% of
Park City’s drinking water came from abandoned mine tunnels between the Ontario
Drain, Judge Tunnel and Spiro Tunnel. The Jordanelle Special Service District (JSSD)
operated the Ontario Tunnel and Park City operated the Spiro and Judge Tunnels. In
2009, PCMC received a letter from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
directing the City to apply for Utah Pollutant Discharge System (UPDS) permits. The
City applied for a permit and negotiated with Utah Division of Water Quality on a
compliance term. In 2013, the City stopped using Judge for drinking water and at that
point Judge Tunnel began discharging into Empire Creek year-round. Prior to 2013,
Judge Tunnel was primarily used as a drinking water source and only intermittently and
sporadically discharged into Empire Creek, usually during spring run-off, or if mine
maintenance was performed.

McAffee reviewed in 2013-2014, the City began construction of the Judge Tunnel
Pipeline where water would drain to the future 3Kings Water Treatment Plant (3KWTP).
As part of the pipeline project, the City underwent an environmental assessment, a
condition of accepting federal funds for the pipeline project. One area of impact that was
studied was the depletion of water in Empire Creek and Silver Creek. It was found that

the depletion of Silver Maple Wetlands was an area of concern. With the help of Fish
and Wildlife and Bureau of Land Management (BLM), a mitigation plan was created in
which Park City would add water to those wetlands once Judge Tunnel was diverted
back to 3KWTP.

McAffee stated in 2014, the City entered a stipulated compliance order that
memorialized the compliance periods and terms where the City would comply with the
new UPDS permits for the Judge and Spiro Tunnel. In that order, Judge was to be
treated by January 1, 2024. After January 1, 2024, Park City could no longer legally
discharge Judge into Empire Creek untreated. The solution identified was to pipe Judge
Tunnel to 3KWTP to treat 100% of Judge at that location. In 2018, that agreement was
amended for more favorable terms and a longer compliance period. From 2013 to mid-
2023, a ten-year time frame, the City was implementing the solution to comply with the
discharge permits for Judge and Spiro Tunnels. The City issued $142 million worth of
revenue bonds in two issuances, both of which had 20-year terms under a 2% interest
rate. In 2023, he expected to complete the project and Judge would be diverted from
Empire Creek to 3KWTP.

McAffee highlighted a few projects that were completed with the $142 million bonds: the
Judge Tunnel pipeline was completed in 2014; the Empire Tank was replaced in 2022;
and 3KWTP, the largest single component of the overall project, would be completed in
2023. Other upgrades over the last ten years included Quinn’s Water Treatment Plant;
Creekside Water Treatment Plant; transmission lines across the entire City; golf course
ponds were dredged; Spiro Tunnel was rehabilitated and all facilities and departments
at this location were relocated.

McAffee stated in 2021, he reengaged the company that did the environmental
assessment and re-affirmed the findings that there was no significant impact on Empire
or Silver Creek due to the de-watering of Empire Creek. Bowen Collins did an
assessment of the creek after having water flowing through it since 2013. Key
conclusions from the assessment were that increased flows benefited riparian
vegetation, with little if any impact on woody tree species, and it was assumed that
snowmelt and excess seasonal runoff flows would continue to sustain existing riparian
vegetation as they did prior to 2013. Furthermore, they did not see any fish and there
was little concern regarding dust. The Division of Water Quality agreed that there were
no new findings that PCMC would be able to implement that were different from the
ones already agreed upon.

McAffee addressed the concerns regarding less water in the creek and discussed the
mitigation plans for the wetlands. The environmental assessment completed in 2013
found that the depletion of water in Silver Maple Claims Wetlands was a concern and
that the City needed to add water. Two locations were identified as potential source
options, but the existing water source was the first choice. He wanted to pipe Judge to
3KWTP and add water to wetlands using a valve from the Paysomer Ditch, which

wouldn’t require any energy. It would be diverted further upstream than it already landed

drought restrictions imposed on the community.

McAffee stated surplus sales to Weber Basin were on a five-year rolling contract and
with the additional demand of 600 gallons a minute to simulate flow to the creek, the
demand would be above the City’s reliable source capacity. In 2026, Weber Basin sales
would total $5 million which was up for a one-year renewal within the year. If the
contract was not renewed, Weber Basin would not be able to sell water to Summit
Water, yet would still owe them water based on their contract. Without Park City’s
surplus, they would have to start developing a new water source for the Basin.

The White Pine Touring (WPT) center is open 7 days per week from November 19, 2022, until approximately
the end of March 2023 depending on weather and snow conditions.

WPT is celebrating its 50th Anniversary as a locally owned PC business and has successfully operated on

Traffic mitigation and business modifications initiated in the 2021-22 and implemented for the 22-23

Parking Mitigation
   Significantly reduce overflow parking hours from 2021 timeframe (7:00 a.m.-10:00 p.m.)
   Directly encourage employee carpool and transit use every day and prohibit employee parking in

   Parking management for event day with PCMC Special Event Application
   Proactive communications with season pass holders, teams, and programs to encourage carpooling,
   and transit

Winter Grooming and Operations
Should the overflow area be considered as proposed, the area needs to be groomed daily to maintain a
solid and safe surface throughout the season. If left unpacked or ungroomed, the area will not be feasible
for use. Ability to provide this overflow option is provided in conjunction with previous operational changes
with the intent to limit impacts on adjacent neighborhoods.

   Relocation of grooming equipment storage to reduce the impact on residential areas
   Modification of lesson areas moved to reduce the impact on residential areas
   Modification daily grooming hours and patterns to reduce the impact on residential areas

Races and Special Events
White Pine Touring Nordic Center has proudly hosted and sponsored Wasatch Citizens Series ski races for
more than three decades. It is one of the longest-running and most successful cross-country ski race series
in North America. This season, two races have been relocated away from the Touring Center. Some will say
the youth-focused, family-oriented, and locally managed events moving away from Park City are a loss.
These have been done to reduce and balance impacts on residential areas.

   Relocated December 17 TUNA fun relay to Mountain Dell
   Relocated January 28 Wasatch Citizens Series to the White Pine Farm
   Discontinued Local’s and Demo Day Special Event

Proposed Season Overflow Parking Schedule
We believe a simple and consistent use of the overflow parking area can balance neighborhood concerns
and still provide a quality customer experience for recreationists. Overflow will only be allowed, when
necessary, on the following schedule:

   Sunday, 10:00 a.m.-2:00 p.m.
   Monday, 10:00 a.m.-2:00 p.m.

   Thursday, 10:00 a.m.-6:00 p.m. (juniors program day)
   Friday, 10:00 a.m.-2:00 p.m.
   Saturday, 10:00 a.m.-2:00 p.m.

Adaptive Nordic Access
The Thaynes Canyon area provides on-snow access for the Adaptive Nordic ski community. This includes
sit-skiers and those who are visually impaired and participate in programs associated with the National
Ability Center, the United States Paralympic team, and Shriner’s Hospital.

   Three (3) parking spaces in the overflow area will be posted to accommodate Adaptive Nordic ski
   access programs daily – 9:00 a.m.-6:00 p.m.
