    2.3. Broadband Task Force Discussion with Todd Marriott – Nathan Cherpeski, City Manager
City Manager Cherpeski acknowledged several questions had been posed regarding the Broadband
Taskforce and indicated he had invited broadband expert Mr. Todd Marriott to the meeting to answer
questions and offer an overview of what the project would entail. Councilmember Jared Henderson
said he didn’t fully understand the recommendation from the taskforce and expressed he would like
to have a greater knowledge of the project. Councilmember Teddy Hodges stated he had met with
other jurisdictions and other elected officials who had brought the infrastructure into their
respective communities and asked them questions to gain a better understanding of this type of
infrastructure. Councilmember Hodges said based on his conversations, he felt he was leaning
toward one partner who could allow to take on additional customers.

Mr. Marriott suggested to put a competitive process in place, and acknowledged several providers
were willing and able to do business with Herriman. He offered an overview of what broadband could
offer a community and explained internet service was only a small byproduct of a much larger
infrastructure. He compared broadband infrastructure to the electricity for streetlights and relayed
its importance to existing and future residents. He felt broadband would be great for a wide variety
of people and industries, including first responders, health care workers, schools, economic
development, and city communications.

Councilmember Henderson questioned the need for the infrastructure. Mr. Marriott explained the
technology could do much more than download movies and media, as it enabled functions like
September 14, 2022 – City Council Minutes                                Page 3 of 15


connectivity, artificial intelligence, ability to monitor work, and censoring. He explained the future
of the infrastructure could vary depending on Council decisions and elaborated the scale for the
broadband ranged from doing nothing to having all utilities with data points throughout the entire
spectrum. He opined based on Herriman demographics; the Council should consider a larger
spectrum operation.

Mr. Marriott stated most cities near Herriman switched to Google, and compared broadband to a
road system, in which there would be a fiber ring around the City, with main arteries which had the
highest traffic, and then smaller fiber cables would splinter off to get to the actual homes. He
understood why some constituents were confused about the difference between the two models
and wanted to avoid certain situations which happened in other jurisdictions. Mr. Marriott impressed
upon the Council a good fiber infrastructure was critical to the community and noted Spanish Fork
had seen success. Mr. Marriott said Spanish Fork had had a high intake rate and the City had “paved
the road with gold” from the extra cash flow of that system. Mr. Marriott discussed models which
had been built and felt cities adopting fiber in the future would learn from previous errors.

Mr. Marriott discussed Google, which had already built networks in many cities throughout the state.
He said Google could come to Herriman on their own and lay down fiber, which would save money
in the short term. However, Mr. Marriott stated Google was a closed network and therefore would
not easily integrate to the preexisting City services. In comparison, Mr. Marriot stated Utopia was
owned and operated by different cities and noted their sister agency, Utah Infrastructure Agency
(UIA), was an agency he had personally set up in 2012 to help Utopia attract other cities to the system
and allowed Utopia to utilize any excess revenues of those cities to pay off debts. Mr. Marriott felt
the Utopia system was a fantastic system, and said a lot of cities had experienced success, although
acknowledged Utopia did require the City to put up the backstop for the funding, with one exception.
He further explained Utopia and UIA would own the network, and the City would have to cosign to
utilize the infrastructure. Mr. Marriott discussed while this was his model, he had never anticipated
asking cities to cosign and not have ownership in the model, and clarified it was not in line with his
original vision. He explained the lack of ownership meant cities would have to give up the network
and the net revenues, which would go back to Utopia.

Mr. Marriott discussed another option would be a public/private partnership in which a private
entity with a core concept for this type of project would come in and build the network and provide
the service, but in this case, the network would be owned by the City. Mr. Marriott felt ownership
was an essential part of the City’s considerations because the networks would be of great worth
once established. He explained there was a lot of private financing coming on board to finance these
types of builds.

Mr. Marriott said to could consider Google in the same way as Comcast, because there was very
little difference between the two aside from the different technologies used. Mr. Marriott said
Utopia had a fantastic network with good people, but the downside was they used the City’s
cosigning ability to own the network.

Mayor Palmer compared the public and private model to an interlocal cooperative agreement. Mr.
Marriott agreed an agreement was an option, and there could be some benefits. However, Mr.
Marriott thought the fourth option, for the City to do it themselves, was the best option. He invited

on them for their opinion. Councilmember Henderson continued to express his desire to understand

the City would own the networks, and so would have to make it available to everyone.

Mayor Palmer commented some of the Homeowner’s Associations (HOAs) in Herriman had
