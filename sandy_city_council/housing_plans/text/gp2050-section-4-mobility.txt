       Existing Conditions
       This section outlines the current status of Sandy City’s roadways, public
       transit, and active transportation network, giving a snapshot of their
       efficiency and reach. This chapter builds upon the recent plans completed
       for Sandy, including the City’s Transportation Master Plan and Active
       Transportation Plan. For recommendations and Key Strategies for
       enhancing mobility and connectivity across these systems, refer to page 9.



       Roadway Network
       As illustrated in Figure 4.1.1, Sandy has an extensive network of roads
       designed to facilitate travel across the city. This network is categorized based
       on each road’s capacity and role within the overall transportation network.
       The types of roads include interstates, arterial, collector, and local roads.
       For example, I-15 is a major interstate that runs through the city from
       north to south, providing essential connectivity statewide. Major arterial
       roads in Sandy, such as State Street (SR-89), 1300 East, and 700 East (SR-
       71), are designed to connect regionally and prioritize mobility. Roads such
       as 10600 South and sections of 9400 South and 11400 South are classified
       as minor arterials, which still prioritize mobility and inter-community
       connectivity but have lower speed limits and traffic volumes than major
       arterials. Collector roads – including Wasatch Boulevard, Sego Lily Drive,
       2300 East, Monroe Street, Sandy Parkway, and Dimple Dell Road – also
       play a critical role to collect traffic from local roads and direct it towards
       the larger arterial roads, balancing local access with broader mobility. Most
       residential areas in Sandy are local roads characterized by low-speed limits
       and traffic volumes, and they typically provide the most direct local access,
       such as from one location to another within a neighborhood.

       EXISTING TRAFFIC VOLUMES & CONGESTION

       Figure 4.1.2 illustrates the existing Level of Service (“LOS”) from
       the 2021 Sandy Transportation Master Plan. This system helps in
       understanding how well traffic is moving on a road or through a
       network. It indicates areas where improvements might be necessary
       to help reduce congestion and enhance overall traffic flow. Roadways
       are given a letter grade, “A” through “F”, with A-C allowing to
       accommodate additional growth and development. "D" has been
       identified by the Public Works Department as the optimal grade.
       Typically, as a LOS grade worsens, traffic congestion increases. LOS's
       currently at an "E" or "F", or projected to be at an "E" or "F", should
       be considered and studied for projects that facilitate better flow and
       traffic distribution. As shown in Figure 4.1.2, most roadways in Sandy
       fall into the category of "D" or higher.

       Active Transportation Network
       As shown in Figure 4.1.5, Sandy City supports active transportation
       through an extensive network that includes bike routes, bike lanes, multi-
       use pathways, sidewalks, and trails. This network includes key regional
       facilities, such as the Jordan River Parkway, Bonneville Shoreline Trail, and
       the Porter Rockwell Trail, which offer substantial pedestrian and bicyclist
       connectivity and integrate residential neighborhoods with natural areas like
       Dimple Dell Regional Park in a way that is fairly unique within Salt Lake
       County. This infrastructure is intentionally developed to promote bicycling,
       walking, and other modes of human-powered transport, aiming to enhance
       connectivity, safety, and accessibility citywide. Sandy's completed Dry
       Creek Trail crosses the entire city from east to west without the need for
       users to utilize an at-grade crossing, with the exception of State St. 11400
       S from it's terminus in east Sandy to the round-about in Daybreak has
       regional connectivity with continuous bike lanes along the entire stretch.
       At the roundabout in Daybreak, the bike lanes transition into a separated
       trail as they continue westward by crossing Mountain View Corridor.

       Additionally, Sandy’s Active Transportation Plan, developed collaboratively
       with Draper City, helps ensure future active transportation development
       and planning are integrated regionally, offering more comprehensive and
       consistent non-motorized options in and between the two cities.

       Challenges exist for various user groups that can impact the effectiveness of
       the active transportation facilities they desire to use. Automobile-dominant
       arterial roadways such as State Street, 11400 South, and 9000 South
       pose significant challenges to overall user experience for those walking
       and bicycling. I-15 and surrounding roads limit non-automobile crossing
       opportunities to designated locations spaced further apart, thus channelizing
       the city’s active transportation network at these locations. These barriers can
       hinder functionality of the network and affect public perception and usage,
       as evidenced by strong community feedback throughout this planning
       process and the City’s transportation plans, calling for pronounced and
       improved access to active transportation connections.

       Active Transportation Network
       Future active transportation networks are identified in Sandy’s 2020 Active
       Transportation Plan. The proposed projects are excerpted from that plan,
       as shown in Figure 4.1.10.

       In addition to these projects, Sandy City has an opportunity to construct
       the planned, grade-separated crossing over I-15 for bicycles and pedestrians
       between South Jordan FrontRunner Station and The Shops at South Town.
       In the 2024 legislative session HB 488, Sandy City was awarded up to $2
       million per year over the next 20 years in funding through County of the
       First Class Infrastructure Bank Fund (CFCIBF) repayments to begin the
       process of designing and building the bridge. Another potential location
       for a grade-separated crossing is the intersection of Sego Lily Drive and
       State Street, where the Civic Center Station area could link up with future
       redevelopment of The Shops at South Town. The State Street crossing is
       be a barrier to walkability and bikeability for people traveling between the
       TRAX station and Sandy’s downtown areas, and a bridge or tunnel would
       better facilitate that movement than an at-grade intersection crosswalk.

       The Cairns, Sandy’s downtown area that contains many important
       regional destinations, is a good location intended to shift focus towards the
       pedestrian realm in key sections. This includes using public right-of-way
       to build new trails, separated bicycle facilities, and on-street parking to
       support local businesses and create a more urban public space.
