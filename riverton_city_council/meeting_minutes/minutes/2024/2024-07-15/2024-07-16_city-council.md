       Rules of Order and Procedure Discussion

Kevin Hicks, City Manager, stated that individual meetings were held with each Councilmember
over the past week to review edits. He said this work session discussion was meant to provide
an opportunity for additional dialogue. He stated that the plan was to bring the document before
the Council at the next meeting in August for approval.

Councilmember Buroker proposed modifying the wording from "public comments for 15
minutes" to "at least 15 minutes" to provide flexibility, or alternatively, removing the time limit
entirely.

Ryan Carter, City Attorney, stated that the Riverton City Council follows Robert's Rules of Order,
which includes a motion to suspend the rules under special circumstances, thereby allowing for
Page | 2 Minutes – City Council Meeting
July 16, 2024




more than 15 minutes of public comments. He noted that this existing procedure already
provides the needed flexibility. However, he also expressed openness to making the change if
the Council prefers.

Mayor Staggs responded by noting that the discretion to extend the comment period lies with
the chair. He explained that typically, if a large crowd is anticipated, comment cards are made
available. This helps manage the number of speakers by requiring them to be called upon,
which often results in fewer comments. He also mentioned that if a Councilmember senses
significant interest from the public, they can suggest having a few spokespersons speak on
behalf of larger groups, allowing more time for those speakers while preventing repetitive
comments. This approach provides flexibility within the established rules.

Councilmember McCay expressed concern that a 15-minute limit for public comments might be
too short, especially for significant or contentious issues. She noted that with a three-minute
limit per speaker, only five residents could speak within that time frame, which may not be
sufficient for important topics. She cited the example of the recycling issue, suggesting that had
residents chosen to speak at the meeting instead of sending emails, the 15-minute limit would
have been inadequate. She supported increasing the public comment period to 30 minutes, with
the understanding that it could be reduced if necessary, to ensure residents feel heard.

Councilmembers discussed and agreed to remove the 15 minute time limit from the Rules.

Councilmember McCay raised a point about retaining the last item in the Rules which addresses
the involvement of the city manager or department heads in local elections. She suggested that
this section could be condensed but emphasized the importance of keeping a portion of it,
especially the sentence stating that Councilmembers should not request these officials to
participate in campaigns.

Mr. Carter noted that while City employees are free to engage in political activities during their
personal time, without using City resources, the City does not have the authority to prohibit such
involvement entirely. He acknowledged that while it might be permissible for the Council to
express a commitment to not expecting employees to participate in their campaigns, enforcing a
prohibition against employees' political involvement outside of work could expose the City to
legal risks.

Councilmembers Haymond and Buroker did not want to retain the last paragraph based on the
feedback from the City Attorney.

         Cargo Container Discussion

Mayor Staggs described a previous situation where complaints from residents led to an
ordinance restricting the placement of cargo containers. However, some residents now view
cargo containers as a cost-effective storage solution, especially if placed behind fences in their
backyards. He suggested that a solution might involve revising the ordinance to allow cargo
containers if certain conditions or materials are used to improve their appearance.

Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, explained that while the ordinance prohibits
the use of cargo containers as standalone structures, they can be used if they are integrated
into accessory buildings that meet specific design standards. The ordinance currently requires
accessory structures to match the materials used on the main dwelling, which means cargo
containers, with their typical corrugated metal exteriors, are not allowed as external materials.
Page | 3 Minutes – City Council Meeting
July 16, 2024




However, if a cargo container is used as the interior framework of an accessory building and
covered with compliant materials, it can be treated as a standard accessory structure, subject to
the same regulations as other buildings of similar size.

Mr. Lethbridge highlighted that this approach has been effective in addressing complaints and
providing a cost-effective storage solution for residents. The City’s webpage provides
information on how to comply with these regulations. The state recently increased the threshold
for requiring a building permit from 200 to 350 square feet, which has simplified the process for
using cargo containers as accessory structures by eliminating the need for a permanent
foundation.

Dana Long, resident, provided feedback on the use of cargo containers, particularly in
agricultural settings. She expressed concerns about the practicality and cost-effectiveness of
applying siding to cargo containers, noting that:

         1.        Material Durability: The material used for siding is of a lower grade and is
                   susceptible to damage from livestock, such as horses, which can easily eat
                   through or scratch it.
         2.        Cost of Siding: The cost of applying siding, estimated at around $4,000, is not
                   considered cost-effective, especially for those with larger properties.
         3.        Water Flow and Versatility: The design of the boundary on the bottom of the
                   container can obstruct water flow, which is problematic for properties that use
                   flood irrigation. She highlighted that cargo containers' current design allows for
                   water to flow underneath, which helps with irrigation and prevents water damage.
         4.        Safety and Environmental Concerns: Cargo containers provide a safe and fire-
                   resistant option for storing materials and protecting livestock. The containment of
                   fire within a cargo container prevents it from spreading to other structures and
                   potentially harming animals.

Wayne Withers, resident, shared his experience with cargo containers on his two-acre lot. He
highlighted the following points:

         1.        Cost of Removal: He faced significant expenses, totaling $9,000 for a trailer to
                   move the contents of the container and an additional $2,000 to have the
                   container hauled away. This cost was incurred after receiving a 30-day notice to
                   remove the container.
         2.        Inconsistent Enforcement: He noted that a neighbor with a similar container on
                   the same street had not been contacted about their container, despite its
                   presence being visible from the street. He felt that this inconsistency in
                   enforcement was unfair.
         3.        Visibility and Complaints: His container had been positioned out of view behind
                   trees and had not generated complaints. He questioned the fairness of the
                   regulations, especially since his container had been in place for years without
                   issues.

Councilmember McCay expressed growing concern about the regulation of cargo containers,
particularly in relation to larger lots and properties with animal rights. She acknowledged that
containers should not be placed in front of houses but could be suitable for larger lots. She
suggested that flexibility in the policy could address concerns about aesthetics and practicality,
such as allowing containers if they are properly screened or surrounded by other materials. She
also anticipated challenges for code enforcement if the regulations were too strict, particularly
Page | 4 Minutes – City Council Meeting
July 16, 2024




given the number of containers already in use and the potential burden on residents to comply
with stringent aesthetic requirements. She recommended exploring policies used by other cities
and potentially incorporating requirements that could accommodate the practical benefits of
containers while addressing aesthetic concerns. This might include options for screening or
surrounding containers in a way that aligns with the look of the property.

Mr. Lethbridge agreed to look into how other cities handle similar situations and bring this
information back to the Council in August.

Discussion/Action Items

         Resolution No. 24-65 – Performance Bond release for Parkside Phase I (5342)

Cary Necaise, Public Works Director, said this bond was specifically for public improvements,
including roadways and water lines, rather than private areas. He expressed a desire to
ensure that all related improvements are addressed comprehensively, but questioned whether
the bond, which is designated for public improvements, can be withheld due to issues related
to private areas.

Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, clarified that the bond in question is very
specific, detailing exact quantities and locations of public improvements such as asphalt and
pipes. He emphasized the need for caution in handling the bond and noted that the release of
the bond is based on verification from inspection staff who ensure that the items listed in the
bond have been completed or meet the required standards before transitioning into warranty.
However, inspections are limited to the items covered by the bond and do not extend to other
areas or issues outside the scope of the bond.

Mr. Carter explained that public improvement bonds are intended to cover City infrastructure
that will be maintained by the City once accepted. These typically include items like curbs,
gutters, sidewalks, roadways, and utility lines. Private developments, which may include
infrastructure not dedicated to the City such as perimeter fences or private roadways, usually
do not require bonds for roads that remain under HOA control. He suggested that while the
bond agreement may not address certain issues, such as sidewalk cracks, a thorough review
of site conditions and photographs could be conducted.

Councilmember McCay MOVED that the City Council bring Resolution No. 24-65 –
Performance Bond release for Parkside Phase I (5342) back to City Council on August
6. Councilmember Buroker SECONDED the motion. Mayor Staggs called for discussion on
the motion. Mayor Staggs called for a roll-call vote. The vote was as follows: Buroker-yes,
Haymond-yes, McCay-yes, and Pierucci-yes. The motion passed unanimously.

         Ordinance No. 24-13 – School Code Text, Amending Section 18-195-060(3),
         regulating the requirements for private or quasi-public schools, Amendments
         proposed by Riverton City

Mr. Lethbridge discussed a text amendment concerning private or quasi-public schools outside
of the school district's jurisdiction. The current code requires a minimum property size of three
acres for such applications, a rule established years ago due to traffic and circulation challenges
with a previous large-scale school project. The proposed amendment seeks to reduce this
minimum to one acre. This proposal emerged from the Planning Commission's detailed
examination and acknowledgment of property interest within the City.

The amendment specifies that while one acre is the new minimum, more acreage may be
required for larger facilities. It maintains that private or quasi-public schools would need a
conditional use permit in residential zones and would only be allowed on collector or arterial
roadways as defined by the City's transportation master plan. This measure aims to prevent
these schools from impacting residential neighborhoods by ensuring they are situated on roads
designed to handle additional traffic.

Further, the amendment allows the City to impose additional restrictions based on traffic, noise,
parking, emergency response, stormwater, and the school's impact on the surrounding
neighborhood. The Planning Commission's thorough review led to these stipulations, ensuring
the City's infrastructure and residents' needs are adequately protected while providing
opportunities for private and quasi-public schools. The Planning Commission recommended

July 16, 2024




approval of these amendments.

         Ordinance No. 24-14 – Amending Title 11, Chapters 5 of the Riverton City
         Municipal Code Relating to Regulations Applicable to Riverton City Parks

Mr. Carter discussed an ordinance aimed at prohibiting the public from setting off fireworks in
City parks. Recently, the City experienced property damage in some parks due to improper
fireworks management. Historically, the City permitted fireworks in parks to accommodate
neighborhoods near urban forests, which are at higher risk for wildfires. This practice was
initially accepted as a means to manage the discharge of fireworks safely within City limits.

Councilmember Pierucci expressed support for the ordinance but emphasized the importance
of coupling its passage with an active social media and education campaign. He highlighted
the importance of preserving community investments in parks and their amenities, noting that
damage from fireworks leads to unsustainable costs for repairs.

Councilmember Buroker voiced discomfort with the proposed change, questioning if residents
who use parks for fireworks do so to avoid the risks associated with densely populated areas,
potentially making parks a safer option. She expressed concern about pushing residents back
into crowded neighborhoods.

Councilmember Pierucci shared his internal conflict regarding the issue, emphasizing his love
for fireworks but highlighting concerns about public safety. He believed that individuals are
more responsible and cautious when setting off fireworks near their own property or their
neighbors' homes compared to public spaces. He suggested that this personal accountability
could lead to safer practices and reduce damage to public parks, thereby supporting the
change despite his fondness for fireworks.

Councilmember Pierucci MOVED that the City Council adopt Ordinance No. 24-14 –
Amending Title 11, Chapters 5 of the Riverton City Municipal Code Relating to
Regulations Applicable to Riverton City Parks and striking the word “rockets” in
section 8 and making it effective on July 31, 2024. Councilmember McCay SECONDED
the motion. Mayor Staggs called for discussion on the motion. Mayor Staggs called for a roll-
call vote. The vote was as follows: Buroker-no, Haymond-yes, McCay-yes, and Pierucci-yes.
The motion passed with a 3 to 1 vote.

         Ordinance No. 24-15 – Amending Riverton City Code Title 8, Adding Chapter 45
         (Prohibited Camping)

Mr. Carter noted that the ordinance had passed through rigorous legal scrutiny, giving
Page | 10 Minutes – City Council Meeting
July 16, 2024




confidence in its language and its ability to withstand future challenges. He explained that the
ordinance prohibits overnight camping on public property, such as parks, while allowing
camping on private property. This measure aims to prevent homeless people from
congregating in public spaces, addressing part of the broader homeless issue. Riverton City
has already encountered instances of homeless individuals using public restrooms in parks,
and the ordinance would provide law enforcement with the necessary legal foundation to
address these situations effectively.

Councilmember Pierucci expressed appreciation for the ordinance draft, acknowledging the
efforts to address the issue of camping on public property in a humane manner. He
emphasized that public spaces should be open, safe, clean, and accessible for their intended
uses. He noted that, in his district, there have been recent challenges with encampments
along the Creek Trail, which have affected neighbors.

Councilmember McCay raised a concern about the provision of the ordinance, which states
that the City shall inform a local agency providing social services to homeless individuals
about the campsite location. She expressed doubt about whether these agencies would have
the resources to respond to such notifications effectively. She suggested that instead of
relying on social service agencies to come out to the campsite, it might be more practical to
provide the individuals with resources directly or offer transportation to a homeless shelter.

Chief Shane Taylor, Riverton Police Department, acknowledged the increase in homeless
populations but noted that the City has not been overwhelmed by the issue. He expressed
support for the ordinance, emphasizing that it provides necessary enforcement tools. He also
highlighted that, in the worst-case scenario, the class B misdemeanor provision could be used
to address violations, potentially leading to further support and resources for affected
individuals.

Councilmember Pierucci MOVED that the City Council adopt Ordinance No. 24-15 –
Amending Riverton City Code Title 8, Adding Chapter 45 (Prohibited Camping).
Councilmember Buroker SECONDED the motion. Mayor Staggs called for discussion on the
motion. Mayor Staggs called for a roll-call vote. The vote was as follows: Buroker-yes,
Haymond-yes, McCay-yes, and Pierucci-yes. The motion passed unanimously.
