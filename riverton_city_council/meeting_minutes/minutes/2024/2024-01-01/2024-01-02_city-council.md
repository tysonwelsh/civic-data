         Resolution No. 24-01 – Rejecting the Findings and Conclusions of Utah Legislative
         Auditor General’s Report No. 2023-16: “A Performance Audit of Utah Housing
         Policy.”

Mayor Staggs discussed the resolution rejecting the findings of the Utah Legislative Auditor
General concerning housing policy. The report had recommendations that drew dissatisfaction
from the League of Cities and Towns, as well as various municipalities. The report suggested a
top-down approach akin to California, advocating for the legislature to dictate housing targets to
municipalities. He found this recommendation offensive, asserting that communities' planning

decisions should be based on local knowledge and expertise rather than centralized authority.
He highlighted the importance of land-use authority being delegated to cities and expressed
concerns about recent legislation encroaching on this authority.

Mayor Staggs said that Councilmember McCay and Councilmember Pierucci had expressed
their concerns to both the Utah League of Cities and Towns and to the state.

Councilmember Pierucci criticized the legislative auditor's report, characterizing it as
"completely and totally ridiculous." He highlighted errors in the report and pointed out the
contradiction within the report itself, noting that it couldn't find instances of cities ignoring state
laws and slowing down processes. Despite this, the report recommended transferring more
authority from cities to unelected state-level bureaucrats. He questioned the logic behind this
recommendation, arguing that it seemed like a "solution in search of a problem." He
emphasized that the housing issues faced by the state were not unique and echoed challenges
experienced nationwide, such as housing affordability, workforce, and supply chain problems.
He expressed support for the resolution, asserting the need for the City to take a firm stand
against blaming municipalities for challenges faced by every state in the country.

Councilmember Pierucci MOVED that the City Council approve Resolution No. 24-01 –
Rejecting the Findings and Conclusions of Utah Legislative Auditor General’s Report No.
2023-16: “A Performance Audit of Utah Housing Policy.”               Councilmember McCay
SECONDED the motion. Mayor Staggs called for discussion on the motion. Mayor Staggs
called for a roll-call vote. The vote was as follows: Buroker-yes, Haymond-yes, McCay-yes,
McDougal-yes, and Pierucci-yes. The motion passed unanimously.

         Light Pollution Discussion

Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, provided a brief overview of the current
lighting ordinance and highlighted key aspects. He discussed the flexibility in city ordinances,
ranging from broadly worded to highly specific. He emphasized the importance of addressing
language ambiguities and tightening up code sections, particularly in terms of light source
visibility beyond property lines.

Mr. Lethbridge outlined the existing ordinance, with a focus on shielding and directing light
downward to prevent visibility from beyond the property line. Challenges in interpreting certain
language, such as projecting "above or beyond the property line," were acknowledged. He
touched on surrounding cities' approaches to lighting ordinances, including addressing light
source shielding, limitations on intensity, specifying fixture types, and introducing time and date
restrictions. He noted that the City's commercial growth might necessitate a more sophisticated
lighting code. While the purpose of the meeting was to gather input, he highlighted the
opportune time for code expansion to meet community needs and commercial requirements.

Councilmember McDougal said he had received an email from a resident in his district,
highlighting a direct conflict related to lighting. He proposed tightening the City code by
implementing a specific time period for commercial lighting adjacent to residential areas. He
suggested a 10 o’clock cutoff for all lighting in such areas to prevent commercial lighting from
spilling over into residential spaces. He extended the discussion to include the growing
residential component, where residents are adding lights around their homes, leading to
concerns about blinking lights and light intrusion into residential areas.

Councilmember Pierucci highlighted the need to strike a balance between the reasonable

expectations of residents and the success of businesses. He suggested the importance of
finding a balance and indicated openness to variable schedules based on the dynamics of
businesses, proposing potential adjustments for weekdays and weekends and expressed
uncertainty about the appropriateness of a fixed time like 10 o'clock. Additionally, he raised
concerns about restricting residential lighting for security reasons and expressed a desire to
understand this aspect better.

Mr. Lethbridge clarified that, currently, the City doesn't have specific restrictions or regulations
regarding lighting on residential homes, except for certain nuisance language addressing
extreme cases like disruptive spotlights. He noted that there are no existing ordinances
regulating the duration or type of residential lighting, such as Christmas lights. He said that the
focus had been primarily on commercial lighting in the proposed ordinance. However, he was
willing to explore the legalities and considerations of extending regulations to residential lighting.
He mentioned the importance of looking into examples from other states to gather more
information on this matter.

Councilmember McCay agreed with the need for changes to the lighting code but advocated for
caution to avoid overly restrictive measures. She voiced concern about businesses investing in
expensive lighting without being informed or educated about potential regulations. She
proposed taking immediate action to address businesses causing disturbances, suggesting that
enforcement could begin before the new regulations are officially implemented.

Councilmember McDougal said his primary concern was the need for an ordinance to establish
clear expectations for current and future commercial establishments. He stressed the obligation
to protect residential rights, pointing out that attempts to work with commercial business owners
had not always yielded positive results. He noted that the issue wasn't solely about the size of
lights but also their angle, duration, and the associated activities occurring under the lights,
especially when adjacent to residential properties. He underlined a preference for prioritizing
residential rights over commercial rights, considering the residents were there first.
