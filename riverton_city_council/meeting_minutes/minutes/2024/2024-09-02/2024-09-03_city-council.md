Presentations

         Water System Presentation

Shazelle Terry, Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District, explained that PFAs (Per- and
Polyfluoroalkyl Substances) are synthetic chemicals commonly found in various products like
water-resistant clothing, firefighting foams, and nonstick cookware. These chemicals are known
as "forever chemicals" because they do not break down easily and accumulate in air, water,
soil, humans, and animals. Concerns about PFAs stem from their potential public health effects,
which include issues like changes in liver function, cholesterol levels, fertility problems,
suppressed immune systems, and increased risk of cancer. She said that in Utah, PFAs
sampling has been ongoing since 2013 as part of an EPA rule, with no detectable levels found
in samples from Riverton or Jordan Valley.

City Manager Report

Kevin Hicks, City Manager, provided an update on several ongoing projects and initiatives. First,
he mentioned collaborating with Communications on a "Shop Local" campaign to be launched in
the fall, aimed at encouraging residents to support local businesses during the upcoming
Interchange project. Plans include working with businesses near the interchange and potentially
hosting a town hall meeting, with the hope of involving UDOT to help educate the public and
businesses on navigating the area during construction.

Mr. Hicks discussed several grant opportunities the City is pursuing, including a potential grant
of $76,000 for projects like EV stations or LED light conversions. The City Engineer is working
with the Wasatch Front Regional Council to secure additional funding for EV stations, and the
deadline for another grant opportunity has been extended until September 11, for which the City
is preparing a submission. He emphasized the importance of including matching funds in the
budget when applying for grants. This ensures that when the City requests funding, it can
demonstrate that matching funds are already secured, improving the chances of receiving
grants. As a result, future budget requests will likely include provisions for such matching funds,
even if the work itself is not yet being proposed.

Lastly, Mr. Hicks mentioned an upcoming meeting with representatives from Bluffdale and
Herriman to discuss the nearly completed 13800 South roadway project..

         Ordinance 24-17 – Amending Riverton ty Code, Section 18.190.050(5), Home
         Occupations to Clarify the Appeals Process, Amendments Proposed by Riverton
         City

Mr. Lethbridge discussed the ordinance amendment related to the appeal authority for home
occupations, which are home-based businesses. The current ordinance contains contradictory
language regarding appeal processes. It designates the Planning Commission as the land use
authority for conditional uses but also incorrectly states that the City Council should act as the
appeal authority. In contrast, Section 6 specifies that appeals should go to the Board of
Adjustment, which is the correct body for reviewing procedural and legal issues. The proposed
amendment seeks to resolve this contradiction by removing the City Council's role as the appeal
authority and aligning the appeals process with the Board of Adjustment's responsibilities. The
Planning Commission has recommended approval of the amendment.

Councilmember Pierucci inquired about the history of the discrepancy regarding the appeal
authority for home occupations. In response, Ryan Carter, City Attorney, clarified that the
discrepancy arose because, at one point, the City Council was the appeal authority for certain

matters, including home occupations. However, a decision was made to consolidate appeal
authority within the Board of Adjustment, which is an expert land use attorney appointed to
handle appeals and ensure that decisions adhere to proper procedures and legal standards.

This change aimed to address issues with a previous system where citizen-appointed boards
were handling appeals, which was deemed inappropriate. The shift to a professionally managed
Board of Adjustment occurred approximately seven to eight years ago.

The Board of Adjustment handles land use appeals for administrative decisions made by the
Planning Commission, such as conditional use permits and variances. It does not address
legislative decisions, like zoning changes. The board reviews appeals based on procedural and
legal grounds rather than personal or economic reasons, focusing on ensuring fair application of
the code.

         Ordinance 24-18 – Code Text Amendment, Amending Section 18.135.200,
         Regulating Permits for Temporary Commercial Uses, Amendments Proposed by
         Riverton City

Mr. Lethbridge discussed proposed amendments to the ordinance regulating temporary
commercial uses, such as Christmas tree lots and fireworks stands. The existing code lacks
clarity regarding the duration and frequency of these uses. Specifically, it does not define
whether the 60-day limit applies within a year or a six-month period or if it can be repeated.

To address this, the proposed changes include:

        Duration Limitation: Temporary commercial uses would be limited to a maximum of 60
         days within a 12-month period. Two approvals may be issued per property per year.
        Fireworks Stands: These stands may receive one approval allowing sales on July 4 and
         July 24, within the 60-day annual limit.
        Property Requirements: Temporary uses must occur on commercially zoned property
         developed under an approved site plan. The property must comply with applicable
         ordinances and standards.

Mr. Lethbridge said that staff recommended against extending the duration to six months, citing
that most temporary food vendors do not operate for such an extended period. Staff suggested
that the existing permit holder should be considered legal non-conforming and allowed to
continue under their original permit terms. The Planning Commission's recommendation
included incorporating the six-month provision to accommodate an existing food vendor, but
staff's recommendation was to keep the 60-day limit for all new permits.

Councilmember Pierucci expressed a preference for maintaining the existing code. He felt that
allowing businesses to operate in available parking lots with minimal regulation aligns better
with free market principles. He voiced concern that the new regulations might overly interfere
with business operations and limit market opportunities unnecessarily.

Councilmember Haymond expressed a preference for reducing government regulation rather
than increasing it. He emphasized the benefits of a more open business environment and
expressed frustration with the extensive regulatory requirements faced by businesses,
advocating for Riverton to adopt a more business-friendly stance.

Councilmember McDougal supported the proposed changes, arguing that the modification
strikes a balance between allowing short-term, seasonal businesses and protecting established
local businesses. He highlighted concerns that extending temporary permits to six months could
disadvantage long-term, brick-and-mortar businesses by drawing customers away and not
contributing to property taxes. He noted that temporary businesses, such as fireworks stands or
food trucks, could use up parking space intended for primary businesses, further impacting
established businesses. He supported the ordinance as a way to limit temporary uses while
ensuring that the City's long-term businesses remain viable and contribute financially to the
community.

Councilmember Pierucci MOVED that the City Council DENY Ordinance 24-18 – Code Text
Amendment, Amending Section 18.135.200, Regulating Permits for Temporary
Commercial Uses. Councilmember McCay SECONDED the motion. Mayor Staggs called for
discussion on the motion. Mayor Staggs called for a roll-call vote. The vote was as follows:
Buroker-no, Haymond-yes, McCay-yes, McDougal-no and Pierucci-yes. The motion passed
with a 3 to 2 vote.

         Resolution No. 24-88 – Fee Schedule Amendment (Animal Intake Charge)

Mr. Geer said that intake fees for animal services from January to August of 2024 were
presented as part of the agenda, showing a total of 26 intake fees and one second intake fee,
meaning one animal was flagged as being taken in more than once. This brought the total
number of intake fees to 27 for that period.

Councilmembers discussed simplifying the intake fee structure for animal services, particularly
for animals that are taken into the shelter. The goal was to streamline the fees, moving away
from a tiered system to a single intake fee of $100. This fee is higher than the base cost of $75,
which only covers the initial costs without any overhead, such as the animal control officer's
time.

Some Councilmembers expressed concerns that jumping from $65 (current initial intake fee) to
$100 might be too steep for residents, especially since boarding fees also accumulate quickly
($10 per day). A suggestion was made to possibly set the intake fee at $75 or $80, to keep it
closer to the actual cost while still simplifying the structure.
