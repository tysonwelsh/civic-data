5.     PUBLIC HEARING. CONSIDERATION. AND RECOMMENDATION on Proposed
       Text Amendments to the Bluffdale City Land Use Ordinances Regarding Accessory
       Dwelling Units.Bluffdale City Code("BCC)Chanter 11.340 and Associated Definitions
       in the BCC 11.20. City of Bluffdale. Applicant. (Application 2022-49)-Staff Presenter,
       Grant Crowell.


Chair Cragun reported that the public hearing was still open for the above agenda item. She noted
that at the previous meeting the discussion ended after Section 11.340.040. City Planner/Economic
Development Director, Grant Crowell, confirmed that he had that same information and planned to
resume the discussion with Section 11.340.050: Detached Accessory Dwelling Unit ("D-ADU")
Development Standards. He noted, however,that the previous discussion included several questions
that remained unanswered. Specifically, there were questions regarding the rental of Accessory
Dwelling Units("ADU")and the consensus that it would not be necessary to rent an ADU.

The definition section included a lot of redundancy about occupation. There was also discussion
about an exception for occupancy during ownership changes. The Planning Commission could revisit
those issues if desired. There were also policy questions he planned to research further. Mr. Crowell
was asked to look at rural areas in Utah to get additional insight. His research was ultimately
inconclusive because there are so many different options being implemented by the cities he looked
at. There was no real consistency among the cities he researched with regard to permissible minimum
lot sizes. There were opinions that the minimum lot size should be at least one acre; however, the
draft specified 10,000 square feet. In Tooele,the minimum lot size requirement for a D-ADU is 8,500
square feet.   Some areas have robust allowances and others are more restrictive.         He found
communities that did not address internal ADUs though it is required by State Code.

Earlier in the day, Mr. Crowell received legislative training at a meeting ofthe Utah League ofCities
and Towns("ULCT"). The first issue addressed was to identify the problem cities are trying to solve.
That issue provided a good fi-ame ofreference for discussion. Chair Cragun asked that the discussion
resume where the Planning Commission left off before returning to the previous pending questions
and issues.


Mr. Crowell explained that there are general standards, standards for internal ADUs, and standards
for D-ADUs, which are the focus of this section. D-ADUs are separate from the primary residence,
and only one D-ADU is allowed per lot. The issue ofsetbacks was still an item of discussion. In the
R-1-43 one-acre zone, the side and rear setbacks for an accessory building are 10 feet. Small
Accessory Buildings, which have their own definition, can have setbacks of just two feet. That
setback allowance would not apply to D-ADUs. They would still have to have at least a 10-foot
setback on the side and rear. D-ADUs also need to be on the rear or side ofthe lot, as proposed, and
cannot be in the front. An ADU cannot exceed 50% ofthe size ofthe primary dwelling, not including
the garage but must be 400 square feet or larger.


Commissioner Swanson noted that there are home designs that could feasibly justify lowering the
minimum size to 250 square feet. Commissioners Luker and Kraupp opined that the minimum should
not be less than 400 square feet. Chair Cragun agreed. Mr. Crowell noted that the construction codes
would have to be followed.


Chair Cragun explained that she lives on a one-quarter-acre lot. Her home is multi-story and has a
smaller footprint than a rambler-style home. As a result, she could easily meet all ofthe setbacks and
other requirements for constructing a D-ADU on her property. She could locate a D-ADU in her
backyard but her neighbors, who have ramblers, could not. Her comments related to the size of the
lots that would be allowed for a D-ADU. She felt that a one-quarter acre lot would be fine because
other factors would dictate the viability ofplacing a D-ADU there. She felt that there are factors that
would naturally impact the feasibility of a D-ADU so the size of the lot could be as small as one-
quarter acre. Mr. Crowell and Commissioner Luker agreed.

Mr. Crowell reported that the lot is 10,000 square feet in size and he could develop a D-ADU if he
removed a massive tree to make room for the structure. Consequently, there would be lots in the R-
1-10 zone where people could feasibly place a D-ADU based on the size and configuration of the
primary residence on the property.


Chair Cragun stated that the challenges are the housing shortage, affordable housing, and property
rights involving things that are within reason and in compliance with City Code. Commissioner
Swanson's recollection of the intent behind limiting the size to one-acre lots in the R-1-43 zone was
to follow a phasing approach to implementing D-ADUs. Chair Cragun agreed but over time she has
shifted away fi-om phasing to basing the size on the viability ofthe lot configuration. Issues pertaining
to lot size were discussed. Commissioner Swanson did not support restricting lot size because other
requirements must be met that would determine whether a D-ADU would be viable such as parking,
setbacks, and the minimum size ofthe building. Commissioner Kraupp commented that there was a
risk with that approach but acknowledged that many factors would determine whether a D-ADU
would be allowed. Commissioner Luker added that Homeowners Associations("HOA")would also
be a factor in the construction ofD-ADUs.


Chair Cragun sought to confirm that the consensus ofthe Planning Commissioners in attendance was
that the one-acre phasing approach was no longer the preference. Commissioner Kraupp stated that
the proposed minimum lot size is 10,000 square feet or one-quarter acre. He was amenable to
abandoning the idea ofhaving the minimum lot size be set at one acre. He was willing to go to 6,000
square feet given the factors discussed regarding the barriers that would have an impact on the
viability ofa D-ADU. Chair Cragun agreed with allowing more flexibility in lot size.

Mr. Crowell reported that the Code for Internal Accessory Dwelling Units ("I-ADU") has always
stipulated that the property attributes would control regardless of whether the lADU would fit. The
availability of on-site parking would be the primary controlling factor for an lADU. Commissioner
Swanson acknowledged that the size ofthe footprint ofthe primary residence would be the controlling
factor for the viability of a D-ADU. Commissioner Luker did not see the need to restrict the lot size
because several other factors would dictate whether a D-ADU could be placed on the lot. Mr. Crowell
suggested keeping that on the table.

Mr. Crowell brought up the question of what the D-ADU should look like, as discussed in Item 6 on
Architectural Design. Sheds, shops, and garages currently do not have any architectural design
standards. Chair Cragun commented that ifthe City cannot legally tell people what their house,shed,
or garage must look like, the City should not specify what the D-ADU must look like. She did not
support dictating design. Commissioner Kraupp would not want to see an anything-goes philosophy.
He agreed that design standards should not be excessive but believed there should be consistency
between the original primary dwelling and a D-ADU that is being added to the property. He felt that
the proposed text amendments were a good start in terms of verbiage since they specify that the
design, color palettes, and materials need to be reflective of the existing primary residence. He did
not see the need to be overly restrictive.


Chair Cragun was open to that idea if there was a good way to articulate it. She struggled with the
idea of specifying what is and is not acceptable. Mr. Crowell stated that part ofthe problem is that
currently, any type ofaccessory building is a hodge-podge. If design standards are to be established,
they would have to be objective, enforceable, and easy to administer.


With regard to item 6. A,Chair Cragun asked how matching materials would be acquired ifa D-ADU
were placed on a lot that has an older home with siding that can no longer be duplicated. Mr. Crowell
stated that ifthe siding was brick, brick could be used,regardless ofcolor. The same would apply to
other types of materials. Commissioner Kraupp stated that an easy solution would be to replace the
word "same" with the word "similar." Mr. Crowell commented that parameters would have to be
established for what constitutes "similar," especially with color tones. Chair Cragun did not object
to the color requirement stated in 6.B. If brick has to be used on a D-ADU because the primary
residence is brick, that would be much more expensive. Therefore, she felt it would be acceptable to
have the wood painted in a color that is similar to the brick color.


Commissioner Swanson noted that Mayor Hall indicated that her home has stucco siding and she
would not want to have a D-ADU with stucco siding. Commissioner Swanson asked if design
standards have been established for accessory buildings. Mr. Crowell stated that they have not. The
only requirements relate to height, setbacks, and lot coverage. He commented that accessory
buildings in Bluffdale vary greatly. Chair Cragun wrestled with the inconsistency of the fact that

Mr. Crowell reviewed an exhibit that specifies where parking can be located. The most important
restriction is that parking for an I-ADU or D-ADU cannot be located in the front yard. It also specifies

Commissioner Swanson commented that if a home is 5,000 square feet in size, a D-ADU could
potentially be 2,450 square feet. In that case, there would still have to be adequate space for parking.
He asked if there would be a limit to the number of bedrooms or occupants a D-ADU could have.

D-ADU could be only a single-family dwelling. Commissioner Swanson asked for the rationale
behind allowing only one kitchen. Mr. Crowell stated that a second kitchen would constitute a third
dwelling unit. He stated that he receives frequent phone calls about the permissibility ofa 60' x 80'
shop in Bluffdale, which would be 4,800 square feet. If an ADU were located on the upstairs floor,

larger than the home. Rather than placing a cap on the size, there could be a cap on the number of
bedrooms. Chair Cragun was reluctant to establish design standards due to the possibility that a large
two-story metal shop might occupy the upper floor and be converted into a D-ADU.

Section 11.340.080 - Non-Conforming Units was discussed. Mr. Crowell reported that there are
currently numerous instances of nonconformity. This section stipulates that ADUs that were legally
established prior to May 1, 2023, may continue to operate under the provisions that were in place at
the time of approval. Chair Cragun asked how ADUs that were not in compliance before May 1,
2023, will be dealt with. Commissioner Swanson stated that issues of non-compliance only occur
with I-ADUs since D-ADUs are currently not allowed. Mr. Crowell reported that if someone with a
non-compliant I-ADU wants to come into compliance, the only real need would most likely be an
official inspection. The main concern was how an existing shop would be converted into a D-ADU
when the shop does not meet the design criteria. It was reported that any non-compliance with I-
ADUs would be minor, such as an entrance location that does not meet the ordinance specifications.

Mr. Crowell reported that 11.340.090 - Violations became more robust since State Code specifies
how notices of violations must be issued for I-ADUs. The draft was crafted to make the State Code

apply to all ADUs. The policy provides the opportunity to resolve non-compliance issues rather than
going to court. Mr. Crowell indicated that sometime this year, the Planning Commission will review
proposals on how to enact administrative code enforcement. Remedies could include fines and liens
rather than going through the criminal court system.

Commissioner Swanson asked about the enforceability ofthe proposed Code with regard to existing
non-compliance issues. Mr. Crowell stated that if he received a phone call fi:om someone claiming
that a neighbor has an illegal D-ADU, the first task would be to gather substantial evidence in the
record without a Search Warrant. The City would have to prove that something illegal is taking place.

Erik Swanson moved to forward a POSITIVE recommendation to the City Council for the
Accessory Dwelling Units Text Amendment, Application 2022-49, based on the four findings
stated in the staff report dated February 24,2023,along with the additional findings 5(1)and 5
(2):


Findings:

       1.    That the proposed amendments further the options for housing in the community
             as contemplated in the City's adopted General Plan and the Moderate-Income
              Housing Plan.


       2.    That the regulations proposed balance property rights and community interests.


       3.    That the ordinance provides a path for property owners to legalize any currently
              non-compliant detached accessory dwelling units.


       4.    Allowing accessory dwelling units will increase safety through compliance with
             adopted building codes.


       5.    That the following modifications to the proposed text amendments be made:

              i.    That the City Council removes lot size standards for detached accessory
                    dwelling units because there are self-limiting barriers that control the
                    viability of a D-ADU (see 11.340.020.B).


             ii.    That the City Council removes design restrictions from the proposed code
                    (see 11.340.050.6.a.b.c).


Michael Kraupp seconded the motion. Vote on motion: Erik Swanson-Aye; Michael Kraupp-
Aye; Kory Luker-Aye; Debbie Cragun-Aye. The motion passed unanimously.
