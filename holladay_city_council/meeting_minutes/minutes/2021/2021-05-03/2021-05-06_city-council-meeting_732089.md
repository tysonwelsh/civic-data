IV.     Public Hearing on Participation in Ranked-Choice Voting for 2021 Municipal Election.
Mayor Pro Tempore Durham explained that Stan Lockhart was involved with the group that proposed
the change. Mr. Lockhart noted that in a traditional voting system, a voter votes for one person. But
with Ranked-Choice Voting, voters are able to rank candidates in order of preference. First-choice
candidates are counted the same as in a traditional election. If one candidate has more than 50% of
the vote, that candidate would win. However, if no one receives over 50% of the vote, the last place
candidate would be eliminated and the process would begin again. This would repeat until there was
a majority winner.

Mr. Lockhart reported that three years ago, the State Legislature passed a pilot program that allowed
cities to opt into Ranked-Choice Voting. The cities of Payson and Vineyard adopted Ranked-Choice
Voting. After the election, the County Clerk who administered the election sent out a survey to those
that participated. 86% found Ranked-Choice Voting to be “very much” or “somewhat easy” to use
and only 4.2% found it “not at all” easy to use. 82.5% of voters felt that Ranked-Choice Voting
should be used in future elections. Additionally, it was reported that 87.5% of candidates liked
Ranked-Choice Voting and 75% wanted to use it again in the future.

Mr. Lockhart overviewed some of the reasons for Ranked-Choice Voting. They were as follows:

   •   Saves taxpayer dollars by going directly to a general election, approximately half of the
       election costs would be saved;
   •   Makes for shorter and less expensive campaigns. Candidates can focus on a single election
       in November rather than an August primary followed by a November election;
   •   Ready for cities to implement. ES&S, the voting equipment awarded a State of Utah contract,
       was capable of running Ranked-Choice Voting elections;
   •   Voters more freely express their will. Voters could freely vote for the person they support
       most, even in situations where that candidate was not favored to win;
   •   A winner by majority vote. The final tally would be between the top two vote receiving
       candidates and the winner would receive more than 50% of the vote; and
   •   Eliminates the spoiler effect. Longshot candidates do not draw votes away from candidates
       that are preferred by most voters. There was also a tendency to focus on issues instead of
       personal attacks between candidates.

Council Member Fotheringham noted that the City of Holladay was holding a public hearing on

Legislature did not require that process. Mr. Lockhart stated that by virtue of the legislative body
being the decision-maker, that implied a public hearing. Additionally, all 23 cities and towns that
opted into Ranked-Choice Voting held public hearings.

        b.      Update on ADU Plan – LaNiece Davenport.
Ms. Davenport shared an update on Accessory Dwelling Units (“ADU”) with the City Council. She
reported that the State Legislature passed HB-82 - Single-Family Housing Modifications during the
2021 General Session. The slideshow presentation would describe the new legislation and provide
an overview of the ADU parameters that the City had already considered. Ms. Davenport would then
look to the City Council for direction on possible next steps.

The population growth in Utah was overviewed. Ms. Davenport explained that the population is
currently 3.3 million and was expected to reach 5.25 million by 2050. Having a lot of people within
a limited geography required local governments to be proactive and make educated decisions.
Ms. Davenport discussed two bills that had come out of the State Legislature: Senate Bill (“S.B.”) 34
- Affordable Housing and H.S. 82 – Single-Family Housing Modifications:

Ms. Davenport explained that an IADU is a new definition in the Code that came out of HB-82 and
was defined as follows: An IADU is a unit created within the primary dwelling or the footprint of a
primary dwelling, that is detached and owner-occupied, with a long-term rental of 30 consecutive
days or longer.

An image was shared that included examples of IADUs. Discussions were had about the examples.
Ms. Davenport noted that if a homeowner built a new garage, that would be considered an external
or an additional accessory unit. Council Member Fotheringham asked if that was effective by a

certain date. Ms. Davenport was not certain and made note of the question. Council Member Gibbons
believed that in order for there to be an IADU, the residence needs to be the primary dwelling of the
owner. Ms. Davenport confirmed this. Council Member Gibbons noted that there could be
enforcement issues as well as potential short-term rental issues.

Ms. Davenport overviewed some of the major land use changes of HB-82:

   •   Requires cities and counties to permit IADUs in at least 75% of all residential zones;
   •   Prevents cities from establishing certain IADU restrictions;
   •   Prohibits HOAs from prohibiting IADUs; and
   •   Allows persons to occupy IADUs whether they are related or not.

Further discussions were had about the 75% requirement for IADUs in residential zones.
Ms. Davenport reported that the City has the ability to create a 25% exclusion area. This could be an
action item for the City Council and would need to be done by October 1, 2021. The Council
discussed potential areas where the IADU requirements may not apply, such as the Holladay
Crossroads Zone and Holladay Hills Zone.

Ms. Davenport shared a slide that showed Council Members what a city could and could not do with
HB-82. She then overviewed a chart that compared the direction from the Planning Commission and
City Council in 2020 with the HB-82 requirements. For zones, the direction in 2020 was to only
allow ADUs on lots greater than 10,000 square feet regardless of the zone. However, HB-82 stated
that IADUs have to be permitted in all residential zones. In terms of size, in 2020 the direction was
to only allow ADUs on lots greater than 10,000 square feet and that they could not exceed the floor
area of a main home. Ms. Davenport explained that HB-82 stated that the total land size could not be
restricted (except on lots that were less than 6,000 square feet) and the street frontage could not be
restricted. There was alignment on the owner-occupied regulations.

The comparisons for internal versus external ADUs were outlined. Both the 2020 direction and HB-
82 allowed for internal. It was noted that there was the ability to allow for external units as well.
That would be a future policy discussion. The direction in 2020 for parking requirements was to
require minimum off-street parking stalls. HB-82 allowed cities to require one additional parking
stall and the replacement of any parking that was removed if a garage or carport was turned into an
IADU. Discussions were had about the current requirements for off-street parking. Mr. Godfrey
explained that in Chapter 13.80 there were off-street parking requirements for single-family homes.

Ms. Davenport reported that there was alignment on utilities. Neither the 2020 direction nor HB-82
wanted a secondary metering system in place. There was also alignment in terms of fees. There had
been discussions about there being a fee for new units as well as an annual renewal fee. The bill
would allow cities to obtain a permit or license, so that could be a registration fee and ongoing fee.
In terms of the uses, there was alignment on there being no short-term rentals. However, there was
not complete alignment with the uses, as the 2020 direction was that it must be the same use for the
main home. In addition, some home-based businesses would be allowed. According to HB-82, none
of the uses could be restricted.
