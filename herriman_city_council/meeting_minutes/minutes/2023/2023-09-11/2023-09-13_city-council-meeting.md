         2.1.   Providence Hall Performing Arts Center Proposal – Wendy Thomas, Assistant
               City Manager
      Assistant City Manager Wendy Thomas explained how Providence Hall wanted to build a
      performing arts center on campus. She noted they had been touring other performing arts
      centers for inspiration.

      Nate Marshall of Providence Hall introduced Kim Crandall as the president of the
      organization and Lorena Ohrn as the vice-president. They oriented the Council to the location
      of the high school campus and proposed site for the center. Mr. Marshall shared an overview
      of the floor plan for the performing arts center and expressed it would not only serve the
      high school but also Friends of Herriman. He noted the drawings were conceptual as final
      plans had not been drafted. Mr. Marshall highlighted other performing arts center site visits,
      and noted they served as a point of reference and insight for this design. He noted some of
      the main features of the lower floor, including the inclusion of a set room, a black box stage,
      and a loading deck to build sets off-site and bring them in. He added the lower floor would
      also include classrooms for their students. On the upper floor, he indicated they planned to
      have an additional deck for seating, a catwalk, as well as some storage space and possibly
      additional classrooms.

      Ms. Ohrn reviewed the benefits of the arts center and spoke about how unique their school
      was and the benefit to the City. She felt the center would be a good community center not
      only for the students and their families, but also Friends of Herriman. She pointed out this
      would be the first center of its kind in Herriman and thought this would bring a deeper
      appreciation of the arts to the area. Ms. Ohrn noted this facility would accommodate
      performances year-round. She proposed a usage agreement between the school and Friends
      of Herriman, and added the rough draft if the document was amenable to all involved parties.
      Ms. Ohrn then went over estimated costs and quoted the entire project at $6.5 Million. She
      noted they were lucky to have an architect who was a fan of the arts and said Providence
      Hall had been working closely with him on the design. She explained the school would donate
September 13, 2023 – City Council Minutes                                               Page 3 of 15


      land, provide all the parking, and contribute $3.5 Million of their own capital. She summarized
      that between Friends of Herriman, Herriman City, and some other large donors, the
      remaining portion would be $3 Million. She then asked the Council if they would consider a
      partnership.

      Mayor Palmer suggested conversations with Friends of Herriman prior to considering to
      commit to the project. Councilmember Shields agreed there was a high demand for arts,
      including orchestras and plays in the City, and he thought Herriman needed a venue to
      accommodate the interest. He also referenced a feasibility study for a performing arts center
      had been conducted, which yielded results which implied the cost would be around $600 per
      square foot. He pointed out that with the size of the building, based on the study the overall
      cost would be closer to $9 Million. Ms. Ohrn indicated the team was being conscious of the
      cost of the facility as it was important to keep it low. Mr. Marshall also elaborated as to how
      the team had come up with the cost estimate of $6.5 Million.

      Councilmember Shields discussed how the City would not have money in their budget for the
      next couple of years to give any capital for the project. Ms. Ohrn replied they did need capital,
      and asked if the City knew of any grants or other funding mechanisms which they would be
      able to pursue and utilize. Councilmember Shields commiserated about how tight their
      budget was, and reiterated his comments about how important the arts center was to have
      in the City. Councilmember Shields expressed the Council would absolutely help Ms. Ohrn
      with the grant application process and emphasized his support of the project even though
      there were no funds to donate at this time.

      Councilmember Henderson echoed Councilmember Shield’s comments and expressed his
      support of the project. He urged Ms. Ohrn to communicate with Friends of Herriman and see
      what funding they had available. He also discussed how there was money in Herriman, and
      suggested they hold fundraising events throughout the City to encourage donations from
      residents.

      Councilmember Shields asked Ms. Ohrn about her ongoing operation budget and made a
      comment about how most performing arts centers did not make enough money without taxes
      to cover their expenses. He asked what kinds of things they planned on doing to cover their
      ongoing costs. He discussed they would need to consider things such as endowments and
      naming rights which would help fund them moving forward.

      The Councilmembers all expressed appreciation for Ms. Ohrn and emphasized their support
      for the performing arts center as well as their regret that they were unable to provide funding.
      Councilmember Shields recommended Ms. Ohrn to review the feasibility study which was
      conducted as well.

              Update and Discussion Regarding Public/Private Open Fiber Utility Partnership –
              Nathan Cherpeski, City Manager
      City Manager Cherpeski explained this was a continuation from the discussion the Council
      had previously had regarding this item and said they had since outlined what basic utilities
      constituted. He discussed how they still needed to determine the level of service they wanted
      to provide as well as the wholesale rates. City Manager Cherpeski highlighted work still
      needed to do with how opting out would work, and agreed people needed the option to not
      utilize the service. He said the opt-out structure needed to be set up in a way that would
      leave people the option to opt in later. He also noted the need to consider if they wanted to
      enter into an interlocal agreement with other cities, which had the advantage of purchasing
      economies of scale, although he clarified each City would still be responsible for covering
      their own costs. Councilmember Shields clarified the interlocal agreement would not be a
      financial agreement, but rather a funding mechanism. City Manager Cherpeski also noted
      they needed to have these things finalized by November. Councilmember Shields clarified
      the Council would not be the ones to make these final decisions such as selling bonds; it
      would be the financial arm of the managing board. City Manager Cherpeski added the
      broadband committee had met with Strata to consider the wholesale rate.

      Councilmember Shields discussed how the utility fee was not just paying for bare minimum
      internet usage; it provided a much nicer service. He said due to this, it was less simple for
      people to opt out, and he said it would be a more robust legal process for people to opt out.
      Councilmember Henderson asked what the process of opting out entailed, and specifically
      wondered what would happen if later owners of the property wanted to have fiber.
      Councilmember Henderson pointed out in the future it would be much more expensive to
      have the fiber cables laid in the ground, so they needed to consider how that would work.
      City Manager Cherpeski thanked the Council for the feedback and said they would work on
      figuring out how the opt out process would work.

      Councilmember Henderson said beyond the financial component, they had to determine
      when and if people would be able to cancel service. He understood it was like phone
      contracts, and noted there was no such thing as a perpetual service contract so they needed
      to figure out how people would be able to leave and get rid of fiber. Councilmember
      Henderson added the City would then need a certain amount of time to collect the equipment
      and end their service. Councilmember Shields clarified the $15 utility fee encompassed many
      things beyond just internet; it also included things such as storm water, which people were
      not allowed to remove. Councilmember Shields explained the utility fee included a basic level
      of internet service which everyone in the City would receive and said the fiber would be
      considered the same as other utilities in the City, such as water or police. Councilmember
      Shields elaborated people could also chose to enter into agreements with different internet
      providers other than what the City offered and either pay month to month or enter into a
      contract, which would be a more analogous comparison to a phone contract.

      Councilmember Shields reported they had held a meeting with Utah Taxpayer’s Association,
      who had previously made public statements saying they were opposed to municipalities
      owning and operating broadband on the grounds that in the models they had looked at, there
      was some liability to the taxpayer should the model fail. Councilmember Shields explained
      the unique feature of their model was the revenue bonds were going to be strictly isolated
      for just the entity, and no sales tax would be dedicated to it. He explained the liability would
      be solely with the bond holders and there would be no risk to the City or the taxpayers as
      there were no City revenues dedicated to broadband. He emphasized it would be a separate
      entity, almost akin to a Public Infrastructure District (PID).

      Councilmember Henderson understood the taxpayer’s concerns, and asked what the
      safeguard was. He asked how they would separate the entity from the City if the Board of
      Directors were also the Council and City employees. City Attorney Sheeran replied if the
      City went out to bond, the interest rate would be lower because of taxpayer bonding. He
      clarified there was more risk for people buying the bond, and since it was not backed by
      taxpayer dollars the interest rate would be higher. Councilmember Shields reiterated this
      would be like a PID and said they would never generate revenue if they did not actually build
      the infrastructure.

      Councilmember Shields said the answers to some of the questions could be found through
      the model developed; there were no other models in other cities which satisfactorily
      answered these questions. He said the City was not building a bigger or faster internet, they
      were installing a municipal broadband network which could be utilized by internet providers
      as well as things like traffic monitors, first responder communication, and storm monitoring.
      Councilmember Henderson cautioned the need to be careful how they communicated this
      information to residents since it might be confusing. Councilmember Shields summarized the
      question at hand was how to quantify the benefits people would get from the $15 a month
      fee. Councilmember Shields said the most apparent benefit would be the more robust
      network and higher speed internet. He also discussed there was a net benefit in that existing
      internet providers would be able to reduce their prices to undermine anyone who wanted to
      join the network. He explained a reduced monthly rate effectively subsidized the $15 fee.

      City Manager Cherpeski acknowledged this would be difficult to explain and there would be
      some people who would not understand this, but for that reason it was important to have
      several levels to people opting out. Councilmember Shields agreed and said they needed to
      make every effort to explain to people the cost savings, and noted at some point their existing
      infrastructure would not be able to provide the speeds they wanted to have.


      City Manager Cherpeski mentioned they had a right-of-way, and said they needed to control
      what went in it. He pointed out if they had more fiber lines, it would be impossible to repair,
      and he commented if they were to accidently cut a fiber cable while working on a water or
September 13, 2023 – City Council Minutes                                              Page 6 of 15


      electric line it would be very expensive. He added another benefit of this was it limited
      franchises and the number of cables which would be in the ground. He said if the Council
      decided not to move forward, they would need to have an important discussion about how
      many providers the City wanted to allow in the City before the cables took up too much space
      in their rights-of-way. Councilmember Shields agreed this was an important thing to

          2.3. Review and discuss proposed zoning text amendments in Title 10 of Herriman City
               Code to regulate outdoor storage of recreational vehicles and trailers on private
               property – Clint Spencer, Planning Manager
      City Planner Michael Maloy began by stating this was a somewhat difficult proposal and
      highlighted feedback received from the Council about the proposal. He said he was looking
      to take direction from Council, and acknowledged staff had anticipated the Council would
      want to make changes to the document.

      Councilmember Shields commented he knew Councilmember Ohrn had vested interest in this
      topic and had some thoughts about it. He reported Councilmember Ohrn understood the
      reason for the ordinance and the need to prohibit commercial parking on residential lots,
      although she was concerned the number of trailers which could be parked was too restrictive
      and thought there was room for improvement. Councilmember Shields, speaking on behalf
      of Councilmember Ohrn, thought limiting the number was illogical if the person who owned
      the property also owned all the trailers, and proposed it might alleviate concerns about
      commercial parking if the stipulation was that the trailers needed to be owned by someone
      who lived on the property.

      City Planner Maloy elaborated on the reasons for the ordinance and said part of the reason
      they wanted to limit the number of trailers since they were only allowed to collect evidence
      from what they could observe from the public right-of-way. He explained while
      Councilmember Shields’ comments made sense, it was not enforceable. He added how in the
      past, they had only applied these rules to residential areas and not agricultural zones,
      although they experienced this issue more in the agricultural areas and so they wanted to
      have a way to enforce the Code. He emphasized how often complaints were received about
      excessive RVs in the agricultural zones. Police Chief Troy Carr interjected to say it would be
      easier to have allowances rather than exceptions, and he thought they would have a better
      time enforcing allowances to the ordinance rather than granting individual exceptions.

      Councilmember Shields reiterated his comment stipulating the trailers must be owned by
      people living on the property, and Councilmember Henderson wondered if they should limit
      the number of permitted RVs by acreage. Councilmember Henderson asked what the goal of
      the ordinance was, and City Planner Maloy explained they wanted to avoid commercial
      parking on private property. City Planner Maloy discussed it was difficult to enforce since
      they could not check it by license plate.
September 13, 2023 – City Council Minutes                                            Page 8 of 15


      City Planner Maloy discussed what other cities were doing. He said some cities stipulated
      the trailers had to be parked on a paved pad, while other cities were more relaxed in rules.
      He acknowledged all options available presented enforcement issues. He said the issue with
      Herriman was how many people could afford RVs and enjoyed outdoor recreation, and
      expressed this was not a minor issue since it would impact so many people in the City. He
      described how detailed and lengthy the document was, and said it was not the goal to be
      overly restrictive. He specified one of the standards in the document was that storage could
      not be a primary use. Councilmember Henderson observed how difficult it was to write a
      “one size fits all” ordinance.

      Councilmember Henderson indicated a photograph of a large lot with several trailers
      scattered throughout and expressed that to him this was not a concern if there was no threat
      to safety, and they were not creating a nuisance with the constant coming and going. He
      summarized the main issue was complaints from neighbors who found it to be an eyesore,
      and City Planner Maloy agreed it was primarily an urban issue, rather than being a safety
      issue.

      Councilmember Hodges pointed out if they changed the ordinance, the responsible owners
      would make changes and abide by the new Code, while the people who were currently
      breaking the law would not change anything until they were caught by Code enforcement.
      He thought this ordinance was a broad stroke and might not make all the changes the Council
      wanted to see since it was likely the people who were in violation of this ordinance were also
      breaking other standards for the City. Councilmember Henderson acknowledged this
      ordinance was not perfect, although he felt the value of the rules in the Code was that they
      gave the City tools to help resolve conflicts when they did arise.

      City Planner Maloy asked if they should hold another meeting when Councilmember Ohrn
      was available. They Council agreed she should be included in the process. City Manager
      Cherpeski thought they should simplify some of the ordinance, namely the sections about lot
      size, for simplicity. City Planner Maloy then proposed they could trim the proposal and send
      it back to the Planning Commission for reconsideration. Councilmember Henderson replied
      he was in favor of trimming it down.

          9.3. Discussion and consideration of a shared cost for School Safety Shuttles at Oak
               Leaf Elementary – Bryce Terry, City Engineer
      City Manager Cherpeski offered an overview of the request and indicated where the current
      walk route for Oak Leaf Elementary was located. He noted the City did not control how the
      boundaries were drawn, although it was their responsibility to ensure the safety of the
      schoolchildren as they used designated safe walking routes. City Manager Cherpeski noted
      the path was 3.64 miles, so while it was safer, it was not necessarily the most efficient. He
      also indicated where the crossing guards were located and said due to the construction it
      was difficult to get children across. He explained the proposal was for the City to pay half of
      the cost for a shuttle service that the School Board provided with buses. He added that
      previously, the City had sent a pre-payment of $10,000 to buy themselves some time and
      were now working with the lawyers of the School Board to create an actual agreement.

      City Manager Cherpeski explained the School Board had requested 30 days of notice before
      the City enacted construction of the sidewalk, striping, and signage, since at that point, the
      bus service would stop, and students would have to walk. He said it was likely the bus service
      would stop with Christmas break, although noted the estimates of contractors were always
      subject to change. He said they had received an agreement from the School Board that
