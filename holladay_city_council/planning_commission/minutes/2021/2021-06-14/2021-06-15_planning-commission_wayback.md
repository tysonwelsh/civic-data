WORK SESSION
Chair Jan Bradshaw called the Work Session to order at 5:33 p.m.

The agenda items were introduced. Planning Manager, Jonathan Teerlink, presented the
Conditional Residential Site Plan request for property located at 6393 South Crest Mount. He
explained that the property lies in two zones with the underlying zone being R-1-10 and the
Foothills Canyons Overlay Zone (“FCOZ”) overlay, which is a Hillside Protection Zone. He
explained that the Hillside Protection Zone is a Salt Lake County Ordinance that was established
in the 1980s for foothill development and specifically for canyons development. It is overreaching
and protects steep slopes from development. The ordinance specifically addresses areas with 33%
or greater slopes. Those areas are protected in a manner that is similar to the protection of
waterways. On platted lots, most of the area is steeper than 30%. The intent is to show that steeper
slopes are being protected to favor those that are flatter.

Chair Bradshaw noted that there are homes on all of the properties and asked if they were required
to obtain slope waivers. Mr. Teerlink stated that most did not because the majority preceded the
FCOZ, which was adopted in the 1990s. It was noted that there have been a few requests for slope
waivers over the years. Mr. Teerlink stated that the applicant has identified the flattest areas on
the site. The next step is to present a site plan showing where the proposed buildings are to be
located. It was noted that the applicant is a prospective buyer.

Mr. Teerlink identified the two affected areas, which have slopes of 20 to 30 percent. He explained
that the City’s Engineering Department is interested in conducting a soils test to determine
stability. That will take place at the next stage of the process. Commissioner Chris Layton stated
that these types of slopes are unique for Salt Lake County, however, professionally he was aware
of projects in Summit County that have similar slope characteristics. In his professional opinion,

Planning Manager, Jonathan Teerlink, presented the staff report and stated that the request is for
Conditional Use Site Plan approval that is specifically identified in the ordinance as a Slope
Waiver. The subject property is located in the Foothill Canyons Overlay Zone (“FCOZ”), which
is above Wasatch Boulevard and one of the few remaining undeveloped lots in a subdivision that
was created in the mid-1990s in the R-1-10 zone for single-family development. Salt Lake County
established the FCOZ as a hillside protection overlay to promote development within reason in
sensitive lands. The ordinance specifically addresses slopes of 30% or greater as requiring review
by the Planning Commission. When an application is submitted, a Slope Study is conducted that
delineates the site into sections.

The Technical Review Committee (“TRC”) has reviewed the application and find it appropriate
for a request for approval for this specific area. The City Engineer is requesting that upon possible
approval, that the applicant specifically look for a Slope Study and Soils Test to ensure stability of
the properties.

the property next door that follows the natural slope of the property.

be made part of the public record.

neighborhood and wanted to make sure that the applicants are aware of that. Mr. Teerlink
explained that in the City of Holladay, guesthouses are allowed on properties of one-half acre or
larger. Since the subject property is four acres in size, it qualifies. The definition of ‘guesthouse’
in the ordinance currently is a property that can be used for guests but cannot be leased or rented.
The applicants indicated that they both work from home so it will serve as an office and they have
no intention of renting it out.

3.      Text Amendment – 13.14, 13.04, 13.100; Accessory Dwelling Units, Permitted within
        Single-Family Zones.
Community and Economic Development Director, LaNiece Davenport, addressed HB-82, the
Single-Family Housing Modifications Bill, and had a basic understanding of why the Legislature
might be getting into the housing conversation. She explained that it is typically a local land use
issue so there was some question as to why the Legislature was getting involved. Ms. Davenport
explained that the Legislature has tried to be more proactive in terms of tackling Utah’s population
growth. By 2050, Utah’s population was expected to reach 5.25 million from the current 3.3
million. New residents will principally live along the Wasatch Front. As planners, they need to
be proactive.

In terms of housing, in 2019, the Legislature passed SB-34. It requires all cities and counties in
the State to consider a menu of housing options to implement. If the City did not have a transit
station, they could select three items from the menu. The three that the City of Holladay chose
were identified as:

       1.      Allow density and moderate-income housing at centers.
       2.      Reduce parking requirements.
       3.      Allow for Accessory Dwelling Units (“ADU”).

During the last Legislative Session, Representative Ward from Davis County co-sponsored HV-
82, which is the ADU bill. The objective is to increase the housing supply across the State. Cities
and counties are now required to permit Internal Accessory Dwelling Units (“IADU”) in all
primarily residential zones, which is a significant change. Staff made a similar presentation to the
City Council. Ms. Davenport defined an IADU as a unit that is created within the primary
dwelling; or the footprint of a primary dwelling that is detached and owner-occupied, with a long-
term rental of 30 consecutive days or longer. She clarified that the ADU ordinance is completely
different. She reported that it is a new definition created as a result of HB-82. The Legislature
made it clear that they cannot be short-term rental units and must have a long-term rental of at least
30 days or more. It was clarified that the main home must be owner-occupied. The list of things
cities can and cannot do was discussed.

Cities cannot restrict/require IADU size, use, street frontage and lot size

Cities can:
    • Require a primary dwelling;
    • Require owner-occupancy;
    • Require one additional onsite parking space;

4.      Text Amendment – Chapter 13 Land Use Development Code; Public Notice
        Requirements.
Mr. Teerlink reported that the proposed amendments pertain to senate bills that were recently
passed and changes in general circulation of newspaper print. Municipalities are required to
publish notice in general circulation newspapers but with the daily circulation being eliminated it
has become a hardship for cities to comply with that state law. It has since been amended to forego
any circulation noticing in favor of the Public Notice Website, which is now available to the public.
The amendment also allows staff to propose an opportunity to clarify and simplify the ordinance
in terms of noticing requirements. Staff is proposing to make it simpler for the public and
developers to understand and find specific noticing requirements for a myriad of applications.

Chair Bradshaw opened the public hearing. There were no public comments. The public hearing
was closed.

Commissioner Lloyd moved to recommend approval to the City Council of the ordinance
amendment on public noticing governing Sections 13.07.030, Text Amendment Requirements,
and 13.08. Public Noticing Requirements to simplify and consolidate the City’s Notification
Ordinance into a clear and concise notice matrix referenced as a new Appendix C. It was
recommended that notice be provided in public and community newspapers quarterly and
perhaps bi-annually regarding notifications and the website where they can sign up.
Commissioner Mackin seconded the motion. Vote on motion: Commissioner Chris Layton-
Aye, Commissioner Ricks-Aye, Commissioner Mackin-Aye, Commissioner Lloyd-Aye,
Commissioner Banks-Aye, Chair Bradshaw-Aye. The motion passed unanimously.
