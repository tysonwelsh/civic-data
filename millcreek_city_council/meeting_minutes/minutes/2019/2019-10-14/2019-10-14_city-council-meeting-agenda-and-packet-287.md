1. Overview of H.B. 411 Community Renewable Energy Act
Samantha DeSeelhorst went over her background experience for the Council. She went over
what other municipalities have done with H.B. 411. She said Summit County previously
passed a similar resolution (2017) that has a different aspirational date; it needs to be 2030 to
be eligible for this program. Park City (2018) and Moab (2019) signed resolutions that count
towards H.B. 411. Cottonwood Heights would review the resolution within the week.

Millcreek City Council Meeting Minutes 14 October 2019 Page 2 of 10

Holladay approved the resolution on October 10, 2019. She said South Jordan and other cities
were still reviewing the resolution. Mayor Silvestrini brought up forming a sustainability
committee with citizen volunteers. He said Millcreek has interest in anti-idling and recycling
efforts.

Sarah Inwood, Millcreek resident with experience in the power grid, said she was a technical
expert. She said the bill was written by the utility for the utility. She went over fundamental
misconceptions about the bill. The first is that the bill does not improve air quality along the
Wasatch Front or address the inversion. The second is that there are not bulk energy storage
technologies other than pumped storage hydro and Li-Ion batteries (only consist of 2.5% of
annual electricity consumed in the U.S.) that are affordable and scalable. The third
misconception is that solar and wind PV are cheap and will soon be cheaper than natural gas
generating assets. She gave the Council a high level overview of the power sector: it is highly
capital intensive; it is reliable, resilient, and affordable; it is environmentally responsible; it
follows the laws of physics with frequency at 60.0 Hz; and it is heavily regulated by state and
federal commissions. California, Oregon, and Vermont have instituted variations of renewable
energy options. She said Millcreek being 100% net renewable by 2030 was not achievable.
She said cities need flexible power generation to accommodate the demand and net
renewables are not flexible. She said the last misconception is that building solar and wind
options in the U.S. is the best way to combat climate change.

Mayor Silvestrini said he wanted the Council to adopt a resolution to be at the table during the
rate making process and not miss out on the program. Inwood said H.B. 411 has unique
features: 1) Rocky Mountain Power and the Public Service Commission of Utah select the
consultant and there would be no reviewing consultant; 2) the resident opt out period would
be 60 days with two notices; 3) there would be a penalty to opt out after the opt out period
was to be determined; 4) the rate making process would increase annually without city
involvement; and 5) after the launch of the program, there is no defined role for the cities to
participate. She said San Francisco offers three service options and the residents get to pick.
Those rates are three times higher than what Utah residents currently pay. H.B. 411 does not
mention rate cost controls. She felt there were misconceptions of what the bill offered. Her
recommendations were: 1) cities select the consultant/reviewer for the rate impact study; 2)
tiered service options be offered; 3) there be no penalty to leave the program; 4) start with
renewable energy credits and 5-year power agreements to reduce price risk; and 5) empower
the cities with oversight authority over Rocky Mountain Power’s submissions to the Public
Service Commission. Council Member Marchant asked about renewable energy credits
(RECs). Inwood said a power plant that produces renewable energy has the right to sell the
claim of renewable energy to anyone in the country. Some entities could buy the credits
without being connected to them on the grid. Mayor Silvestrini said there was a benefit to
buying RECs by reducing the burning of fossil fuels to reduce carbon output.

Council Member Marchant inquired about the State Legislature being able to accommodate
her recommendations by the cities opt-in deadline. Mayor Silvestrini said there would need to
be some measure of control for the participating cities, otherwise the cities would not have to
continue with program. Inwood said she had been told that the cities may sit on the selection
committee and that the rates would be reasonable. Council Member Jackson suggested
approving the resolution contingent on certain conditions to ensure participation. Council
Member Marchant mentioned that there would be a cost for the study by opting in. Mayor

Silvestrini said the cost would come when signing an agreement with Rocky Mountain Power.
Mayor Silvestrini said Hillary Lambert, another Millcreek resident with a power background,
recommended that Millcreek opt-in to the program.

Travis Jones, Rocky Mountain Power Business Manager, said Sandy City had investigated the
bill and its implications. Mayor Silvestrini asked Jones to ask Rocky Mountain Power
authorities if they would be willing to address the concerns that Inwood raised and in letting
the cities be a player.

Kate Bowman, Utah Clean Energy, said a lot of the concerns raised by Inwood had been
discussed but they were not at that stage in the process yet (stages 3, 4, and 5). There is no
formal process for the cities’ participation. The cities will work with the utility on procuring
resources. Council Member Jackson asked about the press release from PacifiCorp and its
relation to H.B. 411. Bowman said Rocky Mountain Power 20-year resource plan shows 7
gigawatts of solar battery storage and wind. Rocky Mountain Power customers as a whole will
not be 100% renewable by 2030. She said the analysis shows that the shift to renewable
resources makes economic sense.

Hunter Holman, Utah Clean Energy, said the statute is intentionally ambiguous as far as how
the program will be administered. The Steering Committee will be made up of the
participating communities which will have a say in how the program is administered and in
resource selections. There will be some discretion that the communities will have throughout
the process. They are in the rulemaking process right now and will send rules to the Public
Service Commission next month. Passing the resolution reserves the cities’ right to participate
in the program. By participating in the negotiating process, Millcreek would not be bound to
anything. The consultant will not be hired to design the rate but evaluate the proposal at the
Public Service Commission review phase. There is city discretion in the agreement
negotiation phase to work with Rocky Mountain Power to design the rate. Council Member
Uipi asked about public comment taking place before cities enter into an agreement. Holman
said stakeholders will come up with a joint set of rules to propose to the Public Service
Commission and the Public Service Commission may adjust the proposal. They would then
take public input about the rules before adopting any. Council Member Uipi asked if any
legislation would go to the upcoming legislative session that would affect H.B. 411. Holman
did not anticipate any.

Holman discussed the opt-out phase. He said there would be a 60-day period where customers
will be given two notices that they will be opted into the program unless they actively opt out.
After the 60 days, the program begins and then residents can still opt out during the next three
billing cycles without being subject to a termination charge. They can still opt-out after that,
but they may be subject to a termination charge. Council Member Jackson asked why there
would be a termination fee. Holman said the termination fee helps balance customer charges
because the rates have been designed for the year, so if too many people opt-out then the rates
are left to the remaining customers. Council Member Jackson expressed concern about
burdening vulnerable populations that do not pay attention to opting out. Council Member
Uipi asked who would be on the stakeholders committee. Holman said the meeting would be
public and the stakeholders would be the Division of Public Utilities, the Office of Consumer
Services, Rocky Mountain Power, Utah Clean Energy, the Interwest Energy Alliance, Heal
Utah, and others. Mayor Silvestrini said Kurt Hansen from Millcreek staff had been attending.

2. Urban Land Institute Presentation; Francis Lilly, Planning and Zoning Director, Mike
Winder, Economic Development Director, and Nicole Chidester, Assistant to the
Economic Development Director
Mayor Silvestrini said three members of staff attended the Urban Land Institute in
Washington D.C.; Francis Lilly, Mike Winder, and Nicole Chidester. They then went over the
top ten takeaways from the Institute.

Li.

Community Land Trusts - Lilly said the availability of housing is not catching up with
household creation. Millcreek needs to think about ways to create more opportunities for
affordable housing. A community land trust removes the land from owners’ mortgages
and is owned by a trust. Salt Lake City has initiated a land trust. The advantage is that it
does not disrupt existing neighborhood forms. Mayor Silvestrini asked if cities fund the
land trust. Lilly said yes, sometimes community reinvestment areas fund them. He said he
thought the land trust would qualify for the affordable housing requirement in the
community reinvestment area. Council Member Marchant asked about equity in the land.
Lilly said the resident would not get equity in the land unless there was a deal through re-
financing or at the end of the lease. The trustee would be the City or someone the City
designates.

Tools to Make It Happen - Winder said business improvement districts can make parts of
the City special, like installing furniture and greenery. He said naming rights are another
tool. Winder said some states are giving incentives for opportunity zones. Cities could
utilize public air space and should maintain focus through the plan.

Transportation Demand Management - Lilly said the management would be a series of
tools in ordinance that forces a developer who commits a lower parking ratio to a
performance measure that is measurable and verifiable. He said some cities require the
developer to post a bond which binds the developer to honoring on an ongoing basis a
commitment to reduce travel demand through incentivizing walk trips or subsidizing
transit passes. Mayor Silvestrini asked about discretion in ordinance. John Brems said
discretion could be added to the ordinance. Lilly said cities have written standards. Brems
said the development agreement would be approved by the City Council and the Planning
Commission could make a recommendation on it.

Water Works: Why H2O0 Make Good Places Great - Chidester said adding a water
element creates ambiance and an oasis feel in a concrete jungle. She said water creates art,
makes a place marketable, grabs attention for memorials, and provides entertainment.

Looking Up: Lessons Learned from Arcades, Strategic Architecture, and Palmer Alley —
Lilly said an awning needs to be at least 10 feet wide. He said signs could be integrated
into the structure. He highlighted pictures of examples for alleyways with matrix material.

2. Planning Matters
2.1 First Reading:

a. GP-19-002, Amendment to the Millcreek Together General Plan; Blaine
Gehring, Planner
Mayor Silvestrini said planning matters are heard through a first reading by staff
and the Council then acts on the items at another meeting. Blaine Gehring said
planning staff had been requested to process an amendment to the Millcreek
Together General Plan in response to a neighborhood petition. The request is to
change the area in the vicinity of 4000 S. and 700 W. designated as Neighborhood
2 in the Future Land Use Map to a Neighborhood 1 designation. The subject area
lies in the area that is one of the few remaining agricultural areas left in Millcreek,
many of which are slowly being sold off and rezoned for housing. Millcreek has
two agricultural zones remaining in the area which host a variety of agricultural
uses that are no longer compatible with urban living. The subject area is
approximately one mile west from the Meadowbrook TRAX Station and is
situated among other medium to high density housing just to the north and into
South Salt Lake. Due to the proximity of the TRAX station, the area has become a
site for medium to high density housing which also contains some low-income
housing. Basic planning concepts encourage a higher density of residential when
in close proximity to public transit and parks. One of the goals of the Millcreek
Together General Plan is to increase the amount of affordable housing in the City.

Gehring said Millcreek’s Future Land Use Map indicates the area to be designated
as “Neighborhood 2” to accommodate a mix of different higher density housing
types. During the overall General Plan process, it was felt that this was a good
area where medium density housing could be developed at a more affordable
price range. The area was designated Neighborhood 2 because of its adjacency to
existing multiple family housing, a large public park and next to industrial zoning.
At their meeting on September 3, 2019, the Millcreek Community Council voted
unanimously to send a favorable recommendation to the Planning Commission on
this General Plan amendment. At their meeting on September 18, 2019, the
Millcreek Planning Commission voted 4 in favor and 3 against to send a favorable

2.4 Discussion and Consideration of Ordinance 19-43, Approving a Development
Agreement for Clearwater Development; Blaine Gehring, Planner

Blaine Gehring said the parcel is zoned MD and is adjacent to M and MD zoning to the
north and west and MD and R-M zoning to the east (Artesian Springs). The property
borders Big Cottonwood Creek with Murray City on the south side of the creek and the
new Front Climbing Gym is being built directly to the east of the property. The
development agreement would allow for up to 150 three-story townhomes with 2,200
square feet of commercial space and 6 live/work units. A portion of the townhomes
would be platted as owner-occupied dwellings, and a portion of the development will
remain as a multifamily rental development. In addition, the developers will develop a
1.09-acre park which will be dedicated to the City to include a pedestrian bridge across
Big Cottonwood Creek to facilitate pedestrian traffic to the Murray North TRAX station.
Both the Millcreek Community Council and the Planning Commission have sent
favorable recommendations on this project subject to the development agreement.

Council Member Jackson asked about the owner-occupied percentage. Gehring said the
development agreement called for 50% owner-occupied. Mayor Silvestrini said 50% will
be for-sale and 50% will be rented and a declaration of covenants will set those ratios.
Council Member Catten asked about affordable housing units. Francis Lilly said it was
not contemplated as part of the development. Council Member Catten asked about park
maintenance. Mike Winder said Millcreek would be responsible for the park
maintenance. Mayor Silvestrini said this was effectively the same park space the Council
previously approved for Mr. Hassenflu. The prior project had greater density that was all
rental units though the amenities the City gets would be the same. Mayor Silvestrini
asked about the conversations with Murray City on the pedestrian bridge. Lilly said
Murray was amenable to the bridge if they did not have to pay for it. Millcreek will help
in the facilitation of the bridge, but the developer will have to pay for and install it per the
City’s standards. Lilly said staff tried to fulfill the Meadowbrook Small Area Master Plan
with a mid-block crossing between the rail line and Main Street and this project
accomplishes that. Council Member Jackson asked who was designing the park. Lilly
said the developer will design the park and it will be approved by staff. Mayor Silvestrini
brought up a typographical error in reference to Big Cottonwood Creek in paragraph 5 of
the development agreement being referred to as Cottonwood Creek. The development
agreement is ancillary to the participation agreement being considered by the Community
Reinvestment Agency. It does provide for reimbursement to the developer using tax
increment for the park and park amenities. The reimbursement amount is the cost of
improvements and some interest on the developer’s loan. Millcreek is pledging only the
increment generated by this project.
