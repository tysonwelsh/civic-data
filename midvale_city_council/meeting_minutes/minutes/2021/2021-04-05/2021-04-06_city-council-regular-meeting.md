e Discuss Residential Planned Unit
Alex Murphy said for many years, the City has had standards for master planned
developments in the residential (SF and RM) zones. This language predates the current
version of the ordinance adopted in 2001. It has seen minor updates over time but has
remained largely unchanged for many years.
Recently, the City has started receiving more complaints regarding the impacts of master
planned developments on existing neighborhoods. These complaints have included
topics such as parking, open space, density, building heights, setbacks, and others. In
response to these complaints, the City Council directed Staff to propose changes to
master planned development standards. The City Council also adopted a moratorium
temporarily prohibiting multifamily master planned developments in the SF zones in July
2020, was made permanent in March 2021.
Staff spent several months meeting with Councilmembers to obtain input on what issues
they see around master planned developments and researching best practices for
allowing this style of development. The attached language is a first draft of standards for
a new development type, a “residential planned unit development” or “RPUD,” that could
replace the existing master planned development language. This new language includes
the following:

e New density standards, with density increasing as project size and unit mix increase.

e Allowed and prohibited uses within RPUDs. All projects may use single family
detached or two-family dwellings. As project size increases, additional unit types are
allowed: single-family attached (townhomes, up to 4 units per building) can only be
constructed in projects at least 2 acres in size and multifamily projects (between 4-8
units per building) are not allowed until a project reaches 5 acres.

e Nondiscretionary density bonuses for preserving existing housing stock, providing
affordable units, and providing for-sale products.

e Minimum lot size and setback requirements. Side setbacks are increased above
underlying zone requirements, providing a better buffer along the edges of the project,
and a maximum number of units per building is established, prohibiting overly large
buildings.

e Housing mix requirements. To receive the highest allowed density in any project, an
RPUD must provide a variety of housing types based on overall project size. Projects
under 5 acres must provide at least 2 housing types, with each housing type
constituting at least 25% of the total unit count. Projects over 5 acres must provide at
least 3 housing types, with each type constituting at least 15% of the total unit count
and not exceeding a limit based on unit type (ranging from 30-60%). Projects could
choose to provide fewer unit types but would not be eligible for the highest density
numbers as a result.

e Building design standards, including requirements for porches on certain units,
minimum roof pitch, garage design, window coverage, and other standards. Existing
units that are preserved for RPUDs are encouraged, but not required, to meet these
standards. The State Legislature adopted a bill that prohibits cities from implementing
building design standards unless some other incentive is applied to the project. As this
language is refined, additional incentives may need to be provided.

e Minimum parking ratios. Per unit calculations are based on bedroom count with a
reduction provided for projects providing affordable housing at or below 30% AMI.
Guest parking is based on project size and unit count. Overflow parking is required for
Stalls located in a private garage; this is intended to provide additional parking
opportunities when garages are converted to storage areas. On-street parking may
be used for a portion of the guest and overflow requirements and must be evenly
distributed throughout the project.

e Design standards for parking areas, including minimum stall size and aisle width,
garage location and orientation, and screening requirements.

e Open space and primary park space requirements. A primary park must be provided
centrally located in the project and include a variety and type of amenities that
increases based on project size. The park may not be used as an above-ground
stormwater management facility unless it includes a permanent wet pond. At least
40% of the project must be open space.

e General landscape standards, including maximum turf coverage, minimum plant
coverage, minimum tree count, and other standards meant to bring landscape design
up to par with existing requirements. l

hold a public hearing and adopt a written recommendation to the City Council, advising

the Council to approve, disapprove, or modify a request on all zoning map amendments.

The City Council may only approve a rezone request if it determines, in written findings,

that the proposed rezoning is consistent with the policies and goals of the general plan

and that the applicant has demonstrated the following:

L 1. The proposed rezoning is necessary either to comply with the general plan
proposed land use map or to provide land for a community need that was not
anticipated at the time of adoption of the general plan;

2. The existing zoning was either the result of a clerical error or a mistake of fact, or
that it failed to take into account the constraints on development created by the
natural characteristics of the land including, but not limited to, steep slopes,
floodplain, unstable soils, and inadequate drainage; or

XB The land or its surrounding environs has changed or is changing to such a degree
that it is in the public interest to encourage redevelopment of the area or to
recognize the changed character of the area.

This property is currently a nonconforming single-family home and has been under

continual residential use. The City has no record of business license being issued for a

commercial use at this property. Historical zoning maps show the multifamily residential

developments surrounding the project were originally under a commercial zone and were
rezoned to multi-family residential between 1983 and 1992, leaving this parcel as the only
commercially zoned property in the area. The history of the area supports a claim that the
surrounding environs have changed to such a degree that it is in the public interest to
encourage redevelopment of the area and to recognize the changed character of the

foreseeable change in the types or intensity of land uses in the future.”
Additionally, the General Plan identifies the following future land use goals for stability

4. Provide for access to parks, trails and recreation facilities
a Provide for appropriate transit opportunities

The proposed rezone furthers goals number 1, and 6 by allowing the property to continue
as a residential use. As discussed above, the surrounding area transitioned away from

Chad’s Ford Apartments were built in 1986 (according to County records). This property

use. Rezoning the property to allow the residential use to continue supports neighborhood
stability, as well as provides mechanisms to accommodate today’s lifestyles and needs.
The proposed rezone is also consistent with the City’s Housing Plan, adopted in 2019 as
an addendum to the General Plan. Goal three is to expand the variety of housing

providing for a mixture of housing sizes, densities, types and affordability in each area of
the City. The project aligns with 3 that goal by providing a variety of housing sizes within
the project and adding to the mixture of housing types in the surrounding area.

Alex Murphy said in 2001, the City adopted a new zoning ordinance following annexation

of the Fort Union area. This ordinance established revised the City’s ordinance and

ordinance, standards for master planned developments were carried forward from the

addressed open space: a requirement for 15% of the project area as improved common

open space’ and 50% of the project area as general open space”. These standards have
frequently been discussed as projects have occurred over the years but have not been
changed since they were adopted in 2001.

In response to concerns raised during the planning process for the Midvale Mills project,
Mr. Nash has proposed a modification to the two open space requirements in the RM-12
zone. Pursuant to the text amendment process described in §17-3-1 of the Midvale City
Municipal Code, Staff has worked with Mr. Nash on the requested changes and a draft of
the proposed changes is now available for Planning Commission consideration. This text
amendment proposes changes to Section 17-7-3.9, adding the following as a new
subsection E.

Notwithstanding the requirements of Section 17-7-3.9(C), if the proposed master planned
development is in excess of 10 acres, anticipates more than 60 residential units, and is
adjacent to properties zoned with an agricultural overlay under Section 17-7-1.15 along
at least 65% of the project boundary, the applicant may reduce the amount of the
improved common open space by up to six percent (6%) so long as the total minimum
open space requirement is increased by the same amount as the improved common open
space reduction.

This change would create an opportunity for larger master planned development projects
in specific locations to reduce their improved common open space requirement, provided
the general open space is increased by the same amount, and has both positive and

L negative aspects for the Council to consider.

On the positive side, projects eligible to take this option will be infrequent and approving
the change would resolve some of the complaints raised regarding the Midvale Mills
project. There are few places left in the City where 10+ acres are available for
development adjacent to property with the Agricultural Overlay, so it is unlikely another
project eligible for this provision will occur. The attached map shows the location of all
RM-12 and SF-1/AO property in the City; the only places where the two zones are
adjacent to each other are along 7800 South and 8000 South. Along 7800 S, there is not
enough property available to use the provision, so only projects around 8000 South are
likely to see it.

Additionally, if the text amendment is approved, the Midvale Mills project will be able to
remove two elements that have caused concern: a trail connection to 150 E and a
community garden on the southeast corner of the 8000 S / 300 E intersection. The trail
connection has received pushback from adjacent neighbors, who are concerned about
possible negative consequences from its proximity to their yards. The developer is
concerned about the community garden, due to its separation from the remainder of the
project and impact on future projects involving the property where the MVCA church
building is currently located. If the amendment is approved, both of these areas would no
longer be necessary to meet ordinance requirements and would be removed.
On the negative side, the requested amendment may not be consistent with the City’s
General Plan and the proposal is requested to address an issue that could be addressed
pursuing other methods. Areas with the Agricultural Overlay generally fall within Stability
Areas as defined by the General Plan. Included in the goals for Stability Areas are the
following:

2. Buffer uses in Stability Areas from more intensive land uses nearby, including

adjacent Opportunity Areas.
3. Provide for better pedestrian/bicycle connections through and between
neighborhoods.

4. Provide for access to parks, trails and recreation facilities. (pg. 46)
The General Plan also states that the City needs more open space because it already
falls below the open space per capita recommended by the National Recreation & Park
Association. Reducing improved common open space requirements for master planned
developments will move projects away from meeting these goals and weaken amenity
options for large master planned developments in the future. Removing the trail
connection directly contradicts goals 3 and 4 above and reduces opportunity for future
access to the Porter Rockwell Trail along 150 E.
Second, the amendment is tailored to resolve an issue that is caused by design decisions.
This request comes as part of the Midvale Mills project on 8000 South. If the amendment
were approved, the trail connection to 150 E and community garden area would be
removed from the plan, reducing improved common open space (from 15% to 10%) and
overall open space (from 56% to 55%) in the project. This text amendment is not the only
option to resolve concerns with the project's design: lot sizes could be reduced across
the project or units could be removed to provide the required improved common open
space without requiring the trail connection or community garden. Modifying the zoning
ordinance to address a single project is poor planning and establishes a precedent that
the City is willing to create exceptions to the zoning ordinance for individual projects.

REZONE

Rezones are legislative decisions required whenever a change in designation on the

City’s Zoning Map is requested. The Zoning Ordinance includes requirements for findings

and procedures necessary to consider and adopt a rezone. The General Plan provides

direction on the City’s future land use goals and must be considered whenever a rezone
request is made.

Section 17-3-1 of the Midvale City Municipal Code requires the Planning Commission

hold a public hearing and adopt a written recommendation to the City Council, advising

the Council to approve, disapprove, or modify a request on all zoning map amendments.

The Planning Commission held a public hearing on the rezone request on May 13, 2020

and tabled the decision. In a later meeting on September 9, 2020, the Planning

Commission adopted a recommendation to not approve the rezone request.

The City Council may only approve a rezone request if it determines, in written findings,

that the proposed rezoning is consistent with the policies and goals of the general plan

and that the applicant has demonstrated the following:

1. The proposed rezoning is necessary either to comply with the general plan
proposed land use map or to provide land for a community need that was not
anticipated at the time of adoption of the general plan;

iz. The existing zoning was either the result of a clerical error or a mistake of fact, or
that it failed to take into account the constraints on development created by the
natural characteristics of the land including, but not limited to, steep slopes,
floodplain, unstable soils, and inadequate drainage; or

rn The land or its surrounding environs has changed or is changing to such a degree
that it is in the public interest to encourage redevelopment of the area or to
recognize the changed character of the area.

The discussion in the October 20, 2020 Staff Report, attached, addresses how the

proposed rezoning is consistent with the City’s General Plan and zoning ordinance. An

G. CONSIDER ORDINANCE NO. 2021-0-08 ADOPTING A ZONING

ORDINANCE TEXT AMENDMENT TO MODIFY DEVELOPMENT
STANDARDS IN THE MAIN STREET FORM-BASED CODE ZONE (MS-
FBC)

Alex Murphy said in 2020, the City adopted a new form-based code zoning ordinance for

the Main Street neighborhood. This ordinance established new goals for the district,

review process, and design standards for development in the neighborhood following

adoption of the Main Street Small Area Plan.

As part of the design process for a new fire station at 655 West Center Street, Mr. Slabbert

of AJC Architects has identified design requirements that conflict with the needs of his

client, Unified Fire Service Area and has submitted a text amendment request to modify

the conflicting standards. Pursuant to the text amendment process described in §17-3-1

of the Midvale City Municipal Code, Staff has worked with Mr. Slabbert on the requested

changes and a draft of the proposed changes is now available for Planning Commission

consideration. The proposed text amendment incorporates changes to those standards

that conflict with UFSA’s design criteria plus additional changes proposed by Staff to

address typographical errors, elements inadvertently left out of the adopted code, and

tweaks to certain open space and landscaping calculations.

This text amendment proposes changes to the following sections:

17-7-11.3 Uses.

L This section includes changes to specific use provisions in subsection B. It has been
modified to relocate specific use provisions from the Definitions chapter and waive certain
design elements associated with fire station and police stations that are not compatible
with the form-based code. As proposed:

e Fire and police stations would be allowed to have:
o Garage doors on the front facade;
o Wider driveways than the maximum allowed by frontage type; and
o Visitor parking areas in the front yard and parking setback areas, provided
all parking-related landscape requirements are satisfied.
e Fire stations would be further allowed to:
o Be located outside of the build-to zone and corner occupation requirements;
o Be exempted from frontage coverage requirements;
o Use equipment storage garages to meet occupied depth requirements; and
o Be shorter than the minimum height.
o Install turf along vehicle access points between the building and street as
needed to address visibility concerns.
These changes would override the design elements described in the frontage and building
type sections of the MS-FBC because these uses have operational requirements that are
difficult to satisfy with the form-based code standards.
17-7-11.7 Open space.
45

The proposed changes to this section would modify minimum requirements for open

space. As proposed: 1

e Minimum seating requirements in all open space types would be reduced to 1 seat
per 500 SF.

e Minimum tree count requirements in all open space types would be reduced to 1
tree per 1000 SF.

e Minimum bicycle rack requirements in all open space types would be reduced to 1
rack per 1200 SF.

e A bicycle repair station would be required in all open space types. For larger open
space types (town square and park), 1 additional bicycle repair station would be
required for each additional acre of area.

e Construction of parks by other governmental entities beyond the City and County
would be allowed.

e Maintenance of park areas would be required to meet the same standards as
landscape areas in the MS-FBC zone.

These changes are the result of attempting to design open spaces to meet current
requirements and discovering they have substantial negative impacts on the design: the
seating requirement resulted in more seats than could possibly be filled in open space
areas; the tree requirement resulted in more trees than could be supported; and the
bicycle rack requirements required more racks than could ever be used. Adding a bicycle
repair station instead of more racks encourages more bicycle traffic in the neighborhood.
Allowing other governmental entities to build parks expands the availability of open space
without automatically imposing additional burdens on the city and adopting maintenance
standards establishes a minimum quality of park space that encourages future users to
continue visiting.

17-7-11.8 Landscaping.

The changes in this section are minor modifications proposed by Staff to address topics
that were not included in the original code. They include:

e Prohibiting flowering pear trees of all species along streets. These trees frequently
drop fruit on sidewalks, smell bad in the spring, and begin losing branches after a
short lifespan.

e Clarifying that landscaping may only be installed as one of the landscape types in
this section. This is intended to encourage more building area and less
landscaping to emphasize the urban nature of the district.

e Modifications to the Side and Rear Landscape Buffer to clarify a fence or hedge is
required, allow sidewalks in the buffer area, and remove trees where space is not
available for them to thrive.

e Error corrections throughout.
