Transportation Planning Update:
Julia Collins, Transportation Planning Manager, Robert Stewart UDOT Region Two
Director via Zoom, Carl Miller, Summit County Regional Transportation Director, and
Gabe Shields, Matt Neeley, John Robertson, Alex Roy, Hannah Pack, and Franklin
Williams, Engineering and Transit Departments, were present for this item. Shields
discussed the Snow Creek Walkability Feasibility Study, which studied a graded
pedestrian crossing in the Snow Creek vicinity of SR248. He reviewed when this was
presented last May, Council favored a tunnel somewhere in that area as well as at-
grade improvements to the SR248/SR224 intersection. His team met with UDOT and
they weren’t interested in helping with the intersection project. Staff also pursued a
public/private partnership to construct a tunnel with a development at the DoubleTree
Hotel site and through discussions, staff determined they did not want to place a tunnel
there and be connected to that project. They were currently putting out an RFP for a
designer to put together designs and cost estimates. He proposed returning in the fall
with the designs and cost estimates and getting Council direction.

Collins referred to the list of disruptive transportation ideas. She noted the ideas were
futuristic and they gave the City tools for planning. She and her team grouped the list
into five categories: Existing City Policy, Regional Support, Special Projects, Not
Currently Pursuing, and Grant Projects. She noted UDOT would give the City and
Summit County a grant to study the disruptors to see which would be best for the City. It
would also study parking, identify park and ride locations, look at future transit needs,
traffic, access, parking policies and parking technologies. Following the evaluation,
recommendations would be made on parking solutions, along with implementation and
funding strategies. They would then present performance recommendations and a
marketing plan. The grant would be awarded in the spring and Summit County would be
the lead.

Collins stated SR248 was a UDOT corridor and it was a priority for the City. She
reviewed in 2009, $10 million was provided by UDOT to the Statewide Transportation
Improvement Program. They started an environmental analysis in 2018, but in July of
2019, the project stopped because the City determined the preferred alternative of a
widening project didn’t fit with the vision of the community. In 2021, UDOT approved a
rehabilitation project including an upgraded westbound shoulder lane, crosswalks, and
the Richardson Flat traffic signal. She recommended advancing two potential paths.
First was a short-term approach of building up the eastbound shoulder of SR248 for bus
usage. They would take space from the median and shift it to the shoulder. They would
also look at ways to advance the shoulder for bus usage from the SR224/SR248
intersection to Wyatt Earp. The other concept was exploring all transit possibilities with
a long-term corridor plan. Collins noted staff needed to act now since these projects
would take many years.

Council Member Dickey asked how they would annualize the disruptive ideas. Collins
stated the list was the foundation. Pack stated they would explore all the options to
make sure any technology presented to the City would provide a benefit to the City and
to those who lived here. Council Member Dickey asked if there was a five-year
implementation plan for the disruptor ideas. Roy indicated Council Members Dickey and
Rubell, along with the stakeholder group, could look at some things outside the box, and
advance those ideas or technologies to an analysis level. Council Member Dickey
asked if those ideas could be looked at independently and if staff could get them to an
implementation plan. Roy agreed five or six ideas could be moved along for viability.

Council Member Toly asked if reverse signaling on SR248 could be considered. Collins
indicated that was good for a long-term solution and it would be evaluated. They would
look at one-way, reversible lanes. Council Member Toly thought the light at McPolin

Mayor Worel stated that was being looked at on a regional level.

Ken Fisher and Tate Shaw, Recreation Department, and Erik Daenitz, Assistant Budget
Director, presented this item. Fisher reviewed the history of the recreation facilities. He
indicated the Recreation Advisory Board (RAB), staff, and community members made
up a steering committee to work on the master planning process and they focused on
asset management, supporting community needs, and building new infrastructure. They
proposed to expand and renovate the summer camp building and aquatics center as
well as construct a sports complex at Quinn’s Junction. A survey was distributed, and
1,134 responses were received. The construction of additional pickleball courts was a
priority. He stated the sports complex would include indoor and outdoor pickleball
courts, indoor space for trail users, parking stalls, a Nordic training area, a new ice
sheet, improved trailhead access, and more. He also noted Council’s highest priority
from the mid-year retreat was to renovate the summer camp building. The board
recommended the following priority if the projects could not be completed
simultaneously: aquatics, summer camp building, and sports complex construction.

Fisher stated the summer camp building was in an ideal location since it was close to
the fields, playground, and the library. He estimated the cost to renovate it would be $13
million. The MARC pool needed to be replaced. When he looked at pickleball courts at
the MARC, the space was too constrained, so they looked at a new facility at Quinn’s
Junction. There was a lot of property there and regional partnerships could be
considered for different amenities. He recommended keeping the bubble at the MARC
for the courts in the winter. The RAB recommended replacing both pools with one pool
that would include lap lanes, zero entry water features, and youth features. He stated an
additional fitness area could go where the current lap pool was. The aquatics project
was estimated to cost $6 million and the fitness expansion was estimated at $8 million.

Regarding the sports complex, the proposed project cost for the first phase was $9
million. A future phase would include a covered outdoor ice sheet, a bicycle pump track,
the expansion of the existing maintenance building, and field lights on the stadium field,
for a cost of $7 million. The total for all the projects would be $43 million.

Daenitz stated the City had funds to approve the full project list. He reviewed Council
was supportive of potentially expanding the uses of the Additional Resort Sales Tax
(ARST) revenue. He noted the project could also be funded with a General Obligation
(GO) bond that would go to voters for approval. He indicated the current GO bond debt
limit was 2% of the assessed value of the City and currently the City was at 1%. He did
calculations of the tax increase for median priced homes. A $35 million bond would
equal a tax increase of $148.88 per year for 20 years. A $50 million bond would equal a
tax increase of 212.69. For secondary homes, the tax increase would be $270.69 and
$386.70 respectively. He reviewed the history of the City’s GO debt service and stated
the first bond was for walkability and the rest were for open space.

and never have lived in Hideout (though at least one member of the Council has

decades ago as a private tennis club. Park City bought it, and in 2010 did a great job of

it is being run in 2023 as if it were still a private tennis club. PB players are afforded little
opportunity to book courts in the bubble. We not only get very few hours, but also only
the worst hours – when it opens at dawn or the evening hours before it closes. PB
players are never able to book a bubble court during “prime time”. Not ever. Mr. Fisher
has misled the City Council by providing incomplete information and offering
indefensible rationalizations for this. He has said repeatedly that tennis brings in far
more revenue, and thus should get the most hours. But as I have told the Council
before, if you do a deeper dive, you will see:
•       Tennis court reservation revenue dropped by over $28K last year
•       Growth in tennis revenue was attributable to lessons, not court bookings
•       PB court reservation revenue rose by over $22K last year (despite the paucity of
hours and the worst possible times).
•       PB total revenue is up over 400% in the last two years
•       Tennis does not get more hours because it brings in more money; it brings in
more money because it gets far more – and better- hours

Mr. Fisher and RAB liaison Max Doilney have offered as a second rationale that the
MARC is a “purpose built” facility, and that its primary purpose was and should continue
to be tennis. I previously shared an Op Ed I wrote for the Park City Record in which that
argument was dismantled. Further, I ask the Council to consider if the use of a public
facility in 2023 should be determined by the purpose for which it was built – as a private
tennis club – decades ago. Finally, I ask whether a Council that meets in a repurposed
school should cling to the absurd notion that a facility’s use should be dictated by the
purpose for which it was first built. I have repeatedly asked this body to look at how
many times bubble courts go unbooked. We get e-mails from Cole Johnson several
times a week notifying us that the MARC has opened courts for PB the next day. Bear
in mind that we only get these e-mails if every court is open. If two people are playing
singles on one bubble court, 16 people are blocked from playing PB. Is this the best use
of public courts? How much money is the MARC losing? In his Nov 3, 2022 Report to
the City Council, Ken Fisher identified 3 things the MARC was doing “to accommodate
the growing demand by the pickleball community for access to indoor tennis courts”.
Let’s take a closer look. Number 1 on Ken’s list was giving PB players more hours for
drop in play in the gym. Clearly more hours in the gym does not give PB players greater
access to the indoor tennis courts. It is completely unresponsive. Number 2 on Ken’s list
was releasing unused tennis courts at 11 AM the day before rather than 11 AM the
same day. This is completely misleading and entirely unacceptable. First, the MARC
only releases bubble courts if all three are empty. Again, remember that if two people
are playing singles tennis on one court, the MARC does not release the other two
courts. They sit unused. Second, tennis players have had one full week to book bubble
courts; PB players get one day. This is blatantly unfair. Number 3 on Ken’s list was
ending the right of PB players to reserve courts from 7-10 PM on Tuesday and
Thursday. He instead dedicated those hours to “open play” and allowed up to 48 people
to sign up, even though only 24 can play at a time. Taking away court reservation hours
and converting them to “open play” is quite literally robbing Peter to pay Paul. PB
players got no added hours. Indeed, In my old days as a consumer lawyer, we would
have referred to this as a scam – double booking the same space and charging twice
for something half the buyers are unable to use. Attached is a picture taken on
Tuesday, Jan. 24. That evening at least 35 people paid $10 each to play “open
pickleball”. There were always 11 people shut out at any given time, waiting for their
“opportunity” to play. This happens regularly. Every time a game ends, 4 players walk
off their court, interrupting the play of every court they must cross to put their paddles in
the slots awaiting their turn. Then 4 other players walk across the court, again
interrupting play as they head to the open court. This happens continuously. Because
the number of people and the distribution of skill levels is random, the games are almost
always worse. You invariably are forced to either “play up” (play with people better than
you) or “play down” (play with people worse than you). Either way it ruins the game. The
PB players are charged more money ($10 instead of $8.50), they play fewer games
(because of all the time they must sit out) and the games are of lesser quality /
enjoyment. And it takes 3 hours instead of 2. Ken often says he does not want to
“degrade the tennis experience” but seems to be perfectly happy to completely degrade
the PB experience on Tuesday and Thursday nights. Many of us have stopped going.
And over 120 members of the PCPB Club have joined the private club in Sandy. I
wonder why the MARC did not institute “open play” for tennis players…could it be
because they (quite rightly) would never stand for it? This is how Ken Fisher has
“accommodated the growing demand by the pickleball community for access to indoor
tennis courts”. He has done nothing of the sort. Your own survey showed demand for
PB courts (26.5%) nearly double the next two items respondents listed – year round
trails (13.8%) and cross country ski trails (13.5%). You clearly recognize the huge
demand for indoor PB courts, as it has prompted a significant investment in the Quinns
Junction project. And yet the MARC continues to give PB players even fewer
opportunities to book a bubble court than we had last year – all while tennis courts often
sit empty. It’s time to run the MARC like a public municipal and recreational facility – not
like the private tennis club it used to be. You can take action NOW to remedy the
injustice while we await construction of new facilities. The aspirational goal of new
dedicated indoor PB courts in 3 or 4 years is no excuse for doing nothing today. PB
deserves parity, now. Here are some immediate steps that the Council can take
tomorrow:
•        Give PB players an opportunity to book bubble courts during “prime time” at least
4 hours each day
•        Switch off peak hours so that they do not always go to PB and prime time always
goes to tennis
•        Implement a pilot program where both PB and tennis share the bubble on a first
come, first served basis
•        If tennis players do not book a court, allow PB players to book them 5 days out
(instead of 1 day)
Here is an example of how these principles could be applied each day of the week in
the bubble:

Monday: PB only from 4-10 PM (tennis gets the rest of the day)
Tuesday: PB only 6 AM to noon, tennis only 12 noon to 7 PM, PB open play 7-10 PM
Wednesday: PB only from 4-10 PM (tennis gets the rest of the day)
Thursday: PB only 6-10 AM, tennis 10 AM-4 PM, mixed use 4 PM to 10 PM
Friday: PB only 4-10 PM (tennis gets the rest of the day)
Saturday: mixed use all day
Sunday: tennis 7 AM to 1 PM, PCPB Club play 1-5 PM, PB only 5-9 PM

If you were to adopt these types of changes, you’d please far more people, the MARC
would significantly increase its revenue, and you would better serve the MARC’s
mission. You would be running the MARC like the public facility it is, rather than the
private tennis club it was. Let me close with one final point. You are going to vote
tonight on whether to approve $7-9 million dollars for a dedicated PB indoor and
outdoor complex at Quinn’s Junction. If you believe that the exploding popularity of the
