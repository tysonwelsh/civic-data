2.0   Discussion: Stop Signs

2.1   Scott Cooley, City Engineer, provided the Council with an update on the Traffic
      Committee. He explained that the committee’s purpose in meeting each month to discuss
      issues was to try and help make everyone’s life easier, including the Council’s. Of course,
      sometimes that does not happen as when they removed the stop sign at Willow Springs and
      received feedback from the neighborhood that was not positive. Therefore, he wanted to
      outline to the Council how the Traffic Committee handles these issues so that they could
      receive feedback from the Council on how they could amend their process for handling
      issues moving forward.

      He explained that in the case of Willow Springs, the Traffic Committee had received
      multiple complaints about two stop signs. The Traffic Committee responded by analyzing
      the two locations over some time, including a warrant analysis. It was determined that one
      of the stop signs was not warranted. He added that in the past stop signs have been used
      in traffic situations that were not appropriate for their use. What happens when a stop sign
      is in place that isn’t warranted, generally when a major street intersects a minor street, the
      volumes of traffic are not the same causing the driver to expect that when on a major street
      they should not have to stop. Studies have shown that when there is a stop sign on a major
      street at the intersection of a minor street, drivers find the stop signs to be a nuisance
      because they come to a stop, but don’t have anyone to stop for. Therefore, they begin to
      ignore the stop sign. This was the situation with the four-way stop at the Willow Springs

                                          Page 2 of 22
      location on Vestry Road and Selina Lane. It was not enough that most people stopped, but
      the Traffic Committee had to consider if there was a large enough percentage of drivers
      failing to stop at that location.

      Mr. Cooley communicated that the Traffic Committee takes the complaints it receives very
      seriously. He reminded the Council that city staff, councilmembers, and police were
      receiving traffic-related complaints before the creation of the Traffic Committee, which
      acts as a centralized organization to address issues.

      He added that when the staff removes signs, they are careful in that process, too. They
      notify residents in the area and add signs to create a safer as opposed to a more hazardous
      situation. Despite this, there will inevitably be people who do not like the action. He said
      that the choices when complaints come in are different based on the findings at each
      location. What is positive is that they create a paper trail of what happened at each location
      to follow.

      He reiterated that he wanted the Council’s feedback on their process to ensure that they
      were moving forward in the right way.

2.2   Mr. Dobbins asked Mr. Cooley to explain where the criteria from the warrant study can be
      found.

      Mr. Cooley said that the criteria originate from the Federal Highways Administration who
      puts out the manual on uniform traffic control devices, the bible on traffic control that the
      City uses. He noted that the manual is fairly restrictive, leaving little leeway for
      interpretation.

      Mr. Dobbins added that it is common for councilmembers or residents to request a stop
      sign to slow traffic down or to serve as a traffic calming measure. Although that may seem
      intuitive that stop signs will slow people down, the staff’s dilemma is that if those signs
      are installed without doing the warrant study and there’s an accident, the attorneys of those
      involved go straight to investigating if the City had followed the American Association of
      State Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO) standards. If it was a random
      decision, the City is opening itself up to liability. The need for this formal mechanism to
      prevent City liability was what was difficult to communicate to residents.

2.3   Councilmember Roberts asked what elements are considered during the analysis.

2.4   Mr. Cooley said that the main thing considered is the traffic volumes of the streets. In the

      added that most of the signage is driven by driver expectation that is uniform across the
      nation. He said that drivers, in general, are behaving differently than they did 10 or 20
      years ago.

4.0   Action Item: Deer Ridge Drive Update and Improvement Plan.

4.1   Mr. Cooley provided some updates on Deer Ridge Drive. He said that there were a few
      points that he wanted to make.

      First, he talked about the City-Wide Road Maintenance Program. He explained how the
      program works. Generally, the staff receives a certain amount of funding from DNC Funds
      to do road maintenance work. By the time that money was stretched out to arterials and
      collector roads there tends to be not a lot of money left for residential roads aside from
      surface treatments. That had been the City’s program for a long time. He recognized that
      there was a shortfall in funding and that there was a need for residential roads to be
      reconstructed beyond a surface treatment.

      Because of the poor condition of Deer Ridge Drive, it was a residential road that would be
      at the top of the list for maintenance and reconstruction if the Traverse Ridge Special
      Service District (TRSSD) was included in the program. Mr. Cooley reiterated that the
      funding shortfall was for all residential roads, not just those in the TRSSD. He said that
      the staff knows that they need additional funding and therefore have been looking at less
      expensive reconstruction alternatives.

      One of these processes that the staff has researched over the past year was Full Depth
      Reclamation (FDR). They believe that it was a viable option for reconstruction of Deer
      Ridge Drive.

      Mr. Cooley took some time to describe the process. He began by stating that the process
      was nothing new and had been implemented by Salt Lake City for the past ten years or so.
      The staff had already hired Utah’s pavement expert to help the City analyze their processes
      and come up with a more cost-effective way to reconstruct residential roads. He suggested
      that the City begin to use FDR.



                                          Page 15 of 22
      Mr. Cooley noted that the City had already used this process on the 300 East project
      spanning from 11400 South to 11800 South that was completed this year and were using it
      on a project in SunCrest in the Oak Vista neighborhood. He said that if you drive it, it was
      a smooth road. He continued that there were many advantages to the process, one being
      that it served as a good bridge over bad soil, something that there was a lot of in Draper.
      He said that when the soil becomes saturated the clay becomes incredibly soft and was
      difficult to move construction equipment across the property. FDR, however, uses the
      moisture to the City’s benefit. The existing asphalt and base are pulverized and then added
      with a cement mixture. In a day or so, some additional prep work is done that becomes the
      structural foundation for the road. This foundation is much harder and stronger than if the
      road were built directly on the soil. He talked about the challenges of the other base and
      provided an example up in SunCrest where, as a result of the poor soil conditions, a grader
      sunk about two feet into the ground. After they got the grader out, they went through the
      FDR process and added the cement mixture and were able to bridge the soil.

      Examples like these make the staff feel as though the process was very viable, especially
      since it had been used by others for a long time. Also, it was significantly cheaper. Another
      major advantage was how quickly the process could be done. The 300 East project only
      took eight days from pulverizing to the completion of asphalt. He said that there was some
      traffic control, striping, and manholes that needed to be addressed, but the eight-day process
      would have taken at least two to three months through the normal process. Mr. Cooley
      continued that the city staff often see a lot of construction fatigue with residents and people
      who use the roadways by the end of the summer and the first part of fall.

      Mr. Cooley cited an example from earlier in the day at SunCrest where they were starting
      the asphalt process. He said that people were stopping on the roadway and getting out of
      their cars to thank inspectors for the job that they were doing. He said that the timeliness
      of the process and to receive praise from residents was unheard of.

4.2   Councilmember Roberts asked why the process had not been used in the past if it had been
      around for so long. Mr. Cooley said that it was not a widely used product yet. He said that
      there could be complications with the process, but that a lot of contractors were getting up
      to speed with the process. He said that there were not a lot of contractors that had
      experience with it. However, when you look into the data behind the process, it was a solid
      practice. He said that he would be happy to demonstrate the strength of the road base.

4.3   Mr. Cooley said that the estimated cost for the reconstruction of Deer Ridge Drive using
      FDR was a million dollars. This would include some drainage and water system
      improvements. This means that the reconstruction part would be $500,000-$600,000.
      Previously, the estimate for the reconstruction was $2.8 million demonstrating the degree
      of savings the City could expect. Mr. Cooley stated that previously the City was planning
      on installing curb and gutter, but the staff did not feel as though that was needed. The
      product does create a bridge on the existing soil. He said that before they implemented the
      process, they would do a geotechnical analysis on each road to come up with the
      appropriate section that needs to be installed based on the existing soils. He said that the



                                          Page 16 of 22
      staff was excited to be able to do the project for a million dollars and that he was confident
      that it would be a good road.

      Regarding how to come up with the million dollars, Mr. Cooley said that funding could
      come out of the proceeds of the future sale of the 10-acre property currently on the market
      at the end of Deer Ridge Drive. However, to start the design of the project now so that it
      could be completed in 2021, the staff needed some initial funding for the design. Therefore,
      if directed by the City Council, the staff will identify some initial funding sources to bring
      back to the Council for approval in September.

4.3   Councilmember Roberts asked how Mr. Cooley anticipated this would impact the cost of
      future roads. He wanted to know if it was something that the staff was excited about, as a

      Mr. Cooley said that the cost would typically be between 50-70% of what a normal
      reconstruction project would cost. He said that there was a larger gap on the Deer Ridge
      Drive project because the initial estimate included improvements that the staff decided not
      to install. However, they were intending to upgrade the drainage system.

      Councilmember F. Lowry asked if there were any unknowns about the drainage system

      an appropriate design. He said that he felt that more contractors were coming on board

      everything there. Councilmember F. Lowry asked if the process worked on all land
