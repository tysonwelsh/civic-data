     2.5. Discussion of a draft ordinance to regulate metal storage containers as accessory
          structures in Herriman - Michael Maloy, City Planner
City Planner Maloy outlined they had met with the Planning Commission several times to narrow down the
ordinance. He said the Planning Commission recommended approval, 5-0. Prices for shipping containers had
inflated, and it was now about $4,500 for a 20-foot container, and up to $10,000 for a 40-foot container. He
noted the pricing varied based on condition, number of uses, and modifications. He said people had gotten
very creative with shipping containers, and had valuable use in construction. He explained the key issue with
shipping containers was what the roof level requirements should be if the container was to be used for something
like a “she-shed” or “man-cave”.

City Planner Maloy went over what the ordinance from the Planning Commission had outlined. He said metal
shipping containers could still be used, but now a building permit would be required for permitted use. He
reported he had checked with their building official, who had confirmed it was best practice to have some kind
of review and approval for permanent use. He clarified temporary use was defined as six months or less, and
this did not require a permit. He continued security locks would be required, so the doors could not be opened,
and if the container was visible from the street, the outside paint color needed to be compatible with the main
building on the property. He said once completed, all economic value of purchasing a shipping container would
be lost, so Planning Commission had recommended to not require the same compatibility standards for the roof
of the container. It was clarified these standards only applied in residential zones, and the regulations were
different in other zones. City Planner Maloy said the setback standards for the shipping containers would the
same as the setback guidelines for accessory units, but Planning Commission had decided the shipping containers
would have to be smaller than what an accessory building or a kit could be, as they felt there was more of an
impact on the neighborhood from a shipping container than an accessory structure. He continued with the
requirement of a conditional use permit, and a building permit would be needed for a purely agricultural use.
He said the draft ordinance outlined the shipping container could be used for a living space or as storage, and
he said “living space” was defined as a structure with utilities, such as water, electric, or gas hookups. The utility
hookups were currently allowed by the City in accessory buildings. Mayor Watts added a living space also needed
to have a clear form of egress, and he did not want a scenario in which someone could get trapped inside a
shipping container. He wanted to specifically add in the ordinance there had to be a clear way to get out of a
shipping container.

Councilmember Shields asked what the requirements to get a building permit for a shipping container. City
Planner Maloy responded the site would have to be level, and met health safety codes, otherwise it would follow
the same requirements for a standard building including having a clear form of egress. Mayor Watts reiterated
his satisfaction with the regulations of the ordinance, as long as a specification about egress from the containers
was clarified in the final draft.

Councilmember Smith pointed out in the “placements and standards” section of the draft, it said no more than
two shipping containers would be allowed on less than two acres, and asked for the rational behand the proposed
standard. City Planner Maloy said staff had followed the accessory structure standards, but Planning Commission
felt shipping containers had more of a potential impact on the surrounding property values, so they chose to
minimize the amount of shipping containers permitted. Mayor Watts suggested a change the rule to no more
October 13, 2021 – City Council Meeting Minutes                                               Page 9 of 15
