2.1 Discussion of Residential Treatment Facilities and Reasonable Accommodations
Code. (Presenter, Fred Donaldson).
City Attorney, Fred Donaldson, presented a potential change in the Bluffdale City Code for
discussion. He explained that the City Code currently limits the number of unrelated persons who
can reside together to four. A change was previously made to allow up to five unrelated handicapped
or disabled persons to live together. Disabled persons are referred to as “handicapped” in the Code
because that is the terminology used in the Fair Housing Act.
After the Code change, a request was made to allow 16 unrelated disabled persons to live together in
a dwelling. At that time, the Hearing Officer approved 12 of the requested 16 beds. The same
applicant recently requested 24 beds and that application was denied. In the denial,, the Hearing
Officer stated that, based on expert testimony, the appropriate maximum number is eight.
Mr. Donaldson noted that the denial of the 24-bed request has been appealed to the District Court and
is now pending litigation.

The question before the City Council is whether to amend City Code to allow up to eight disabled
individuals to live together in a single residence. Reasonable accommodation would still need to be
requested for more than that number. The City Attorney suggests that the number be increased to
eight, as the Hearing Officer held that number to be reasonably and medically necessary.
Council Member Crockett asked if facts such as the number of available bedrooms and parking would
be taken into consideration. Mr. Donaldson confirmed that those issues are addressed in the
Reasonable Accommodation Code and that part of the Code would not be altered.
The Hearing Officer considers the following criteria in determining Reasonable Accommodation:
e Whether the housing which is the subject of the reasonable accommodation will be used by
one or more persons with a handicap or disability.
e Whether the accommodation is necessary to afford such persons an equal opportunity to use
and enjoy the dwelling.
e Whether it's reasonable.
e Whether it imposes an undue financial or administrative burden on the City.
e Whether the request would require a fundamental alteration in the nature of a City program,
including land use zoning.
e Whether it would result in a fundamental alteration to the residential character of the
neighborhood.
e Whether the property would be used by persons that would constitute a direct threat to the
health or safety of other persons.
Mr. Donaldson noted that is appropriate to consider the impacts of traffic. In the previously decided
cases, the individuals did not own vehicles, so there was no increase in traffic except on certain
occasions like graduations or pickup days. The facility would have to comply with Building Code
and occupancy requirements.
Council Member Lord asked how this differs from Renaissance Ranch zoning, and if it would allow
a similar facility to be opened in the future. Renaissance Ranch has more than eight beds.

2.2 Discussion of City Council Districts and Related Code Changes. (Presenter Fred
Donaldson and Bruce Kartchner).
Mr. Kartchner presented the Staff Report and stated that during his eight-year tenure on the City
Council and in the eight years since, at various times the Council has discussed the possibility of
electing Council Members by district instead of at-large. Currently, all five City Council members
and the Mayor are at-large positions. The Staff Report details the necessary Code changes to create
voting districts, as well as sample district maps.
Mr. Donaldson covered the legal constraints related to geographic redistricting, which include:
e Existing voting precincts remain intact and cannot be split.
e Districts must be as equal in population as possible without splitting precincts.
e Districts must be reasonably geographically compact and contiguous.
In one possible scenario, four City Council Members would be elected from districts and the Mayor
and one City Council Member would be at large. Each election cycle would have two districted
Council positions and one at-large election. Every four years, the at-large position would be the
Mayor. The other option is to have five districts and no at-large Council Member.

Precincts are based on the number of voters and districts are based on total population. In response
to a question from Council Member Lord, Mr. Kartchner confirmed that population numbers are
determined by the U.S. Census. Each district should have approximately the same population, but
the precinct requirement can make that challenging to achieve.
Pros and cons that have been discussed include certain areas being under or over-represented.
Currently, City Council Members are responsible for representing all citizens but there are times when
citizens do not necessarily feel represented because all City Council Members are from a certain part
of the City. At one point when Mr. Kartchner served on the City Council, three members lived west
of 2700 West. Mr. Donaldson added that cities set up districts based on their desires and goals. Some
neighboring cities have districts, and some have only at-large seats.
Council Member Crockett was in favor of creating districts. She believes that four districted seats
and one at-large member give more opportunity for different communities to be represented. She
noted that four of the sitting Council members live west of Redwood Road. If each area is represented,
the Council Member who represents an area can concentrate on their district, enabling citizens to feel
more represented and heard. She was concerned about the possibility of candidates running
unopposed.
Mr. Kartchner mentioned that Mayor Derk Timothy ran unopposed for two of his three terms. He
stated that if residents are comfortable with the City's leadership, they might not want to run. It's also
possible that a particular district might not have a lot of politically active people who are willing to
take the time to stump for votes or do the job after they are elected.
Mr. Donaldson added that this is a disadvantage of having districts. Each district must find a candidate
who is willing to serve, which can be challenging at times. There are times when people run
unopposed because things are going well but there are likely also times when they are unopposed
because no one else is interested in running. In a conversation with him earlier in the week, City
Manager, Mark Reid, mentioned that if there are four districts and one at-large member, everyone
will vote in every election for either the mayor or at-large seat, so that is a potential advantage.

2.3 Legislative Updates from 2024 Session (Presenter Fred Donaldson).
Mr. Donaldson provided an overview of updates from the 2024 Legislative Session. There were 591
bills passed during the last session, which is a state record. The State Legislature passed a $29 billion
budget. The presentation included a list of the bills that are relevant to the City, but he would only be
highlighting a few of the more relevant bills. The Council Members were welcome to contact him
for more details on individual bills. They are also available on the legislative website.
HB-36 Open and Public Meetings Act Amendments. A change was made to the definition of the term
"meeting." Language related to chance and social gatherings was repealed. A gathering is not a

meeting unless it is convened by an official with the authority to convene a meeting. If a group of
Council Members is at a ribbon cutting or social event, that is not a meeting.
SB-91 Local Government Officers Compensation Amendment. Specific notice and a hearing are now
required if a final budget amendment will increase the compensation of certain officers, including the
City Manager, City Attorney, and Department Heads. This does not apply to elected officials. The
hearing can be held on the same night the Council considers the budget amendment, but it has to be a
separately noticed public hearing to discuss increases in compensation for these specific officers.
HB-256 Military Compatible Land Use Amendments. Previously, cities were required to develop a
compatible use plan if the City was within a certain distance of Camp Williams or other military
installations. That continues to be a requirement, but the City is now also required to consider it when
making decisions on land use applications.
HB-289 Property Rights Ombudsman Amendment. If a judge finds that a city has not followed an
appealed advisory opinion, the city may be responsible for attorney fees. If the city knowingly and
intentionally violates the law contrary to an advisory opinion, there are additional penalties.
Land Use Planning Amendments. There were a number of changes related to development
agreements, including what they can and cannot require and what they can contain, as well as permits
related to walk-out porches, setbacks, sidewalk phasing, and the timing of subdivision reviews.
HB-502 Critical Infrastructure and Mining. There was an effort to define quarries and mining as
critical infrastructure and to develop further protections for those activities. Instead of implementing
those protections, a bill was passed to study it.
SB-13 Education Entity Amendments. This amendment is relevant to Bluffdale as there is a pending
application to open a micro-school. Micro schools are similar to charter schools. They are often
home-based, but can also be located in a commercial zone. The current application will be in a
residence. Staff was working to update the City Code to make it consistent with State law related to
micro-schools.

SB-185 Residential Building Inspection Amendments. State law requires that building inspections
be performed within three days of the request. Some cities were consistently missing that deadline.
This bill implements a requirement to have a list of other inspectors that can be used if necessary.
Cities are working together to create agreements and identify a list of inspectors they can point
developers to when necessary to meet the three-day requirement.
HB-488 Transportation Funding Modifications. This bill creates an infrastructure bank. Bluffdale is
working to secure funding for significant improvements to 14600 South, where the old trestle and
single-lane bridge are located. The current spend is $30 million. This bill provides for the creation
of the County of the First Class Highway Projects Fund Infrastructure Bank Fund that will lend out
money. As those loans are repaid, 30% of the proceeds will come to Bluffdale for the project. Mayor
Hall added that that will only occur if Salt Lake County passes the fifth of the fifth. That has not
happened, so the City may need to consider other options.
HB-2 New Fiscal Year Supplemental Appropriations Act. This includes a one-time appropriation of
$15 million to the Transportation Investment Fund for the construction of a FrontRunner stop in
Bluffdale. That will result in some changes related to planning around the station. Mr. Reid added |
that the City will need to comply with certain requirements related to the station. Usually, cities
campaign for a FrontRunner stop, and by doing so they commit to making certain changes and agree
to certain terms. Bluffdale did not campaign for the FrontRunner stop, but it does have to meet the
requirements.
Mr. Kartchner asked the Council to review the information on SB-86, Local Government Bond
Amendments. The City is considering a Lease Revenue Bond for the Public Works Building, so an
updated resolution will likely be required in order to comply with the new law. A public hearing will
also be required. In response to a question from Council Member Lord, Mr. Kartchner confirmed that
the new law affects the City's ability to issue bonds. A hearing is required prior to issuing bonds. The
requirement is the result of a situation where a General Obligation Bond was proposed, citizens voted
it down, and then the organization switched to a Lease Revenue Bond. To avoid similar situations in
the future, all Lease Revenue Bonds will now require a public hearing.
