   2.1. Developer update relating to the proposed development of the Game Point
        property – Tami Moody, Assistant City Manager
Assistant City Manager Moody turned the time over to the Game Pointe Development Team
to discuss their plans moving forward. Game Point Development Representative Aaron
Osmond expressed he was looking forward to sharing what he saw as a more refined plan
which had been designed to attract more entertainment-oriented tenants. He hoped to get
general approval from the Council on the general proposal.

Mr. Osmond shared the new proposed site use plan and indicated they had incorporated
some elements which had been suggested by the Council in a previous meeting. He
highlighted the revised plans which outlined a two-story office and retail solution. He said
there were three retail pad areas along Miller Crossing and expanded on the restaurant and
dining pad location while focusing on making it more prominent. He expressed the desire to
alignment with City Code for parking and asked the Council for feedback on the design.

Councilmember Shields liked the efforts the developers had made to incorporate previous
recommendations from the Council. He also liked the flexibility of the building as it would
allow for many different kinds of tenants. He also expressed that he liked the two-story

gone back and forth with the idea of a 40,000 square foot entertainment building and said

possible. Mr. Osmond discussed there were many examples of big entertainment areas
which came in and subsequently disappeared and felt it left a lot of empty space which was
hard to fill. Mr. Osmond acknowledged Herriman’s desire for an entertainment area but
highlighted many retail and office tenants now who were ready to move in, whereas
developing an entertainment area could take some time. He explained it was best to start
with other tenants who could act as an anchor for an entertainment tenant.

   2.2. Discussion regarding a city-wide fiber-optic broadband open-access internet
        network – Blake Thomas, Community Development Director
Community Development Director Thomas explained a task force had been assembled to
look into options for broadband internet, and the task force explored many options and
submitted a report to the Council. He reported the team had worked with many industry
professionals to solicit several proposals for a network provider partner. He received
proposals back from Strata, Entry Point, Coherence Networks, and Google Fiber. The group
determined Strata was the best option for what the City wanted to accomplish. He asked the
Council for direction on how to move forward with Strata.

Councilmember Henderson asked how many of the proposals would allow the City to own
the infrastructure. Councilmember Shields replied all proposals received allowed the City to
own the infrastructure. Director Thomas said financials needed to be considered and he said
they would need to budget at the end of the negotiations to determine how much subscribers
would need to be charged. He said Strata’s proposal claimed to have all the City up and
running in three years. He explained a cost analysis was not completed currently and would
start to determine costs as the team working through the project. He also highlighted the
possibility of entering an Interlocal Agreement.

Councilmember Shields expressed concern Strata only had about 90,000 subscribers
currently and wondered if the firm could support the entire community. Director Thomas
thought the firm would be able to scale up operations well but thought challenges would
come up during construction due to the company running more in-house. Director Thomas
said it would be easier for them to add new subscribers as they moved along with the
November 9, 2022 – City Council Minutes                                        Page 5 of 19

installation, rather than getting all new customers set up at once.

Councilmember Henderson asked about the key points of the contract and wanted to see
off-ramps provided in the contract, in addition to a full financial analysis not only of
construction costs, but ongoing operational costs as well. Councilmember Shields agreed it
was important to see the financial analysis and thought it was crucial to get all the answers
needed before they made any formal decision.

The Council discussed the process moving forward and it was determined a draft agreement
would be presented to the Council at a future meeting. It was also suggested to solicit public
comment as well as investigate the possibility of entering the Interlocal Agreement.

Councilmember Henderson brought up the topic of financing the infrastructure.
Councilmember Shields emphasized he would not put the budget at risk for broadband
infrastructure as it was not reasonable and would not jeopardize the taxpayers in this
process. Councilmember Shields opined the possibility to obtain financing in the market
which would be able to stand on its own or reconsidered entirely. Councilmember Hodges
asked if grants were available; however, staff observed the unrealistic funding option grants
provided.

Councilmember Hodges asked if it would be a better take rate as an opt-out. Councilmember
Shields discussed the results of the study in which about 70-percent of residents expressed
interest in broadband infrastructure. He said even with most of the City opting-out the
project could still function. He said this would be successful in Herriman and reiterated this
would be a City-owned utility. Councilmember Hodges asked about competition, and
Director Thomas said the firm would have a non-exclusive franchise agreement and
encouraged competition to reduce rates for the consumer. Councilmember Shields talked
about the drawback of a monthly payment but pointed out most residents were already
paying for internet and this option could provide a savings. He thought the net benefit to the
residents would be significant regardless of what option was selected. City Attorney Todd
Sheeran said there were many things which could save the City money, not just regarding
the internet. The Council agreed.

      2.4 Update to Title 7 Chapter 8 – Parks, Park Facilities, Trails and Open Space -
Anthony Teuscher, Deputy Director of Parks, Recreation and Events

Deputy Director of Parks, Recreation and Events Anthony Teuscher offered updates of park
ordinances. He reported they had been in the process of reviewing chapter eight of title
seven looking for inconsistencies and deficiencies. He said currently, park hours of
operation were all over the board, which was difficult for enforcement, and wanted to make
them consistent. He proposed to make all their parks and park facilities open at 7:00 AM and
close at 10:00 PM. He said they did not have significant issues with people being in the park
before 7:00 AM. Councilmember Ohrn liked this aligned with the noise ordinance.
November 9, 2022 – City Council Minutes                                           Page 8 of 19

Deputy Director Teuscher added they were in the process of putting automatic locks on all
the restrooms, so staff didn’t have to physically go out to the park daily solely to unlock
doors. The Council discussed they did not have an issue with people being in the park early,
but people would understand they could not get into the amenities or dog park until 7:00 AM
when everything was open.

Deputy Director Teuscher reported they had also cleaned up some phrasing in the ordinance
which discussed what was prohibited but clarified no changes were made to the content of
the ordinance. He asked the Council if they wished to prohibit private instruction or group
classes within the parks, for example a for-profit business holding a private class. Assistant
City Manager Thomas recalled going to Rosecrest Pavilion and seeing a karate class. She
thought it would be okay if they wanted to rent the space, but she did not think groups should
be allowed to monopolize a space without paying a fee. Councilmember Henderson agreed
groups should have to reserve the space and pay a fee.

Deputy Director Teuscher also brought up overnight parking was becoming more of an issue.
He asked the Council if they wanted to clearly prohibit overnight parking. He also wished to
prohibit motorized vehicles on all primitive trails, except for motorized wheelchairs or class
one e-bikes. He elaborated there were three classes of e-bikes and thought it would be
appropriate to allow class one e-bikes since they did not have a throttle and the maximum
speed was only 20 miles per hour. He said class one e-bikes still needed to be pedaled,
whereas class two and three did not need to be pedaled and had the capacity to go much
faster. He said class two and three e-bikes could cause damage to the trails. He reported
the Trails Committee was in favor of these changes. Councilmember Shields asked how this
would be enforced if a bike had different modes which took it from a class one designation
to a class two. Councilmember Ohrn thought they needed to have a hard line about not
allowing e-bikes with a throttle. Deputy Director Teuscher thought from an enforcement
point of view, not allow those bikes on a trail. He added this was in line with what other
cities had done, including Provo, Orem, and Sandy.

Deputy Director Teuscher next discussed primitive trails. He wanted to include language in
the Code which prohibited the building and modification of any trails. He also wanted to
include language which discouraged trail use when excessive mud was present, and require
all hikers to use good trail etiquette, stay on the designated trail, and yield when appropriate.
Councilmember Ohrn thought ‘muddy’ was subjective and did not think anyone would
functionally be deterred if there was signage which discouraged hiking in the mud.

Councilmember Ohrn indicated section 7-8-7 which discussed the prohibition of e-collars.
She thought this was unfair since some dogs were very well trained to use an e-collar, and
she did not want to penalize people who had successfully trained their dog. She proposed a
system in which people could demonstrate to Animal Services their dog could perform well
with an e-collar, and if they could do so, they could receive a certificate which would allow
them to take their dogs on the trail with an e-collar. She did not think this would be an undue
November 9, 2022 – City Council Minutes                                        Page 9 of 19

burden on Animal Services as she doubted many people would come in to get their dog
certified and pointed out many dogs would not be able to pass a certification. She thought

Deputy Director Teuscher spoke about the parking issue at Black Ridge Reservoir and
offered an overview of some options previously discussed. He shared some potential plans
for parking expansion. He said they could push the south boundary out and add 54 new
parking stalls along the inside portion. He estimated it would cost about $60,000. He said
another option would be to add an additional parking lot near the dam and estimated they

enforcement staff was necessary. He explained how the license plate recognition software

10. Discussion and Action Items
    10.1. Discussion and Consideration of an amendment to the Environmental Overlay
          Zone, Chapter 10-15A-4: Action Levels for Non-Residential Property – Blake
          Thomas, Community Development Director
Community Development Director Thomas stated the EPA had issued a record of decision
in 2001 when Herriman was a new City. He said the EPA had split up the impacted area into
three operable units, and two of those areas were under the jurisdiction of the EPA and the
County. He stated the last operable unit was managed by Herriman, and they had been
allowed to set the allowable levels of contaminant in the soil. He discussed a study completed
by the University of Utah which studied the bioavailability of the contaminated site and said
the City had used the figures from that study in addition to the EPA’s recommendations to
determine the range of action levels allowed in non-residential areas. He added for the
residential areas, the City had gone with the EPA’s standard at the time. He noted the EPA’s
current level for residential areas was 1,200 parts per million for lead action level, but they
might reduce that number to an even lower standard.

Director Thomas reported at some point, the Herriman City Code had been updated to allow
up to 5,000 parts per million of lead in non-residential areas. He said the EPA’s highest
recommendation was up to 4,000 parts per million in commercial and industrial areas and
reported Herriman had been approached by the State and the EPA multiple times to have the
standard lowered. He stated the EPA had requested Herriman make an adjustment to the
level of contaminant and reduce it to 4,000 parts per million for non-residential uses. He
added the Planning Commission had further suggested arsenic levels be reduced to 300 parts
per million in non-residential areas.

Director Thomas expressed this was the first step in a long process, and their overall
intention was to do a revamp of the entire environmental overlay zone. He said there were
many things they wanted to discuss further, especially in areas like Olympia Hills where there
was contaminated soil. He discussed many parts of the Code were very basic right now and
November 9, 2022 – City Council Minutes                                        Page 17 of 19

they wanted to have more detail.

Councilmember Shields asked why they did not want to reduce the level to 1,200 parts per
million at this time, and Director Thomas said it had not yet been requested by the EPA and
opined they may request the change in the future. Councilmember Ohrn worried they might
not make the change to 1,200 in time, since Olympia was already in development and moving
soil. Director Thomas explained the areas of Olympia currently being developed did not have
contaminated soil. The Council thought it would be best to preemptively make the change
to 1,200 parts per million, since it would be required of them in the future anyway. Director
Thomas expressed if the Council wished to make the change to 1,200 parts per million, there
would be no pushback from staff, and agreed they would likely have to reduce the standard
to 1,200 parts per million in the future.

Director Thomas also briefly discussed how the numbers for the range had been determined
by the University of Utah study. He also clarified within the operable unit, only about 2-
percent had lead levels of 4,000 parts per million. He outlined the scientific process for how
the soil samples were taken and evaluated to determine if it met the standard. He said some
recent developments had kept the soil on-site and stockpiled it, and he expressed this was
allowed per the EPA requirements, but the Council and staff would need to come up with a
list of requirements for those developments as to how to manage the soil.

Director Thomas said this text amendment would satisfy the EPA’s request and reported he
had expressed to the EPA their intent as a City to work on revamping the entire ordinance.

Councilmember Henderson expressed concern about the residential areas and thought there
should be a different threshold for residential zones which bordered the contaminated areas.
He felt they should go off the low end of the range. Director Thomas pointed out the ranges
did not make sense, since they had been determined based on site-specific data.
Councilmember Henderson clarified his main concern were the areas bordering the operable
units and asked if there was room for them to examine this with the overlay zone in which
they could make changes for specific sites. Director Thomas replied they were looking at the
overlay zone and did not think any of them had the expertise to determine which sites should
have different thresholds. Director Thomas thought they would need to bring in consultants
for the project.

Director Thomas recalled this had originally been brought to the Planning Commission in the
previous spring, and not much had come of it at that time. He recalled more details of the
history of their relationship with the EPA regarding the operable units and discussed the
needs of Herriman had changed a lot since the time the EPA had issued their original record
of decision. He pointed out Herriman used to be a farming community, but now they had
distinct commercial, industrial, and residential areas which all had different thresholds for
contaminants in the soil. He also reviewed some of the main changes they needed to make
in the ordinance. Councilmember Henderson asked for clarification on the changes, and
