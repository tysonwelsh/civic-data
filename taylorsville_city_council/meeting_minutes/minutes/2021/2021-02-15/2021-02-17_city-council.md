           3.3         Legislative Updates – John Hiskey

      It was currently the fifth week of the legislative session and John Hiskey pointed out that as
with everything else, COVID-19 had an impact on the way legislators related to their constituents
and on the entire legislative process.

Because of the major tax changes that were anticipated for 2020 which were delayed due to
COVID, there was a restriction placed on the number of “priority” bills a legislator’s staff could
handle. Many more bills were likely to be introduced in the final two and one-half weeks of the
session.

Mr. Hiskey appreciated how active Mayor Overson was in the Utah League of Cities and Towns’
Legislative Policy Committee. The committee applied a particular test to all potential bills:
   1. Does the bill respect the role of local government?
   2. Does the bill respect the individuality of each city/town or is it “one size fits all?”
   3. Will the bill result in an unfunded mandate?

HB82 was Rep. Ward’s well-intentioned attempt to address the housing crisis but because he had
never worked in local government, the proposed bill did not acknowledge the unique challenges
cities and towns faced. House leadership was in favor of the bill but Rep. Dunnigan spoke up and
explained some of the challenges to Rep. Ward. There was now a 3rd Substitute of the bill which
suggested some progress was being made.

Mr. Hiskey pointed out that outdoor advertising (billboards) was another very challenging area
legislatively. One size did not fit all, despite what was currently under consideration in SB144.
The bill did not differentiate between on premise and off premise advertising, could impact
neighborhoods in many cases and in short, would take away any ability for the city to regulate
outdoor advertising.

Mr. Hiskey then outlined the gist of multiple law enforcement bills currently pending at the
legislative session.

HB244 involving first class county road funds was being sponsored by Senator Harper and Rep.
Dunnigan and would, if adopted, provide some funding for all cities within counties of the first
class. This bill was drafted because when similar funds were previously distributed, only eight
municipalities in Salt Lake County received any.

Finally, Senator Harper had made a request for ongoing appropriations for the Jordan River
Parkway. Mr. Hiskey reminded the Council that everything taking place during the legislative
session remained very fluid, and things could change quite quickly.

      Jay Ziolkowski explained that the transition team was making great strides both with the
charter and with their timeline. In fact, they were ahead of schedule in a couple of areas. It was
his hope that evening to address three specific topics: the Strategic Plan, the Salary/Pay Step Plan,
and Policy Review.

Proposed Strategic Plan
Mr. Ziolkowski explained that the strategic plan for the Taylorsville Police Department was
intended to be a road map for the department and the city administration over the next three years.
Within the plan were vision, mission and value statements that would guide police administration
through everything from personnel matters to many action items necessary to move the police
department forward. Each of those statements as well as various other topics were all created
when the community team was brought together in the Fall. The team was made up of individuals
with a variety of ethnicities, religions, backgrounds, qualifications, etc. They provided input
regarding what they felt should be the priorities in creating the new police department. In total,
there were sixty data points collected which led to the creation of the vision and mission
statements. He then referred to specific pages of the Strategic Plan. (Although the Strategic Plan
was not included in the agenda packet, a copy of the plan in draft form will be retained with the
permanent packet since it was referenced in the meeting.)

The department’s vision statement was “Preserving Community Rights and Responsibilities.”

The mission statement was “In partnership with our community leaders and citizens, it is the
mission of the Taylorsville Police Department to provide high quality law enforcement services,
thus preserving and protecting the people as well as the diverse and unique culture that comes from
living in Taylorsville City. This is accomplished through adherence to best practices and
department values.” The five chosen values were Transparency, Respect, Innovation, Connection
and Collaboration.

Laid out in the Strategic Plan were five topics identified in the data points that came out of the
community meeting. They were:
   • Best practices
   • Administrative support and collaboration
   • Personnel investment
   • Capital planning
   • Community outreach and engagement

Each topic had a goal statement as well as several intended outcomes. Over the next three years,
police administration would work with city administration to identify how best to meet each goal
statement by setting SMART objectives (Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant and Time-
bound). These activities would be critical in determining what ‘good’ looked like.

The information in the Strategic Plan would be shared with the community team which would
again be meeting the following week. Mr. Ziolkowski wished for feedback from the council
although there was not a formal resolution requesting their approval.

Proposed Step/Pay Plan
Mr. Ziolkowski reminded the council that they had received an electronic copy of the proposed
Step/Pay Plan earlier in the day and explained that traditionally, police and fire personnel worked
in step plans with merit increases that were based on performance, service time and grade. The
transition team was proposing a nine-year step plan that was designed to maximize officer
recruitment and retention. The proposed plan would make Taylorsville officers, once they hit the
seven to nine-year mark, the second highest paid out of 17 different police departments surveyed.
Sergeants would be fourth place and lieutenants would be fifth. Mr. Ziolkowski stressed that it
was critical to pay a competitive wage to attract the most qualified and talented officers and to
keep them actively engaged with the community. The plan was designed to incentivize officers to
remain with Taylorsville Police Department long-term.

Councilmember Armstrong asked if the pay plan came with updated budget projections. He also
wished to ensure that the City would receive the vehicles and equipment that Taylorsville UPD
precinct officers were currently using. Mr. Ziolkowski responded that he projected the budget at
roughly $10.8 million, and he understood the current target to be $10.9 million. He stressed the
numbers were still tentative. He also pointed out that Unified Police was being very gracious and
there was no concern that the City would receive the oldest, most used assets. Additionally, UPD’s
precinct chief had taken steps to speed up the timeline of the switch from Spillman to Versaterm
(the computer aided dispatch and electronic ticketing system) so that it would be ready to go for
the city’s new police department on July 1.

Councilmember Barbieri inquired about the average time a police officer typically worked for a
department. Mr. Ziolkowski deferred to Chief Brady Cottam who responded that generally, if an
officer stayed past the 5 to 10-year mark, he/she would stay until retirement. The national average
for a career in law enforcement was 33 years.

Mr. Ziolkowski asked if the council had any concerns with the proposed pay/step plan being shared
with potential applicants. Councilmember Harker asked when the front-line officers would be
hired for the new department. Mr. Ziolkowski responded that recruitment would begin in March
with selection to take place in April. He also mentioned that the city was already receiving
inquiries from interested applicants.

7.         OTHER MATTERS

           7.1         Resolution No. 21-07 – A resolution of the City of Taylorsville supporting the
                       Western Growth Coalition’s 2021 Legislative Transportation Priorities –
                       Council Chair Cochran

      Chair Cochran explained that he represented Taylorsville on the Western Growth Coalition
which also included member entities throughout the west side of the Salt Lake valley. The WGC
had been working for the last year or 1.5 years on identifying the priorities of these cities and the
overwhelming top priority appeared to be transportation. He had been asked to present the
resolution to the city council seeking their support so that the Coalition could then take the
resolution to the legislature.

Councilmember Armstrong asked what the Coalition’s stance was on heavy rail and rapid transit.
Chair Cochran responded that it was primarily focused on busses as opposed to rail.
Councilmember Armstrong pointed out that the coalition seemed to be focused more on
geographical area as opposed to specific types of transportation.

Mr. Cowdell pointed out that there were two questions for the council to consider. 1) Can the city
council get behind city Resolution No. 21-07; and 2) Does the council support the Western Growth
Coalition’s vision as outlined in their own resolution?

Councilmember Armstrong expressed concern about “mission creep.” He was fine supporting
what was currently in the Resolution but did not wish to provide blanket support for any future
endeavors the Coalition might express interest in.

Councilmember Burgess felt similarly.              Moreover, he wished to ensure that the lives of
Taylorsville residents were enhanced.

Mayor Overson explained that although this was a statement of support for the Western Growth
Coalition, it didn’t necessarily have teeth.
