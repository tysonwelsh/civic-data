Mark Montgomery, Light and Power Director & Yuqi Zhao, Electric Resource Manager
addressed the City Council.

Chair Simmonds requested that Yuqi Zhao introduce herself for the benefit of the public.

Yuqi Zhao addressed the Council and said she is the Electric Resource Manager for the
Logan Light & Power Department. She has worked for the City over 20 years. Her
responsibilities include forecasting energy, baseload growth and needs.

Mark Montgomery clarified that baseload energy refers to peak energy needed during the
summer or winter. There are also peaks during the day when more energy is consumed.
However, the City needs to have a certain amount of baseload energy always available,
hence, baseload power.

Logan Base Load Forecast:
• Logan needs 30 MW of baseload generation.
• New market conditions will require day-ahead resource sufficiency with financial
penalties for non-compliance.
• Solar generation has a 24% contribution to passing resource sufficiency tests.
• Market capacity purchases are required if there is no baseload generation.
• Single 75 MW solar resource challenges resource diversity.
• Baseload resources provide maximum resource flexibility in the market.
• Logan may be able to bid any excess power into the market at a premium.

Assumptions:
• The 20-year base load and peak forecasts are based on Logan's 24-year historical
average from FY 2001 to FY 2025.
• Base year: 2024, ~ 54 MW
•Peak in 2044: grow at 1.07% annually, linear to 128 MW.
•Base load in 2044: grow at 2.32% annually, linear to 84 MW
• The expiring baseload resource:
•Sunnyside-Coal fired: ~5 MW, end in 2026 or 2028.
•Hunter- Coal fired: 13 MW, end in 2032.
•Payson-Natural gas generation: 17 MW, end in 2034 (built in 2004, last 30 years?
UAMPS assumed 2044; avg lifespan=20-40 yrs. Or use as peaker).
• The base load needed 35 MW replacement even without growth if the Payson plant
goes out of service in 2034.

Hunter (if applicable) is assumed to go online through 2032.
Nebo (if applicable) is assumed to go online through 2044.

30 MW baseload needs to be satisfied. What are our Options?:
The following are a few feasible options (nuclear and geothermal baseload projects have
been studied but have currently failed):
1. Larger amount of block purchase:
• Increased market exposure = volatility increases drastically.
• Short on capacity coverage = increased financial risk (2022 case - can wipe out multiple
years of savings and reserves in a few months).
• Will not satisfy the limitation of market exposure to be less than 10%.
• Will not satisfy EDAM Capacity=Peak +15% requirement.

2. Build local generation facility:
• Will increase local pollution, especially winter inversion in Logan (Compared to ST.
George: 80 MW of natural gas generation at Millcreek generation facility (2006 40
MW+2010 40 MW), and another 11 MW hot standby plant at Bloomington generation
facility.)
• Current 22 MW mostly for peaking power running a few hours in weekdays in summer.
• More gas lines (subject to pipeline size, pressure etc.); Air permits; Fed and local
industrial and environmental compliance requirements; Bond and increased crews for the
new facilities.
3. Join UAMPS CC NG Generation Base Load Project
UAMPS NG Base Load Project:
• 360 MW combine cycle plant; need 85% CF participation to proceed, Need Logan’s
participation at least 26 MW to go.
• UAMPS talking to other utilities to take ownership of the remainder of subscription o In
discussions with UMPA to take the remainder • Current participants: 33 members total
230 MW.
Pros:
•Many participants share overheads, services, and risks.
•COD: June 2031, one year before Hunter expiration: one year overlap for testing and
adjusting, load forecasting and NG hedging, well planned.
•Large unit, shared O&M cost, more efficient.
Cons:
•Maybe less flexible in scheduling for individual needs compared to local generation.
•Non dispatchable close to 30 MW in one unit, replacement resource and cost can be
more during unscheduled or maintenance outages

Recommendations:
•Due to the long leading time,~7 years, for the project of this scale to develop, there may
not be another opportunity in the next 20-30 years if we missed the current project.
•Recommend joining 26-30 MW in the UAMPS NG 360 MW CC base load project.
•This will partially satisfy the base load need for the next three to four decades and still
have room to grow for renewables when opportunity comes.

Councilmember López asked what the cost would translate into for the average customer
if the City is exposed to market prices.

Ms. Zhao responded that the cost would reflect on customers’ bills as surcharges as they
did in 2023 or in 2022.

Mr. Montgomery said that it was costing the average customer $5 or $7 more a month.
The Light & Power Department budgets for each month in anticipation of needing to buy
on the open market. If there is not sufficient budget, the power is still purchased, and the
difference is spread to all customers.

Ms. Zhao clarified that the amount only applies to residential, on the other hand,
commercial customers are impacted by thousands of dollars per month.

Mr. Montgomery stated that UAMPS will proceed with the base load project regardless
of whether the City participates or not.

Councilmember López inquired as to the reason why there may not be another
opportunity for baseload power of this scale to develop.

Ms. Zhao explained that the project will cover many of UAMPS member's baseload
needs. There won't be a need for another facility of this type or size for at least twenty to
thirty years. This kind of project does not typically occur twice, it is unlikely that another
project of this type will occur until the life span of this project is coming to an end.

Chair Simmonds requested clarification on what Ms. Zhao said in regard to the project
covering the baseload need of all of the UAMPS members that will participate in the
project, and if that’s the reason why another project of this type will not be brought forth
by UAMPS. Also, if the City opts out what will be the consequences.

Ms. Zhao confirmed that is the reason why another project of this type will not occur.
UAMPS will not have any need for it until the project is coming to the end of its life
cycle. Out of the 39 members of UAMPS, 33 members are participating in the project.
The City will be on its own and baseload power will be purchased on the open market.

Mr. Montgomery stated the situation is becoming much more complicated since the City
will be required to have enough baseload power and extra to cover our needs. Solar only
accounts for a quarter, it is not sufficient to cover all our baseload needs.

Chair Simmonds asked why the City would be joining Extended Day-Ahead
Market (EDAM).

power is needed for nighttime when solar is not available.

Ms. Zhao said 40 MW solar storage battery is needed with a 70 MW capacity which is
not available at this time.

Easton Baker, CEO of UAMPS approached the podium at the request of the Council.

Chair Simmonds requested Mr. Baker provide the history of UAMPS, the purpose of
UAMPS, and the overall energy goal for UAMPS participants is.

Mr. Baker introduced himself along with Mathew Hasting, Assistant General Manager,
and Jackie Coombs, Manager of Member Relations.

Mr. Baker gave a brief overview of the history of UAMPS. He stated that UAMPS was
formed in 1980 and exists for the purpose of its members and it’s a not-for-profit
company. The project was identified as part of an integrated resource plan which
analyzed and identified the collective needs of its members. It is a very competitive
environment, and there is a need to meet the surge for power. With the new generation
energy, transmission is needed and needs to be built.

Vice Chair Johnson requested confirmation that the project has been in development for
seven years.

Mr. Baker clarified that the project has been developed during the last two years. As part
of the new energy resource plan, there is a need for baseload power for UAMPS
members.

Vice Chair Johnson said that UAMPS plans for the next 10 years to pursue other energy
resources, will most of those resources be renewables and what is the expected portfolio.

Mr. Baker answered there would not be other natural gas projects. UAMPS wants to
ensure there is versality to its portfolio and the reality is that most of the new resources
are renewable energy resources. However, with that energy need, it also needs to be
reliable, and renewable resources are not always reliable.

Vice Chair Johnson asked if there would be other projects of similar scale that may be
available within the next ten years.

Councilmember López requested an explanation of a power purchase contract and a
power sales contract.

Mr. Baker explained the difference between a power purchase contract and a power sales
contract. In a power sales contract, the City has an ownership obligation to pay all costs
associated with building/managing the facility. In a power purchase contract, the City


                                                                                   11 | Page
              Logan Municipal Council Minutes ~ Logan, Utah ~ January 7, 2025
does not have ownership obligations rather is only purchasing a fixed amount of power
for a set cost.

Councilmember López wondered why the City should absorb the costs and liability for
the creation of the facility.

Mr. Baker answered that ownership is part of long-term resource planning. With the
renewable development, these resources are power contracted, the City has no ownership
over the power. The liabilities are avoided, however, there is a lack of control over said
resources. UAMPS hasn’t always had the best experience with long-term power purchase
contracts. Developers have different motivations than the City.

Vice Chair Johnson inquired if it would be possible for the City not to invest in a power
sales contract rather it would be possible to instead have a power purchase contract.

Mr. Baker responded that the current arraignments are for outside entities to purchase the
resources as owners. There would not be any intention to reserve any of the power to sell.

Vice Chair Johnson asked if there would be an increase, decrease, or neither in renewable
energy production.

Mr. Baker stated that a renewable incentive was passed in 2022, and the incoming
administration may change that incentive. The renewable community is concerned, but he

Finance Director Richard Anderson addressed the Council regarding the proposed
resolution.

Resolution 25-01 is what is referred to as a parameters and reimbursement resolution and
is the first required step to ultimately issue bonds. Resolution 25-01 is meant to
accommodate the bond financing needed for a water tank project (including water lines
and associated infrastructure). It is not intended to select a route, nor is it setting nor
authorizing the budget for a specific project (to date $20 million has been appropriated by
council from Water Fund reserves). The budget and specific project selection will come at
a later date (appropriating bond proceeds and Water Fund reserves for the remainder of the
project ultimately selected).

The bond process is specifically outlined in State Code. The City employs financial
advisors and bond attorneys to make sure we follow the bond process explicitly. According
to Code, council first adopts a parameters resolution outlining the conditions under
which the City is authorized to ultimately issue bonds. The Finance Department is
requesting the parameters to be no more than $60 million in bonds, no higher than 6 percent
interest, and no more than 31 years in length. We feel that these parameters
should accommodate the bond issuance required for any reasonable option or route that
has been discussed for the water tank and water lines. The actual amount of bonds issued
is estimated to be something less than $60 million - could be as low as $31 million, but
likely somewhere between $35 and $51 million depending on many factors including the
route selected, known factors and conditions, and most importantly, the contractors
selected and their bids for the project.

Part of this parameter’s resolution is referred to as a reimbursement resolution, authorizing
the City to "reimburse" itself for hard costs incurred, up to 60 days prior to the adoption of
the parameters resolution (See section 13 of the resolution). In December, the City incurred
$776,000 of land and right-of-way acquisition costs. It is the Finance Department's intent
that these costs associated with the Water Tank be reimbursed by the ultimate issuance of
the bonds. For that reason, we have to bring this resolution to the council now. While I
would generally prefer that the City have greater specificity on the ultimate project and
therefore greater accuracy in the estimate for the parameters resolution, the reimbursement
resolution necessitates that we proceed with the initial steps to bonding now. In discussion
with our Financial Advisors, this situation is fairly common with complex projects.

If the council adopts the parameters resolution at the January 7 meeting, the City will then
hold a public hearing on February 4 asking for public comment on the parameters
resolution and on the issuance of bonds. While it seems unusual that the City would hold
the public hearing after the council adopts a parameters resolution, that is the way Utah
law reads.
