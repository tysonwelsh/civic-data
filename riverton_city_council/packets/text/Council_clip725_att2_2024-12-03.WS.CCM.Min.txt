38          Speed Dips vs. Speed Bumps Discussion
39
40   Cary Necaise, Public Works Director, provided an update on the City’s current traffic calming
41   policy, specifically regarding speed bumps, speed humps, and dips. It was noted that in 2018,
42   the City Council passed an ordinance prohibiting the installation of speed bumps, humps, and
43   tables. Since then, staff have declined requests for these features based on the ordinance but
44   still conduct speed studies in response to complaints. Most studies show that the 85th percentile
45   speeds—the speed at which 85% of drivers travel—are within acceptable limits, suggesting that
46   perceptions of speeding often differ from actual behavior.
47
48   Mr. Necaise presented a detailed comparison of traffic calming options:
49
50
     Page | 2 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     December 3, 2024



 1   Speed Bumps:
 2
 3   Advantages: Can be placed almost anywhere on the road and constructed using asphalt or
 4   concrete. Concrete is preferred for its durability during future road maintenance.
 5
 6   Disadvantages: Removed during road overlays due to milling, issues with visibility and lighting
 7   at night, and potential for vehicle damage. They also disrupt snow plow operations and are less
 8   favorable for emergency vehicles.
 9
10   Speed Humps/Dips:
11
12   Advantages: Cause less wear on vehicles, better accommodate snow plows and emergency
13   vehicles, and remain in place during road maintenance. Recent installations on Shaggy Peak
14   and Lampton roads are under evaluation to measure their effectiveness.
15
16   Disadvantages: Require careful placement due to road grading and cost more than speed
17   bumps. Minor water pooling may occur if deeper dips are constructed.
18
19   Mr. Necaise explained that the City has an internal traffic committee, which evaluates traffic
20   assessments, crash data, and other factors to determine appropriate traffic calming measures.
21
22   Councilmember McCay asked about the cost difference. In response, Mr. Necaise explained
23   that the cost difference between speed dips versus speed bumps largely depends on the
24   materials used. When comparing asphalt dips to asphalt bumps, the costs are nearly equal.
25   However, if both are constructed in concrete, speed bumps are approximately $2,000 to $3,000
26   more expensive than dips due to their larger size (14 feet compared to 4 feet). Regarding the
27   analysis for installing stop signs, he explained that in the evaluation process, they consider
28   factors such as speed studies, traffic counts, crash data, proximity to other intersections, and
29   whether stop signs are necessary for traffic control or safety.

31            Off-Leash Dog Park Proposal
32
33   Ryan Carter, City Attorney, explained that a resident had requested that Margaret Park be
34   designated as a dog park, either entirely or during specific hours. The resident's request
35   stemmed from concerns about their dog being considered "at large" while off-leash in public
36   areas. He clarified that, under current laws, any off-leash dog in public spaces is considered at
37   large, even during activities like fetch.
38
39   Mr. Carter discussed the logistical and legal considerations of converting Margaret Park into a
40   dog park. He emphasized the importance of fencing to ensure safety and outlined the minimal
41   fencing modifications required to enclose the park. However, he noted that creating a
42   designated dog park area would necessitate regulations to ensure non-dog-owning members of
43   the public avoid the park during dog park hours for safety reasons. He also reminded the
44   Council about a previous plan to convert a detention pond near 3600 West and 11800 South
45   into a dog park, which was ultimately deprioritized in favor of other amenities. He suggested
46   revisiting this plan as an alternative to modifying Margaret Park. Finally, he recommended
47   gathering feedback from residents near Margaret Park to assess broader community interest
48   and potential concerns.
49
50   Councilmember Pierucci expressed support for exploring the idea of a dog park in the City,
51   noting its potential value given the changing dynamics of the community. He emphasized that
     Page | 3 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     December 3, 2024




 1   Margaret Park need not be the only option and suggested staff identify multiple potential
 2   locations or consider parks with designated off-leash hours.
 3
 4   Councilmember McDougal expressed a preference to table the discussion for now, suggesting
 5   that further exploration of a dog park should occur within the context of the City's broader
 6   strategic planning. However, he supported incorporating a dog park into the design of new
 7   parks in areas with greater housing density, as this could reduce costs and align with strategic
 8   planning.
 9
10   Councilmember McCay reflected on previous discussions regarding TRCC funding for a dog
11   park, noting that additional requirements, such as the inclusion of bathrooms, had increased the
12   costs at the time, leading the City to prioritize another project. She expressed familiarity with
13   Margaret Park, emphasizing its potential as a cost-effective option for a dog park due to its
14   existing fencing, which would require only minimal additions. However, she agreed with waiting
15   to discuss the matter within the context of broader strategic priorities.
16
17   Councilmember Haymond suggested that a dog park near the west side or closer to Mountain
18   View Village might better serve the community.
19
20   Kevin Hicks, City Manager, raised concerns about the potential expectations and associated
21   costs of developing a dog park. He noted that residents who desire a dog park often expect
22   more than a simple fenced area—they may want restrooms, separate sections for large and
23   small dogs, and other amenities to enhance usability and safety. He recommended that any
24   future discussions about a dog park should include clarity about the desired standard and scope
25   of amenities to ensure alignment with the City’s strategic planning and budget priorities.

25            Ordinance No. 24-27 – Roberts Rezone, rezone of approximately 0.91-acres
26            located at 4191 West 13400 South from R-1 (Residential 1-acre min) to C-R
27            (Regional Commercial)
28
29   Tim Prestwich, City Planner, presented the Roberts Rezone, following a recommendation for
30   approval by the Planning Commission in a 7-0 vote. The property in question, currently zoned
31   R1, is the last remaining parcel in the area that has not been rezoned to regional commercial.
32   Neighboring properties have already been rezoned, some as far back as 2008, and the area
33   has been designated for commercial use in the general plan since at least 2001. The rezone
34   aligns with the surrounding properties and is being pursued as part of a plan involving
35   developers. The rezone request aims to fit in with the area's zoning, which includes regional
36   commercial, and the proposed rezone to regional commercial reflects the area’s existing land
37   use plan.

 1            Resolution No. 24-101 – Amendments to Fee Schedule
 2
 3   Nick Geer, Administrative Services Director, expressed appreciation for the staff and for Mayor
 4   Staggs’s work in updating the fee schedule. Two key points were emphasized: first, the
 5   inclusion of a false alarm fee, which was already in the code but had not been listed in the fee
 6   schedule, to increase transparency; and second, removal of dog licensing fees due to upcoming

10   Councilmembers and staff discussed changes to water fees, transitioning from a flat-rate
11   discount to a percentage-based discount for certain groups, such as veterans and seniors. The

13   consistent with the County's existing practices. However, it was acknowledged that this change
14   could result in a reduced discount for about 50 seniors currently benefiting from a flat-rate
15   discount. They discussed the need to ensure that those affected by the reduction are informed
16   about the changes and given the opportunity for hardship considerations. Staff was tasked with
17   reaching out to the 50 affected residents to communicate the changes and the potential for a
18   hardship waiver through the County's process.
19
20   Additionally, metering for secondary water use was discussed, with the hope that residents will
21   be more aware of their water consumption and reduce usage, thereby alleviating some strain on
22   the City's infrastructure.

33            Resolution No. 24-102 – Budget Amendment
34
35   Mr. Geer explained that the proposed budget amendments included several key items:
36
37   Carryovers: These involve projects that extend beyond a single fiscal year. If funds allocated for
38   a project aren't fully spent by the end of the fiscal year, the remaining budget is carried over to
39   the next year to complete the project. The reason for carryovers is that some projects,
40   particularly construction-related, can’t always be completed by June 30 due to scheduling
41   constraints.
42
43   New Animal Shelter: The Council is being asked to amend the current year's budget to allocate
44   an additional $700,000 for the design and construction of a new animal control facility.
45
46   Water Van Replacement: A water van was involved in an accident (which wasn't the City's
47   fault), and the City received an insurance payout. The budget amendment is to cover the cost of
48   replacing the vehicle, with no additional funding required beyond the insurance payout.
49
50   Additional Vehicle Requests: Two vehicles are requested to be purchased early. These vehicles
51   were planned for the next fiscal year, but due to favorable market conditions, purchasing them
     Page | 8 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     December 3, 2024




 1   now at a reduced cost is seen as a better option.
 2
 3   Councilmember McDougal expressed strong support for the proposed budget amendments,
 4   acknowledging that many of the amendments are related to carryovers for projects that are
 5   already underway. He emphasized that securing bids early is important to avoid delays and
 6   inflationary cost increases, particularly for the animal control facility, which has a hard deadline
 7   that needs to be met.
 8
 9   Councilmember Pierucci expressed concern about the proposed $36,000 per animal cost for the
10   new animal shelter, questioning whether the City has thoroughly explored all options. He felt the
11   decision to move forward was being made under pressure and might be a short-term solution
12   that does not justify such a significant investment.
13
14   Mayor Staggs explained that alternative clinics were explored, but other facilities were not
15   interested in the model or had similar limitations regarding euthanization and interactions with
16   the public. He compared this situation to City-run services, like garbage collection, where the
17   City must step in due to lack of interest from private businesses.
18
19   Mayor Staggs also discussed the long-term costs of the shelter, suggesting that while the cost
20   per animal might seem high, it would be more manageable when considered over the facility's
21   lifespan. He acknowledged that, despite his reluctance to spend money on such a service, the
22   shelter would provide better control and care for animals, especially in cases where animals
23   were aggressive or violating ordinances.
24
25   Councilmember McDougal emphasized that the animal shelter issue was primarily a public
26   safety concern. He acknowledged the high cost but argued that the City's responsibility to
27   ensure the safety of residents outweighed the expense. He further highlighted the rising
28   construction costs and suggested that delaying the project would only result in higher expenses
29   in the future.
