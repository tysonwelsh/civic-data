RESOLUTION 25-100: APPROVE A FUNDING AMENDMENT TO A
COOPERATIVE AGREEMENT BETWEEN THE CITY AND THE UTAH
DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION
Dan Johnson, Public Works Director, presented proposed resolution 25-100 that would
approve a Funding Amendment to a Cooperative Agreement Between the City and the Utah
Department of Transportation.

Written documentation previously provided to the City Council included information as
follows:

       West Valley City submitted a Safe Sidewalk application in 2024 to construct
       missing segments of sidewalk along the north side of 3500 South between
       Sunshade Drive (5450 West) and approx. 5350 West. The estimate submitted
       with the application to construct the entire length of the missing sidewalk, along
       with the associated right-of-way and improvements, was $500,000.

       The Safe Sidewalk program is administered by UDOT and requires a 25% local
       government. WVC was successful in the 2024 application and executed a
       Cooperative Agreement with UDOT in the amount of $225,334 ($169,000 State /
       $56,334 LG Match). Realizing that $225,334 is not enough to construct the entire
       project limits identified in the application, UDOT allowed flexibility to adjust the
       limits of the project as necessary to maximize the use of the available funds.

       As the design progressed, WVC continued to coordinate with UDOT to identify
       additional funding for the project. UDOT agreed to find additional funding to
       construct the entire segment if WVC acquired the right-of-way needed for the
       project. The City was successful in getting all landowners to donate the property
       and UDOT is therefore providing the additional funding.

       This modification enables access to the additional funds being contributed by the
       state from the Safe Sidewalk Program. The required 25% match for the additional
       funds will be met with a soft match including the design, ROW Acquisition, and
       construction management services.

RESOLUTION 25-102: AWARD A CONTRACT TO EDA ARCHITECTS INC. TO
PREPARE COST ESTIMATES AND RENDERINGS FOR THE MAVERIK
CENTER
Jake Arslanian, Facilities Manager, presented proposed resolution 25-102 that would award
a Contract to EDA Architects Inc. To Prepare Cost Estimates and Renderings for the Maverik
Center.

Written documentation previously provided to the City Council included information as
follows:

       The Maverik center is almost 30 years old and in need of major renovations to keep
       up with the industry as a Sports Arena and Entertainment Center. The Maverick
       Center is also a major venue for the upcoming 2034 Olympic Games. A renovation
       of this magnitude will need a cost estimate and presentation tool to generate funding
       from many partners, such as the State of Utah, and the Olympic Committee. This
       contract will provide a cost estimate for the renovations and presentation tool to
       promote and solicit funding for the Maverik Center’s critically needed renovations.

       The City advertised for the Maverik Center renovations cost estimates Request for
       Proposal (RFP), EDA Architects Inc. submitted the highest scoring proposal.

       The necessary repairs and renovations list will include some of the following: A
       new roof, design and build-out of an “Ultra-Lounge” on 4th floor – combining the
       restaurant and Centennial room, renovation and upgrade of all arena suites and
       furnishings, remodel and update of the arena lavatories and add additional women’s
       lavatories, entire arena seat replacements, and replace Ice Sheet and chiller system.

       The proposal from EDA Architect for these services is $307,000.00.

       The funding for this project is from the Olympic and Paralympic Venues Grant
       Fund in FY 2025 for Olympic venue renovation projects as follows. $500,000 to
       West Valley City for the Maverick Center.

Mayor Lang asked where the funding is coming from. Jake replied that the City received
$500,000 from the State. Councilmember Harmon asked if EDA will provide a
comprehensive report of what needs to be done for staff and the Council’s consideration.

RESOLUTION 25-103: AWARD A CONTRACT TO LANDMARK DESIGN, INC
TO PREPARE A PARKS MASTER PLAN
Jamie Young, Parks and Recreation Director, presented proposed resolution 25-103 that
would award a Contract to Landmark Design, Inc to Prepare a Parks Master Plan

Written documentation previously provided to the City Council included information as
follows:

       The Parks and Recreation Department would like to perform a master plan in order
       to establish a long-term vision for Parks and Recreation facilities. This will guide
       future development and the allocation of funding. It will provide statistical data to
       identify community needs and identify where we may need different features or
       facilities throughout the city.

       A RFP was advertised, and proposals were submitted from the following
       companies:

       Berry Dunn                    $142,711.00
       PSOMAS                        $179,970.00
       Landmark Design               $103,640.00

       After a review of the proposals and speaking with references, the Parks and
       Recreation division selected Landmark Design to perform the Master Plan.

Councilmember Wood asked if this would include an analysis of existing park projects that
are pending. Jamie replied yes. Councilmember Wood asked if a recommendation would
be made and what that recommendation might include. Jamie replied that future park
development and renovation decisions will be considered, including possible adding
amenities currently lacking in the city, such as a splash pad and a dog park. Equity across
neighborhoods will also be a factor, as some areas have more access to parks and amenities
than others. Councilmember Wood asked how long this would take. Jamie replied that the
       MINUTES OF COUNCIL STUDY MEETING – JULY 8, 2025
                            -6-


goal is to complete the study by the end of the calendar year to coincide with the budget
process. Councilmember Harmon asked if a list could be provided of current parks and
addresses and list of park property that the City owns. He indicated that he feels this type
of analysis could be done by City employees instead of an outside firm. Jamie explained
that the City has historically followed an internal prioritization process for park
development and has done so since incorporation. She noted that involving a neutral third
party will help mitigate bias. She emphasized the value of adopting a formal, City Council-
approved guiding document to clearly communicate priorities and planning decisions to
residents. This approach ensures transparency and broad community input, rather than
relying solely on internal direction. Mayor Lang asked if the date the City acquired property
could be included in the document Councilmember Harmon requested. Jamie replied yes.
Councilmember Huynh asked for further clarification. Jamie explained that the master plan
process will include extensive community engagement, including surveys and public
meetings, to gather input on desired amenities. A needs assessment will be conducted, and
existing construction standards will be evaluated. This process is intended to guide and
enhance future development. When designing parks, input from neighborhood meetings
helps determine which playground features and amenities to include.

The City Council will consider Resolution 25-103 at the Regular Council Meeting
scheduled June 24, 2025 at 6:30 P.M

       Written documentation previously provided to the City Council included
       information as follows:

               The Housing Authority was created in 1984 to run public housing projects
               and administer the HUD Section 8 Housing Choice Voucher Program.
               The Housing Authority’s voluntary transfer of the HCV program to

       Housing Connect has taken effect as of July 1. One of the Housing
       Authority’s final remaining programs is its interest in West Valley
       Permanent Housing, LLC. That entity owns and operates the Jared Alan
       Campbell Apartments, which were established as a federal low income tax
       credit housing project. The property is required to operate in this fashion
       until 2100.

       As the Housing Authority winds down operations, it became necessary to
       identify another entity who could administer the project as required by
       federal law. Utah Non Profit Housing Corporation has operated as the
       contract manager of the property for many years and is familiar with the
       project’s operational needs. UNPHC is willing to assume ownership of
       and responsibility for the project, including compliance with all federal
       and state regulations.

Councilmember Harmon asked when the low income tax credit was applied.
Brandon replied 1999. Councilmember Harmon asked if the property could be
sold. Brandon explained that the property's value is effectively negative due to
the deep affordability requirement, with units restricted to under 40% AMI rent
levels. These properties operate at a deficit and rely on subsidies such as CDBG
grants, state funding, and housing authority support. Because of these restrictions,
the property cannot realistically be sold. Additionally, selling would require
redeeming tax credits—costing tens of millions of dollars—and any proceeds
from the sale would be restricted for use in public housing, meaning no
unrestricted funds could be generated.

The Housing Authority will consider Resolution 25-05 at the Special Housing
Authority Meeting scheduled July 22, 2025 at 6:30 P.M

A.  CITIZEN SURVEY UPDATE
    Lauren McPeak, Administrative Analyst, introduced Ron Gailey.

      Ron presented key findings from the community survey, which received 1,456
      responses—far exceeding typical survey response rates. The survey had a margin
      of error of ±2% and was distributed via utility billing emails, resulting in a sample
      skewed toward homeowners. Responses were geographically diverse, with strong
      participation from Districts 2 and 4, and reflected a broad range of demographics,
      including age, gender, and race (with racial data weighted to match city
      demographics).

      Key Findings:
      • General Sentiment:
            o A majority feel the city is moving in the right direction.
            o About a quarter believe conditions have worsened in the past 3 years.
            o Compared to other cities, West Valley City ranked low in “refer a
               friend” scores, but high in improvement trajectory.
      • Community Likes:
            o Positive feedback included themes like beautiful surroundings, sense of
               community, good location, and safety.
            o 65% of respondents voluntarily left comments.
      • Top Concerns:
            o Cleanliness and maintenance topped the list—uniquely high compared
               to other cities.
            o Safety, infrastructure strain due to growth, and traffic were also
               frequently mentioned.
            o Residents generally want more visible police presence, not less.
      • Prioritization Insights:
            o Top 4 priorities selected from the final round:
                   1. City Beautification
                   2. Parks and Open Spaces
                   3. Public Streets & Infrastructure
                   4. Crime Prevention

       o  Additional concerns included housing affordability and neighborhood
          safety patrols.
       o Amenities like golf courses and sports facilities were lower priorities for
          most, despite vocal niche groups.
       o Fire and EMS received high performance marks but were not cited as
          urgent needs.
•   Leadership Expectations:
       o Residents expect leaders to:
              ▪ Manage growth wisely
              ▪ Value public input
              ▪ Strategically plan for the future

Ron emphasized the importance of communicating progress on resident-identified
priorities and aligning city actions with community expectations. He concluded by
noting that leadership gains trust and traction when it clearly responds to the
public’s top concerns.

Councilmember Huynh noted that this is a great tool that the Council can use to
guide decisions.

Councilmember Whetstone asked how the number of survey responses compared
to other cities in terms of percentage of total population, noting that while the
response count seemed strong, he was curious about its relative representation. Ron
explained that the survey intentionally capped responses at 1,500 to maintain
efficiency, as additional responses beyond that would not significantly affect
results. He noted that response rates vary by city size and demographics:
    • Large cities typically see 4–5% response rates.
    • Small cities may achieve 20–30%.
    • Affluent communities often have higher engagement.
Given this, West Valley City's response rate was considered very respectable and
on par with or better than similar-sized cities.

Mayor Lang noted that the Council also identified beautification as an important
priority during the annual budget retreat held in February.

Ron provided insights from survey responses regarding desired business types,
employee interactions, and final community priorities:
• Business Preferences: Residents most frequently requested restaurant chains,
   grocery stores, local and small businesses, and specialty food stores. Specific
   businesses like Trader Joe’s and Chick-fil-A were named. These preferences
   could support economic development outreach efforts.

     •   Employee Contact & Ratings:
            o 66% of residents reported having contact with a city department in the
                past 6 months, most commonly with utility billing, police, or code
                enforcement.
            o Departments such as fire and the Utah Cultural Celebration Center
                received very high satisfaction ratings.
            o The Mayor and Council received mid-level but respectable ratings,

     Councilmember Huynh asked what the next steps are. Ron explained that the survey
     tool can be used repeatedly, either by conducting the same broad survey again or
     by focusing on a completely different topic depending on the city’s needs. He
     emphasized that this approach works well for both the consultant and the city,
     helping to ensure that priorities remain aligned, especially as part of the strategic
     planning process. Ron offered to assist in designing follow-up surveys that dive
     deeper into specific topics whenever more detailed information is needed to make
     sound decisions. He noted that a more effective way to gather feedback is to present
     residents with concrete ideas or options rather than open-ended questions, which
     can lead to confusion. Many cities nearby have successfully used this two-step
     process, starting with a broad overview and then doing a focused deep dive to guide
     future actions.
