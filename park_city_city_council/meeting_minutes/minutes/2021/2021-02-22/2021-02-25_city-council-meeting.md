Mayor Beerman read the Determination of Substantial Health and Safety Risk.

WORK SESSION

Park City Arts and Culture District Follow Up Discussion – Finance Plan:
Jed Briggs, Budget Manager, reviewed that the December sales tax revenue was down
nine percent from last year, but it was higher than the projected numbers and he felt
encouraged. With regard to the Arts and Culture District, he displayed three scenarios
for how the project could be financed:

Scenario One – the full project buildout/$89,165,450: Housing revenue included funds
from Transient Room Tax (TRT) and Additional Resort Sales Tax (ARST) bonds, the
remaining funds from the senior center and the firehouse sale, the remainder of the
soils repository budget, and funds from the Housing fee-in-lieu. Non-housing revenue
sources were the TRT 2022 bond, TRT related Capital Fund balance, and the assumed
sale of land to the district partners. Transportation and walkability revenue would come
from the Transportation Fund.

Scenario Two – Parking, Buildings Two, Three, and Four, and a RFP for Building One
(private developer)/$63,957,714: The Housing revenue would be removed, the TRT and
ARST bond would be higher and all other revenue would be the same as Scenario One.

Scenario Three – Parking, Buildings Two, Three, and Four, and bottom level of Building
One/$67,707,714: Revenue would come from the same sources as Scenario Two with
an additional amount on the TRT and ARST bond.

Council Member Gerber noted the cost was only $25 million less than the total budget if
Building One was removed. David Everitt stated there were fixed costs that would still
be needed for the other aspects of the project and the housing money would not be
used to cover those costs.

Mayor Beerman asked what revenue would be from TRT bonds and what revenue
would be from ARST bonds. Daenitz indicated a $9 million TRT bond and a $33 million
ARST bond would be issued. Briggs stated ARST funds could only be used for Main
Street and Open Space, but Council could appropriate them as they chose, so they
could be used for the Arts and Culture District. Council Member Henney asked to see
the list of Old Town Infrastructure Study (OTIS) planned and completed projects.
Discussion ensued on some of the projects from that list. Dias stated staff could come
back with the status of the projects on the list. Council Member Doilney was interested
in seeing the opportunity costs that would come with using each category of funds.
Council Member Joyce stated there was a lot of discussion on connectivity being part of
the Arts and Culture District project, but he hadn't heard that funding had been allocated
for connectivity. He stated if it was planned to be part of the district, the cost should be
part of the budget.

Council Member Henney stated the scenarios showed that no property taxes were
being used to pay for the district, but the funding was coming from tourism dollars and
the district was going to be repurposed for community benefit. Council Member Joyce
knew this was tourist funded, but his concern was the opportunity costs that the City
would now have to delay or give up. Mayor Beerman noted that aside from the ARST
funds, the other funds were restricted and could only be used for certain purposes.
Council Member Doilney asserted there was an opportunity cost for not building the Arts
and Culture District. He thought there were only two options for this location - the Arts
and Culture District or 100% affordable housing. Council Member Henney stated this
parcel was always intended for public benefit. Council Member Worel indicated the
Council had an obligation to do what it said it would do. This district was intended to
diversify and expand the economy. Council Member Henney thought the district would
benefit the community and tourists would benefit as a bonus. Council Member Worel
agreed and stated this would be a community gathering center. Council Member Gerber
thought there was room for nonprofits to fundraise for this project in order to take some
burden off the City. Council Member Henney noted Sundance and Kimball Art Center
were investing massive funds as well.

Briggs reviewed the City would only bond against 65% of the total ARST/TRT revenues.
There was TRT and ARST cash funding over and above the annual debt payment, and
100% of that revenue could be used for the Arts and Culture District. Mayor Beerman
asked if cash projects could be done with the surplus tax revenue that didn't get
allocated for bond repayment, to which Daenitz affirmed.

Council Member Doilney discussed future plans for gondolas as a transportation
solution, and asked if that would be one of the opportunity costs for constructing the
Arts and Culture District. Council Member Henney thought that discussion would
revolve around the Olympics, since that event would bring federal money for a gondola

project. Briggs noted staff was budgeting for Transportation over the next five years and

Council Member Joyce asked staff to report back on the average Capital Projects
budgets funded each year for the last five years. Daenitz stated the average ARST cash

Briggs noted the City goal was to never go above 50% debt ratio to revenues collected.
He recommended Council consider expanding the use of ARST from OTIS for the Arts
and Culture District with a 20-year term bond. Daenitz reviewed that the municipal
borrowing rates had a negative interest rate in real terms and noted this would not last
for long.

Briggs displayed a chart showing projects the City completed with ARST revenues.
Council Member Worel asked how market rate commercial units would reduce the
project cost if they were added to the district. Daenitz stated residential rentals would
continue to support housing, but with commercial rental revenue, the City would have
flexibility in how to use that income. Everitt thought the revenue could offset the need for
TRT funds or it could be additional funds for programming or maintenance in the district.
Briggs thought commercial market rate units would be an operational boon for more
programming. Council Member Henney indicated market rate commercial would create
vibrancy in the district. Council Member Gerber asked how restrictions could be placed
on commercial units to make sure the businesses in those spaces were in line with the
community values.

Council Member Joyce worried the bonding for the district would bind future Councils
with debt for 20 years, which was out of the normal bonding bounds of 15-year bond
terms. He suggested the City bond for 15 years and issue a GO bond for the remaining
funding gap. Council Member Henney recognized those concerns, but he disagreed in
the need for a GO bond because of the cash revenue the City consistently received
above the debt obligation, which could be used for projects annually. Mayor Beerman
indicated the City did not go into debt unless the public favored what it was needed for,
so the debt was not irresponsible. Council Member Doilney agreed the City had not
bonded in this way before, but it was a transformational project that would be the heart
of the City.

Everitt reviewed there was an option of value engineering the housing portion and in
Buildings Two, Three and Four, office space could be substituted for residential units.
The tradeoff would be no artist housing for those buildings. He thought the residential
component in Building Three was necessary. Council Member Henney asked how the
Housing funds would be used if they didn't go to the Arts and Culture District. Council
Member Worel asked how much housing would be lost with Scenarios Two or Three.
Everitt thought 50 residential units could be built on the parking lot pad in the future. He
stated if Council went with Scenario Three, there would be additional time to figure out
the housing piece before it was necessary to move forward with the housing
component. Council Member Gerber thought the Arts and Culture District housing would
fund future housing projects. She stated the extra cost for doing the total buildout was
$25 million more, which was a pretty good deal and would result in building efficiencies.

Council Member Henney thought Homestake could be used as the funding source for
future housing projects instead of having the Arts and Culture District as the funding
source. Council Member Gerber indicated it was difficult to find land and a lot of money
would be wasted while Council continued with ongoing discussions on housing and
opportunity costs. Council Member Doilney stated that $25 million broke out to
$500,000 per unit and he thought housing could be built cheaper elsewhere. Council
Member Gerber stated the costs would continue to rise as time went on, and this was
the way to go right now. Mayor Beerman stated Scenarios Two and Three left the door
open to construct housing on this parcel. Council Member Henney favored moving
forward with the project even though a scenario had not yet been chosen. Mayor
Beerman stated there should be one more meeting to receive public input and to decide
how to proceed.

Regarding the 20-year bond, Council Member Doilney asked what projects would not be
implemented if the bond was used for Arts and Culture District. Mayor Beerman stated
public input should be received on this. Everitt asked if office space should be
considered for the district. Council was not in favor of office space and felt it would not
contribute to the vibrancy of the district. Council Member Worel hoped for commercial
spaces with cultural diversity. Everitt asked if a comprehensive resolution would be
appropriate so people could see what was being considered. Council Member Gerber
thought there should be a breakdown of the portion paid by residents versus tourists.
Daenitz stated an estimation could be drafted, but the numbers would not be 100%
accurate. Mayor Beerman preferred drafting a proposal for the public to review. The
Council was ready to hear thoughts from the community. Council Member Henney
noted there had been many emails regarding the Arts and Culture District.

Rebecca Ward, Land Use Planner, Erik Daenitz, Budget Analyst, and Jason Glidden,
Affordable Housing Manager, presented this item. Ward indicated a study was
performed to see what initiatives were needed to promote private development of
affordable housing. The proposed Affordable Master Planned Development (AMPD)
code amendments would increase density, reduce parking, implement zoning
incentives, provide that at least 50% affordable units were in the development, and
permit AMPDs in non-historic zoning districts where multi-unit developments were an

feet in height. Glidden stated there was an allowance for extra height if the building
design was a step back. Council Member Gerber felt it made sense to restrict height in

   AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
                          BACKGROUND


Since 1984, the Affordable Master Planned Development (AMPD)
code provided a 20-unit density bonus for AMPDs that were 100%
affordable.

However, in 37 years, no AMPD has ever been constructed by a
private developer or through a public-private partnership.

While there have been public-private partnerships to develop
affordable units in Park City, the AMPD density bonus was not the
incentive for these partnerships.
   AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
                           BACKGROUND

In 2018, Council directed Planning and Housing staff to evaluate the
AMPD code to identify obstacles.

Staff proposed AMPD amendments to

• Reduce the requirement from 100% to 50% affordable or
  attainable housing
• Create a sliding scale for a density bonus based on the Area
  Median Income served
• Reduce parking
• Exempt micro-units 500 square feet or less from parking
  requirements
  AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
                        BACKGROUND


In 2018, the Planning Commission forwarded a positive
recommendation, but the Council requested further study to
demonstrate whether the AMPD amendments went far enough to
incentivize public-private partnerships
AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
 The City hired Cascadia Partners to complete two studies.

   AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
                         PROPOSED CODE
The proposed Land Management Code amendments create a new
review/approval process for Affordable Master Planned Developments
(AMPDs)

• AMPDs must provide at least 50% of total Residential Unit
  Equivalents as affordable units

• Permits AMPDs in non-historic Zoning Districts where Multi-Unit
  Dwellings are an allowed or conditional use

• Establishes volume-based density

• Aligns LMC definitions with Housing Resolution definitions
   AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
                         PROPOSED CODE

• Allows for parking reductions and increased height

• Allows for mixed-use development (up to 10,000 square feet of
  commercial/retail/office/public/quasi-public uses)

• Prohibits nightly rentals and timeshares

• Requires recordation of deed restrictions for a 40-year period

• Requires annual compliance reports

• Reduces Open Space from 50% to 20% and setbacks to zone-
  required setbacks
   AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
                   SEPARATE AMPDs FROM MPDs

MPDs trigger affordable housing obligations under Housing Resolution
25-2020 and require affordable units for 20% of the total Residential
Unit Equivalents and units for employees generated

This requirement is to mitigate the impact of the development on the
community in part by providing affordable units for the workforce
generated by the specific development proposed

These affordable units do not count toward density on-site – they are
a density bonus
    AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
                   SEPARATE AMPDs FROM MPDs

AMPDs incentivize private development or public-private partnerships
in the development of projects wherein at least 50% of the Residential
Unit Equivalents are affordable

The affordable units count toward the density, but there are other
incentives for AMPDs that differ from MPDs and include:

•   Volume-based density
•   Reduced open space (from 50% to 20%)
•   Reduced setbacks
•   Increased height
•   Potential parking reductions
    AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
                           ZONING DISTRICTS

AMPDs are not allowed in any Historic District

Only in Zoning Districts that already permit Multi-Unit Dwellings as an
Allowed or Conditional Use

•   Residential Development
•   Residential Development Medium
•   Residential Medium
•   Recreation Commercial
•   General Commercial
•   Light Industrial
•   Community Transition
   AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
                             PARKING


Applicants may request a reduction in parking and must:

• Demonstrate that parking reductions materially increase the
  feasibility of the proposed AMPD

• Fund and submit a parking and traffic study completed by a third
  party selected by the City

• Demonstrate that the proposed AMPD sufficiently addresses the
  parking demand for the AMPD
    AFFORDABLE MASTER PLANNED DEVELOPMENTS
               THE COMMISSION MAY REDUCE PARKING

• On sites that are one acre or less by deducting 5,000 square feet per
  15,000 square feet of Gross Floor Area for each floor from the sum of
  total floor area that is used to calculate parking requirements

• Off-Street parking is available along the perimeter of the AMPD

• A clear and irrevocable agreement authorizes AMPD residents to park in
  an off-Site Parking Area or Parking Structure that is located within 1,000
  feet of the AMPD perimeter boundary

• The AMPD is within ¼-mile from a bus stop that includes a waiting
  shelter consistent with City standards

1,2,or 3 Private Houses?: While less impactful, the possible sale and subsequent private
development of this parcel raises the ethical question of whether the city should sell open space for the
sole purpose of raising money? Clearly, the development of this parcel is NOT urban in-fill, but it would
be URBAN SPRAWL; it provides a flat natural scenic stroll on the edge of town like no other; it’s
immediately adjacent to existing open space, the Ontario Mill site, as well as the desired conservation
parcel of the portion of the Huntsman Parcel at 5000 Royal Street. It would aggrieve many, including
myself, who have voted and contributed our own dollars to save open space elsewhere to see this rare
open space in Old Town sold for profit.

We really need to look no further than the General Plan:


Goal 1 Park City will protect undeveloped lands; discourage sprawl, and direct growth
inward to strengthen existing neighborhoods. Development here is not inward growth; it
would destroy undeveloped lands, and IT IS SPRAWL.

Goal 4 Open Space: Conserve a connected, healthy network of open space for continued
access to and respect for the Natural Setting.

EVERY SINGLE ONE OF THESE 4A through 4E apply to this property.


Objectives
4A Protect natural areas critical to biodiversity and healthy ecological function.
4B Buffer entry corridors from development and protect mountain vistas to enhance the natural setting, quality
        of life, and visitor experience.

4C Prevent fragmentation of open space to support ecosystem health, wildlife corridors, and recreation
        opportunities.

4D Minimize further land disturbance and conversion of remaining undisturbed land areas to development to
        minimize the effects on neighborhoods.

4E Collaborate with neighborhoods to create small parks or passive open space areas.

In Summary: Each time this parcel is proposed for development, it has met with stern opposition
from local residents; not a function of NIMBYISM, but of well-reasoned points of view that are supported
by the facts as well as the General Plan.

The city should review its current position on this property and compare them with the core values and
guiding principles throughout the General Plan. By doing so, the value of this property as open space is
priceless.
