     PRESENTATION – Human Resource Annual Report

Ms. Rugg and Ms. Jones presented the annual Human Resource (HR) report for the City of Orem.
Ms. Jones began by sharing employee demographics as of July 1, 2020, including gender,
ethnicity, and residential data. She said approximately $48 million was budgeted for all personnel
expenditures, with approximately $37.5 million from the General Fund and $10.5 million from all
other funds. She shared a graph outlining the percentages of personnel expenditures by department,
with the largest percentages correlating to the police, public works, and fire departments
respectively. Ms. Jones provided a benefits overview from the past five years of Orem costs versus
employee costs for a high deductible family coverage plan with HSA. The City had seen over 29%
increase over the five years but had absorbed those costs to keep employee costs constant. Ms.
Jones then shared a table with fixed benefits comparison to sister cities including American Fork,

Bountiful, Draper, Layton, Lehi, Murray, Ogden, Pleasant Grove, Provo, Sandy, South Jordan,
Spanish Fork, Springville, St. George, West Jordan, and West Valley. She said on average Orem
had a higher deductible plan and contributed more to dental and HSA; employees paid $70 a month
for health coverage, compared to around $100 in other cities.

Ms. Jones continued, discussing retirement costs and contributions as well as employee turnover
rates. She provided data regarding separations from the city from Fiscal Year (FY) 2010 to present
and the most common reasons for separation, which included retirement and obtaining other
employment. Discussion ensued regarding turnover rates, particularly among sworn police and fire
officers, and whether compensation was a key factor in those separations. Ms. Rugg said in some
cases compensation was a main factor, but other factors included opportunities to advance and
family considerations. She said she worked closely with the fire and police chiefs to develop
requirements and titles for advancement. In response to a Council question, Chief Sanderson said
some of the classes required had been postponed or cancelled because of the COVID-19 pandemic,
but he would reach out to Utah Valley University (UVU) regarding alternative courses that may
be offered.

Many council members asked questions specifically about police and fire employees. Discussion
continued regarding recruitment and lateral hires, benefits comparisons, opportunities for
advancement, and open communication within departments. Ms. Rugg said it was important that
employees felt they could speak to their supervisors or herself specifically should any issues arise.
She was working hard to encourage open and transparent communication, and said only in rare
circumstances would any information shared with her ever be divulged to others. This extreme
scenario may include if anything illegal was going on. General discussion ensued.

Ms. Rugg thanked Ms. Jones for the work she put into the gathering and sharing the data in the
report. She also wanted to thank staff that helped with HR outreach, including Pete Wolfley, Matt
Kemmerer, and Jason Adamson. Ms. Rugg said she would send the report to the Council for further
review and said they may contact her with additional questions.

     PUBLIC HEARING
     ORDINANCE – Amending Sections 22-24-3(F)(1)(a) and 22-24-3(F)(2)(d) “Design
     Standards” of the Orem City Code, pertaining to accessory buildings in the Canyon Crossing
     District (CXD) zone

Mr. Bench said the Canyon Crossing District zone (encompassing the area around the intersection
of 800 North and State Street), like the other State Street district zones, contained design standards
that were intended to create an urban design feel and to maximize the development of properties
in that zone. Two of the main development requirements were that buildings must be brought up
close to the street with a maximum setback of ten feet and must also have a minimum of two
stories.

As an exception to the above requirements, the current ordinance provided that one existing
building on a parcel may be expanded by up to 15% of the floor area of the building without
meeting the minimum height and maximum setback requirements. However, all other building


   City Council Minutes – July 14, 2020 (p.5)   A complete video of the meeting can be found at www.orem.org/meetings
additions as well as new buildings must comply with the minimum height and maximum setback
requirements.

Mr. Bench said a property owner in the CXD zone recently expressed a desire to construct a large
accessory structure in the back of his lot and behind the existing buildings on the parcel. Upon
consideration of the request, staff realized that the current CXD zone did not allow the construction
of an accessory building for things like storage unless it complied with the minimum height and
maximum setback requirements. Since it would generally be impracticable to construct an
accessory building that met those requirements, staff believed that it was reasonable to allow an
accessory building in the CXD zone to be built in the rear of a lot even though it did not meet the
height and setback requirements. However, staff believed that the size of such an accessory
building should be limited in order to avoid encouraging the construction of new buildings in the
rear of a lot that ultimately may be devoted to primary uses since the intent of the ordinance was
to bring the primary uses out to the front of the lot.

Staff proposed to amend the CXD zone to allow one accessory building that did not meet the
minimum height and maximum setback requirements to be constructed on a lot provided that the
accessory building was no larger than 40% of the floor area of one primary building on the parcel.

Under the proposed amendment, one accessory structure would be allowed on a parcel in the CXD
zone provided it met the following:

     1.         A maximum area of 40% of the gross leasable floor area (GLFA) of the primary
                structure on the property.
     2.         A maximum height of one story, thirteen (13) feet in height.
     3.         The use must be exclusively for storage in connection with the primary use of the
                property.
     4.         It must be an accessory use to the principal use of the property.
     5.         It must be located behind the primary structure.
     6.         It cannot be connected to water or sewer services.
     7.         The parcel on which it is located cannot have already used the one-time 15% building
                exception noted above.

Mayor Brunst asked the height of the structure they wanted to build; Mr. Bench said it was twelve
feet, seven inches.

Mayor Brunst opened the public hearing. Hearing no comments Mayor Brunst closed the public
hearing and brought the item back to the Council for further discussion. Mr. Spencer thanked Mr.
Bench and Mr. Clark for working with the applicant on this proposal.

Mayor Brunst moved, by ordinance, to amending Sections 22-24-3(F)(1)(a) and 22-24-3(F)(2)(d)

Mr. Bench said this item was continued from the June 30, 2020 meeting to allow the applicant,
German Pardo, and Council additional time to review the request.

Mr. Pardo’s existing business was located nearby the proposed site on State Street. In order to
expand his current business, he was proposing to move it entirely to this new location. He was not
proposing any changes to the site; it would remain as is. However, because the site was in a
residential zone, he was required to receive conditional use approval. Commercial daycares were
permitted in a residential zone provided they were located either on a collector or arterial street.
Both 400 South and Main Street were classified as collector streets. A commercial daycare facility
must also comply with the requirements of Section 22-6-9(G) which were part of the report for
Council review. Staff had determined the proposed site complied with the requirements of 22-6-
9(G) subject to several conditions of approval as outlined below.

As part of the requirements of the conditional use permit the applicant submitted a professional
traffic study that outlined the requirements of Section 22-6-9(G)(13) as follows:
    1. No more than two car trips per hour shall be generated before 5:30am or after 9:00 pm.
    2. No vehicle will back out onto a dedicated street.
    3. Persons who arrive at the facility shall be legally parked on the lot or on that part of the
         street which immediately abuts the lot.
    4. Ingress, egress, drop-off areas, traffic flow, and parking spaces will be contained on the
         site and will not adversely affect surrounding properties.

The submitted traffic study showed that the facility was going to require a minimum of twenty-
four (24) stalls based upon one hundred (100) children attending the day care. The study also found
that they would not generate more than two trips in the unpermitted times and that one of the stalls
in the parking lot would be required to be used as a back-up area. If you combined the total stalls
in both the existing parking lot and the on-street parking and removed the required back-up stall,
twenty-six (26) stalls were available.

The City’s traffic engineer, Brady Hale, had analyzed the clear vision requirements for the
intersection of Main Street and 400 South to determine whether all of the on-street parking on 400
South that the applicant had included as part of his available parking would actually be available.
Mr. Hale shared a diagram with his findings and discussed the parking situation, line of sight and
clear vision areas, and egress and ingress for the proposal. In response to Mrs. Lauret’s question,
he said the places where parking would not be permitted would be red curb. She asked how many
spaces were eliminated from the on-street parking; Mr. Hale said it was four eliminated from 400
South and two from Main Street. Mr. Hale explained the process by which parking ratios were
determined for commercial enterprises like the Smiling Faces Day Care Center. Discussion
continued regarding available parking.

Mr. Bench shared the recommendation of the Planning Commission for approval of a conditional
use permit for the commercial daycare, Smiling Faces, located at 410 S Main Street in the R8 zone
with the following conditions:

Mayor Brunst clarified that a parking ratio of 0.20 stalls per student equated to 100 students
allowed throughout the day, while a ratio of 0.24 equated to 83 students. Mayor Brunst gave time
to the applicant’s attorney, AJ Pepper with Snell & Wilmer, who wished to provide additional
information. Mr. Pepper presented his client’s case as to why the conditional use permit (CUP)
should be allowed, citing legal standards and case studies. He believed his client, German Pardo,
had satisfied all applicable requirements and standards for a CUP in this area. Mr. Pepper also felt
some of the standards determined by the Institute of Transportation Engineers (ITE) did not apply
in this situation and should not be a requirement for compliance.

Mayor Brunst said he had received a letter from Simon Bolivar, program administrator for the
Utah Child Care Licensing Team, which said the children and families served at the Smiling Faces
Day Care Center would be better served at the proposed new facility because it had more space
and was further from major streets. Mayor Brunst read the remainder of the letter at the request of
Mr. Spencer. Mayor Brunst said he was comfortable with the proposal and the conditions
established by the Planning Commission.

Mr. Macdonald said he felt like the position the applicant’s attorney laid out implied that the
Council had no choice to approve this CUP, and Mr. Macdonald wanted to hear what the city
attorneys thought of the argument and the Council’s responsibilities in this matter. Mr. Earl
responded to the points Mr. Pepper made and said he did not feel it was all the correct legal
standing. Mr. Earl opined that there were factors outlined in code the City Council should study
when considering conditional use permits. It was completely legitimate for the Council to consider
the traffic safety, clear vision areas, adequate parking, etc. as they had done; those factors could
impact the health, safety, and general welfare of the city. Mr. Earl further stated that it was entirely
appropriate for the City Council to rely on national ITE standards for these determinations, despite
arguments to the contrary.

Mr. Spencer wanted to clarify that his concerns with the proposal had nothing to do with the quality
of the daycare business itself, but were specifically tied to safety concerns with parking, line of
sight, and traffic. He understood that the daycare center staggered the pick-up and drop-off of
students, but if the number of children present each day was going to double Mr. Spencer was not
sure they could sustain the staggered intervals at the same level. He felt the request to have 100

Mr. Peterson echoed Mr. Spencer’s comments about his concerns and keeping children safe. He

Mrs. Lauret said she conducted her own visit and studied the parking and traffic during peak pick-

Mr. Sumner said he was torn on the matter because there seemed to be different scenarios being

Discussion continued regarding the ITE standards and the diagram produced by the traffic

said they had talked extensively about employee parking; the maximum demand for employees
would be six or seven, and they would utilize the on-street parking stalls.

Mr. Pepper clarified the applicant’s request, which included a parking ratio of 0.20 stalls per child
which would allow for a maximum of 100 children. They felt this was a fair compromise, and that
the CUP made the property more valuable. It was not in the interest of the applicant to step on or
ignore approved conditions, lest his CUP be revoked. Mr. Spencer asked Mr. Earl if the Council
could approve the CUP with one parking ratio condition and then adjust in the future if needed.
Mr. Earl said there was a procedure for amending or revoking a CUP if the Council felt it necessary
and wished to initiate that process based on investigation.

Over the course of the Planning Commission meeting and two City Council meetings, multiple
people have mentioned that Orem needs more daycare and that this business is well-managed
and a valued contributor to the Orem community. I’m not questioning those assertions but they
are not pertinent to this issue. The issue is whether this location can support the business at its
requested client capacity without affecting the safety of the neighbors, daycare clients, and pass-
thru traffic. Nothing I’ve observed during this two week continuation period changes the
parking, traffic, and safety concerns previously expressed to the Council. These concerns are
based on 30 years of experience living in my current home (411 South 60 West) directly west of
the property. In short, this is an increasingly traveled and congested area with poor visibility that
has already been negatively impacted by the Midtown Village development and overall traffic
increases; adding parking and traffic from a business that marginally meets the requirements
only escalates the safety hazards in the area. I ask you not to ignore these safety concerns voiced
from the experience of multiple longtime neighbors by shoehorning this business into this
location.
