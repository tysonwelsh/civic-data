       a.        Utah League of Cities and Towns (“ULCT”) Priorities – Cameron Diehl – Executive
                 Director.
Mayor Dahle introduced Cameron Diehl, ULCT Executive Director, and thanked him for his
participation in the City Council Meeting. Mr. Diehl reported that he has a family history in the City
of Holladay. He has been with the ULCT on and off since 2006 and had the opportunity to present
to the Holladay City Council many times over the years. Mr. Diehl reported that nearly half of the
cities in Salt Lake County will soon have new mayors. Additionally, the level of turnover seen in the
last four years was historic. He noted that the turnover rate was taking place at the same time as
massive population growth. High population growth rates contributed to difficulties for elected
officials.

Mr. Diehl noted that when Ms. Chamness invited him to speak, the intention was to provide
information about what to expect during the 2022 Legislative Session. He reported that population
growth was a significant issue and impacted what would need to be done to address housing
affordability, homelessness, and infrastructure costs. With regard to housing, several years ago, the
ULCT proposed Legislation, which was Senate Bill (“SB”) 34. SB-34 required all cities above a
certain size to focus on moderate-income housing plans and was designed with flexibility in mind, as
a one-size-fits-all approach would not work. The intention was to learn lessons from the
implementation of SB-34 and build on that knowledge.

Earlier in the week, the ULCT Legislative Policy Committee gave preliminary approval to SB-34+.
The idea behind SB-34+ was to build upon what had already been done. Mr. Diehl explained that the
reporting mechanism that was created with SB-34 was non-functional. It was important to ensure
that moving forward the report was focused on the implementation side to determine which strategies
were working. This would improve the effectiveness. Another key component of SB-34+ was to
state that if a city went above and beyond the three required Menu Items, there should be recognition
at the State level. That recognition could allow a city to be prioritized for State funding.

        b.     Highland Drive Corridor Study Update – Horrocks Engineering.
Mayor Dahle reported that Katie Kourianos and Alexis Verson from Horrocks Engineering would
share an update on the Highland Drive Corridor Study. Ms. Kourianos explained that the goal of the
presentation was to inform the Council of public feedback and the traffic analysis. In addition, there
would be a conversation about the best path forward. During the last presentation, five cross-sections
were presented for Highland Drive and the City Council narrowed them down to two cross-sections.
Those two cross-sections were then shared with the public to obtain feedback. Additional data
collection and traffic modeling were conducted as a result of that feedback.

Ms. Kourianos reported that there were approximately 430 responses to the survey. 42% of those
responses preferred Alternative 1: Complete Street and 52% preferred Alternative 2: Five Lane Cross
Section with Multi-Use Pathway. The different forms of outreach were overviewed, and it was
reported that there was a direct mailing campaign, the study area was canvassed, there were email
updates, and outreach was done through City and third-party social media channels. Ms. Kourianos
noted that the survey asked the following questions:

   •   How do you currently use Highland Drive between Van Winkle Expressway and Arbor Lane?
       and
   •   If some of the transportation amenities mentioned in question five were implemented, how
       would you use Highland Drive?

Most respondents drive to businesses on the corridor or commute through the corridor. As far as
future use, if some of the transportation improvements were implemented, respondents stated that
they would walk and bike more on the corridor. The survey also asked respondents to rank
transportation amenities in terms of preference. There were seven categories that were ranked on a
scale of 1 to 7. Those rankings were transitioned into scores to create the highest average.
Ms. Kourianos reported that a center turn lane had the highest score, followed by preserving travel
lanes for cars and enhanced bicycle/pedestrian crossing.

The data was broken down to compare the rankings to the respondent age groups. Respondents
between the ages of 18 and 35 ranked preserving travel lanes for cars in last place. Alternatively,
respondents between the ages of 35 to 65+ ranked preserving travel lanes for cars in first place. The
data was also broken down to compare the use to the rankings. Those that live, work, or visit
businesses in the corridor ranked preserving travel lanes the highest, and those who biked or accessed
transit ranked preserving travel lanes in last place. There were also some open-ended questions

included in the survey and some of those trends were shared. Ms. Kourianos reported that the need
for a more inviting environment for bicyclists and pedestrians was a common theme.

Ms. Kourianos explained that in addition to the public feedback, there was data analysis and traffic
engineering that needed to be considered. That was what Horrocks Engineering had been working
on over the last several weeks. Ms. Verson noted that during the last presentation, there had been a
productive conversation about what the alternatives would mean for the community. Horrocks

       d.      Housing Plan Update – LaNiece Davenport.
               •       Education Plan on Affordable Housing.
Ms. Davenport reported that the discussions would focus on how to spend the Redevelopment Agency
(“RDA”) housing set-aside funds and there would also be a presentation on affordable housing. She
reported that when RDA areas were created, there were certain housing elements associated with each
of those areas. For instance, when it came to one of the RDA project areas in the City of Holladay,
100 units of moderate-income housing needed to be created by 2030.

Ms. Davenport discussed Area Median Income (“AMI”) and explained that it is a number that came
from the County and is based on household size. The AMI for a family of three in Salt Lake County
is $80,000 and an 80% AMI is $64,000. Ms. Davenport stated that affordable housing means that a
family should not spend more than 30% of their income on housing expenses, including utilities. For
80% AMI, a family of three in Salt Lake County should not spend more than $1,600 on rent and

       e.      Discussion on Holladay Business Association.
Ms. Davenport explained that the intention was to share information on the next steps as it related to
economic opportunity and economic development in the City. Ms. Davenport noted that it was
important to understand the changing dynamic of the Chamber of Commerce and overviewed various
models, such as Local Chambers of Commerce, Regional Chambers of Commerce, State-wide
Chambers of Commerce, and City-Led Business Councils/Associations. Millcreek and Cottonwood
Heights had the latter. Ms. Davenport explained that a Business Association was something that the
City was considering because the existing model was not working effectively. Some benefits
associated with a Business Association were shared with Council.

Ms. Davenport outlined possible next steps if the City Council was interested in moving forward with
a Business Association. The beginning of 2022 would focus on the approval of the Business
Association as well as membership, structure, and staffing. The City would also need to create a
meeting schedule, designate or hire a member of City Staff to handle Business Association needs,
and survey local businesses to better understand their overall needs and goals.

Holladay Chamber of Commerce President, Jason Woodland, introduced himself to the Council. He
explained that his objective in 2020 was to create a succession plan for the Chamber of Commerce,
but the COVID-19 pandemic delayed that plan. As a result, he spent time thinking about what the
future of a smaller Chamber of Commerce could look like. The value proposition for a Chamber of
Commerce included ribbon cuttings, serving as the local liaison between the business community and
