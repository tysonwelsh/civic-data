7.     Discussion Relating to Ranked-Choice Voting, Presenters, Council Member Traci
       Crockett and Joshua Daniels, Chief Deputy Clerk/Auditor, Utah County.

Council Member Crockett introduced Joshua Daniels and Amelia Gardner Powers from Utah
County. Ms. Powers described Ranked-Choice Voting, which became legal in Utah in 2019. Two
cities in Utah County were the first in the State to participate in the pilot program. Ranked-Choice
voting is a method of voting that eliminates the primary election and voters rank their top
candidates in a single election.

The two cities where the pilot voting program was conducted have two very different
demographics. Payson is more rural and established with long-term residents. Vineyard City, on
the other hand, has more high-density housing, young families, and has a predominantly millennial
population. Both communities embraced the change and the switch from a traditional election to
Ranked-Choice Voting went smoothly and both cities only had to hold one election, which reduced
their costs by one-half.

Ms. Powers stated that for city and local elections, Ranked-Choice Voting is "better, faster and
cheaper" than traditional elections. It is better because the citizens like being able to rank the
candidates, which helps the electorate feel like they are being better represented. Additionally, no
one feels like they have to vote for the lesser of two evils, which increases engagement in the
voting process.

Ms. Powers reported that Ranked Choice elections cost half of what cities would normally incur
for traditional elections and it is not difficult to implement. Ms. Powers stated that she and
Mr. Daniels took office in 2019 and were able to successfully run two Ranked-Choice Voting
elections their first year in office. She added that Ranked Choice elections give people the ability
to feel represented in a non-partisan city race.

Mayor Timothy asked about the ability of the equipment to handle Ranked-Choice Voting from
other counties. Ms. Powers stated that in 2020 they processed 292,000 ballots. Previously, the
largest election handled approximately 200,000 ballots. Even processing nearly 100,000 more
ballots, they had remaining bandwidth and could have easily handled another 50,000 ballots. She
did not foresee any municipal election overwhelming them and noted that there tends to be less
voter turnout in a municipal cycle. She reported that they laid out the ballot center like a

Mr. Daniels added that they were able to do Ranked-Choice Voting with their existing equipment

Mr. Daniels explained that the process is still largely the same as traditional voting. The paper
ballot contains a grid where the names of the candidates are listed. Rather than simply selecting
one candidate, a voter ranks their choices in a column. They can rank a certain number of
candidates based on the dimensions of the ballot. In the case of Salt Lake County' s ballot style,
voters are likely to be able to rank up to 10 candidates. Mr. Daniels explained that voters are not
required to rank all of the candidates.

The counting process was next described. They still go through the process of verifying the
identity of the voter and scanning the votes. The master data file at the end of the ballot then
automates the counting of the ranked votes until one of the candidates receives a majority of the
votes.

In response to an inquiry, Ms. Powers stated that Bluffdale could choose to run Ranked-Choice
Voting through Utah County because a small portion of the City is located in Utah County.

Council Member Kallas applauded Utah County's efforts to implement Ranked-Choice Voting.
He asked if the electorate sees the series of results through each round of counting. Ms. Powers
stated it would be the same process with Ranked-Choice Voting. They used the same tools for
their elections in Utah County that they used for the Republican party in the Federal election.
Ms. Powers also stated that Bluffdale could have Utah County run their elections this year, and the
next election have them run by Salt Lake County.

In response to a question from Council Member Kallas regarding ballot space, Ms. Powers stated
that they have accommodated up to 17 candidates on one ballot. Mr. Daniels explained that even
if there were 100 candidates, they can accommodate Ranked-Choice Voting. The only difference
would be that not all of the candidates would appear on the first page. He explained that Salt Lake
County's system only accommodates the ranking of up to 10 candidates. Most voters would likely
drop off in their rankings for any more than 5 to 8 candidates.

Ms. Powers added that State law contemplates that not all candidates will be ranked. A
municipality could, for example, limit the number of rankings allowed, notwithstanding the
number of candidates.

In response to an inquiry, Ms. Powers stated that instances where people incorrectly filled out their
Ranked Choice ballot did not vary significantly from traditional ballots. There is a higher rate of
adjudication, where they have to acknowledge that a voter did not rank all of the listed candidates.

Council Member Aston raised the issue of employment contracts for department heads. It was
noted that all other employees have certain protections for employment. She did not want to take
away any authority from the City Council to dismiss or tenninate an employee but she felt it was
important to have a contract associated with those positions so that they know what they can expect

Council Member Gaston requested clarification of what type of contract terms are contemplated.
Currently, it is up to the City Council to determine any severance or other provisions upon
termination. He expressed support for the idea that a contract specify that a department head is
not entitled to anything upon termination but the Council would still have the discretion to provide
severance.

Council Member Kallas stated that he has seen standard employment contracts in other
jurisdictions that simply provide that the Council has the discretion to tenninate and make other
employment-related detenninations. Council Member Crockett commented that such language
could be construed as a benefit to a position but she also did not want to take away the authority
from the Council.

City Attorney, Todd Sheeran described the difference between a merit employee and an at-will
employee. Municipalities employ both merit and at-will employees. At-will employees are those
who are appointed or elected, including department heads, part-time seasonal workers, the City
Manager, and others listed in the statute. Merit employees are full-time employees who can only
be terminated for cause or due to a reduction in the workforce. Terminating a merit employee
without cause creates potentially significant liability for the City. Mr. Sheeran further explained
that at-will employees can be te1minated at any time, for any reason.

Typically, municipalities have several at-will positions for which they also have policies or
employment agreements in place that make a distinction between termination for cause, or

tennination at-will. Depending on the type of termination, the employee has different severance
possibilities available. Mr. Sheeran relayed the opinion of one City Manager who stated that
employment contracts serve as a protection to the employee and the City because they help each
side understand the tenns of the employment, which are different from merit employees, and which
can make for a much more amenable separation if that occurs.

Mr. Sheeran stated that employment contracts serve as an investment in City employees by
attracting and retaining talent. Employment contracts also allow the City to be competitive with
other cities and boost employee productivity and morale. He addressed the typical terms of an
employment contract dealing with duration, which could either be continuous employment subject

Mr. Sheeran reported that employment contracts often include the duties of the employee, and

Depending on what the person that is being sought has had in the past, the City can be creative in

The crnx of the contract is the language regarding termination. He stressed that with at-will
employees, the language would state that they can be terminated at any time and include
protections should a new City Council come on board during the term of the employment. He
cited the example that a contract could provide that the City Manager cannot be terminated within
six months of his or her election. This allows the Council to know the employee and feel
comfortable with them as a City employee.

The contract could also define "cause." Some cities define "cause" as the conviction of a felony
that would allow for tennination with no severance. There could also be provisions defining
"without cause," what happens upon disability or death, and resignation from employment. He
emphasized that severance depends on how the tennination provision is worded.
