Mayor Hall reported that during the Work Session, there would be a presentation with updates to the
Bluffdale Parks, Trails, Recreation, and Open Space Master Plan by Landmark Design as well as
survey results from Y2 Analytics. After the presentation, there would be a discussion between the
City Council and Planning Commission Members. It was noted that Lisa Benson, the Consultant
from Landmark Design, was present at the meeting as well as Kyrene Gibb from Y2 Analytics.

Ms. Benson shared information about the Bluffdale Parks, Trails, Recreation, and Open Space
Master Plan. She reviewed a diagram that summarized the public engagement approach for the
project. There was a booth at the Love Where You Live event in the spring, Y2 Analytics
conducted a scientific survey, and work was done with an Advisory Committee. A project website
was available for residents to leave comments or obtain additional information. Ms. Benson
reported that on the website there was an interactive mapping tool called Social Pinpoint. It allowed
people to drag and drop comments. Toward the end of the process, there would be a Draft Plan
Open House and work would be done with the Advisory Committee and leadership to make sure
that the plan adequately represents the vision ofthe City moving forward.

Additional information about the project website was shared. Ms. Benson reported that there were
565 page views, 288 of which were unique visitors. 29 people signed up for the email list and two
comments were submitted through the comment form. The comments addressed the desire for an
archery range and better connections on the west side of the City. The Social Pinpoint Interactive
Map was reviewed. Several ideas, suggestions, and concerns were submitted. The opportunities
that arose from the mapping exercise pertained to pedestrian connectivity and safety (safe crossings
and connected sidewalks, safe routes to school, utilizing the canals) and recreation (making sure
there were connections to the Jordan River Trail and the existing trail network). As for the Jordan
River itself, there was a desire to protect the river, the trail, and the open space in that corridor.
Completing Day Ranch Park and adding more seating and trash receptacles was also suggested. For

Independence Park, there was a request to add pickleball and basketball courts. There was also a
general comment asking that the safe sight triangles be maintained.

In the spring, a lot ofinput was received from youth at the Love Where You Live event. Attendees
were asked what recreation amenities were most important to them and some great feedback was
received. Swimming pools and splash pads were the most popular responses followed by
playgrounds and walking/bicycle trails. Another prompt asked attendees how they normally reach
their favorite park, trail, or recreation facility. Bicycling or another user-powered mode was the
most popular answer followed by walking and driving. A question was also raised regarding how
frequently people use City amenities. The most common answers were daily and weekly, which
means the amenities are fairly highly used. Attendees were also asked how important it is to have
parks or trails within walking distance to home. The overwhelming response was it was extremely
important.


Ms. Benson shared the Preliminary Park Analysis with those present. When determining park
needs, it is necessary to do a Level of Service ("LOS") analysis, which is a ratio of the number of
acres per 1,000 residents in the City. The Amenity LOS was also examined, which looked at
individual fields and court types to see what the population numbers were per amenity. That work
was still underway. In addition to the LOS Analysis and Amenity LOS Analysis, a Distribution
Analysis was needed that looked at how accessible parks were in the community.

For the LOS, the population projections were examined. Currently, the City has just over 17,000
residents. The General Plan listed the estimated population for 2050 as approximately 32,000
residents. The Wasatch Front Regional Council("WFRC")population projections were also taken
into account. The projections were not significantly different, with the WFRC population estimate
by 2050 being 30,146. After discussions with City Staff, the decision was made to use the General
Plan projections to account for the possibility of additional population growth.


Ms. Benson shared a map that showed all of the existing parks and open space in the community.
The current LOS is 4.5 acres per 1,000 residents. If no additional parks are developed in the future,
by 2034 the LOS would drop to 3.6 and by 2050 it would drop to 2.7. Ms. Benson shared

As for the future LOS for parks, if Bluffdale decides to maintain the current LOS of 4.5 acres per
1,000 people, in 2034,there would need to be a total acreage of 108.6. The existing park acres were
86, which meant 22.6 acres would need to be added. When looking out to 2050, that total number
jumped to 144.8, which meant the City would need 36.2 additional acres to stay at 4.5. This was the
ideal time for the City to think about what the community wants in the future. It is fairly common
for cities to have their LOS drop slightly over time as development occurs. The availability ofland
could be a challenge but acreage could come from Development Agreements or other negotiations.
It wasn't necessarily land that the City needs to purchase outright. Ms.Benson shared a map ofthe

data about trailheads and access points.

Ms. Gibb shared information about the Bluffdale City Parks, Trails, and Recreation Survey. She
discussed the methodology and explained that a random address-based sample was done of
households in Bluffdale. 711 residents were sampled in total. That sample was largely drawn from
the City utilities list, so it was recognized that the results would be more homeowner-heavy. To
address that, registered voter households that were likely renter addresses were also included.
Additional address-based sampling was done. Invitations to participate in the online survey were
sent via email and text message. The survey was out for approximately two weeks, from mid-
August to early September. A sample of 711 residents in a City the size of Bluffdale resulted in a
margin of error of 3.6 percentage points. The sample provided a good statistically valid industry

ofresident demographics,such as age, gender, and ethnicity.


Some of the key findings were reviewed.           Overall, resident satisfaction and utilization of
recreational amenities in Bluffdale were quite high. 77% of residents used Bluffdale parks at least
once per month and 66% indicated that they used City trails at least once a month. It was a very
active community in Bluffdale. The primary driver for which parks and trails residents used most
often had to do with the distance fi'om their homes. Residents were looking for conveniently
located recreational amenities. Council Member Kallas asked if there were similar statistics in other

communities. Ms. Gibb explained that the numbers were quite high, especially when compared to
the national average for park usage. Within Utah and along the Wasatch Front, the numbers were
normally slightly higher than the average, but not necessarily significantly so.


Most residents were happy with the number of parks available in the City. There were enough
recreational amenities to meet the needs of residents. Residents believed there were increased

opportunities for recreation programs, trails, and natural open spaces. Residents wanted to see
increased connectivity in the trails throughout the City as well as additional lighting, more trees,
shade structures, and overall maintenance/cleanliness. Most residents were interested in recreation
programs in the City for both youths and adults. Residents surveyed were most interest in soccer,
basketball, and swimming, while pickleball was the most popular adult recreation program.

In terms of recreational facilities, residents were very interested in having a Recreation Center
within the City and were especially interested in a swimming pool. Two-thirds of residents
indicated that they would be willing to pay a monthly usage fee to help fund the developments and
improvements that were desired within the City. Most residents who responded to the survey stated
that if there was a Recreation Center available, there was interest in a family pass or a monthly pass
for admission. Ms. Gibb explained that this was not common among most of the cities that Y2
Anal)^ics surveyed. That level of interest as well as the willingness to pay for the service was very
promising. Additional information would be shared later on in the presentation.

Overall, 43% of residents indicated that they were very satisfied with City and County parks. 78%
were at least somewhat satisfied with the City and County parks in Bluffdale. As for trails, a total
of 25% were very satisfied and 60% were at least somewhat satisfied. 23% were very satisfied and
a total of 57% were at least somewhat satisfied with the open space in the community. The area
where Bluffdale fell below the majority satisfaction level was with recreation programs. Ms. Gibb
reported that the quality of life for residents overall was 80 out of 100 on average. She explained
that resident attitudes about parks were directly correlated with higher quality of life ratings. Parks,
trails, and recreation opportunities impacted residents daily. Nearly half of the residents (45%)
were interested in seeing more recreation programs offered in Bluffdale.

Residents were asked how important it was to them to have public trails within walking distance of
their home and parks within walking distance of their home. Approximately two-thirds of residents
indicated that both being close to where they live is very important. 9 in 10 residents stated that it
was at least somewhat important. Ms. Gibb reported that a few questions were asked about City
events. Two-thirds ofresidents indicated that they had participated in a City-sponsored event in the
past year. When residents were asked what types of events they were interested in seeing in the
future, there were preferences expressed for food trucks, something that included live music,
Bluffdale Days, movie nights, and farmers markets. Approximately half of the residents surveyed
were somewhat likely to volunteer for a City event.

Ms. Gibb shared additional information about City parks. 61% of respondents visited City parks a
few times a month. 77% indicated that they were visiting parks in the City at least once per month.
There were very high rates of participation. Only 2% of residents indicated that they never used or
visited parks in the City. Residents were asked which parks they had visited in the past year and
which were visited most often. More than half of the residents had visited the Wardle Fields

County Regional Park, Bluffdale City Park, Independence Park, and Day Ranch Park. Something
worth noting was that Vintage Park had a high rate of residents who used that park most frequently
compared to the total share of residents that were currently utilizing that park. 55% of residents
stated that having a park close to home was the primary driver in their choice of park. Sports fields,
courts, playground equipment, and other special features were other motivators.

Based on the survey results, the trees and the atmosphere at Bluffdale City Park were especially
appealing to residents. The playground equipment was a driver for those who visited Day Ranch
Park. As for Vintage Park, residents indicated that having a quieter space to recreate was appealing.
Trees and atmosphere were also mentioned concerning Independence Village Park. Ms. Gibb
shared information about the improvements residents wanted to see in City parks. The most
common requests were for trees and shade structures. However, some residents did not feel that
improvements were needed at the parks. There was a lot of happiness with the parks overall.
Continuing to take care ofthe recreational amenities and focus on maintenance was a priority.


Looking at trails, 56% of residents indicated that they had visited or used trails a few times a month
or more in the past year and two-thirds of residents used them once a month or more. 9% of
residents stated that they had not used trails in the City in the past year. Ms. Gibb explained that
some of the barriers to entry would be explored shortly. For the residents who were recreating on
City trails, walking or jogging was the most common use case. Recreational bicycling was the
second most common followed by commuting (bicycling) and equestrianism. For residents who
were not currently using trails to recreate within the City, a large share of those residents indicated
that they did not have a lot of information about where the trailheads were or how to access the
trails. The next most common barrier to entry related to the location ofthe access points.

Residents were asked which trails they used within the past year. 71% had used the Jordan River
Parkway, 50% had used the Independence Community Trails, and 40% had used the Porter
Rockwell Trail. 37% of residents stated that the Jordan River Parkway was their most frequently
used trail and 43% stated that the Independence Community Trails were their most frequently used
trails. When looking at the improvements that residents were interested in on the trail system,
linking neighborhoods with the trail system, increased lighting, maintenance to allow more winter
use, making trails more complete or connected, improved trail maintenance, and pet waste disposal
stations were the most highly requested improvements. Residents in Bluffdale were not particularly
interested in more concrete trails, but residents did want to see more asphalt trails. 21% wanted to
see a large increase in the length of natural surface trails in the community.

Ms. Gibb explained that residents were asked whether they felt there were conflicts on City trails
between different user groups. Most residents did not feel that was an issue. 44% of residents did
not see those t5^es of conflicts on City trails. She noted that this was not particularly common and

solution for those conflicts was to have designated trails for specific uses.

Survey results related to recreation programs were shared. 17% of residents indicated that a
member of their household had participated in Bluffdale youth soccer in the past year and 23% of
households stated that they had participated in adult or youth recreation programs in neighboring
cities. There was some drive for residents to look outside of Bluffdale to find recreation program
opportunities. The recreation programs that residents mentioned participating in outside of
Bluffdale included soccer, baseball,flag football, and basketball in Draper and Riverton.

Residents were asked how likely they were to participate in additional recreation programs if those
were offered in Bluffdale. Ms. Gibb pointed out that people were generally poor predictors of their
future behavior, but the question had been asked to gauge the level of interest that residents
expressed. 31% of residents stated that they or members of their household would be very likely to
participate in youth sports programs in Bluffdale. A total of57% would be at least somewhat likely
or somewhat interested. 19% of residents would be very likely and a total of 59% stated that they
would be at least somewhat likely to participate in adult sports programs in the City. When it came
to the most commonly requested adult programs, pickleball was at the top of the list. At the youth
level, the requests were for more soccer, basketball, swimming, and pickleball.

interested in having a public Recreation Center within Bluffdale City limits. The amenities that
they would most like to see in that Recreation Center included an indoor pool, weight or cardio

       8.1     Ordinance 2023-22- Amending the Bluffdale Employee Policies and Procedures
               Manual Related to Sick and Annual Leave Changing to PTO.(StaffPresenter,
               Bruce Kartchner)


Administrative Services Director, Bruce Kartchner, shared information about proposed amendments
to the Bluffdale Employee Policies and Procedures Manual. He reported that there had been
discussions about modifying the Sick and Annual Leave section of the manual. Some of the
policies in the manual did not align with how the industry at large handled things. The intention
was to structure it like many other agencies, combining both sick and annual leave into paid time off
("PTO"). People could then use that time off however they chose whether it be for sick days or
vacation days. After speaking to many new hires there were concerns that certain issues needed to
be dealt with but no time had been accrued. As a result, it was proposed that for the first year, five
days of vacation be preloaded so newer employees could deal with critical issues.


The structure was very similar in that as there were additional years of service the number
increased. Mr. Kartchner reported that the timeline of maxing out the amount of time off had been
shortened, so there was an incentive for people to stay. The incentive would be created earlier as
opposed to making it so far out that there was no real incentive for employees to remain long-term.
The first year was 20 days or four weeks of total time, which was the equivalent of two weeks of
vacation and two weeks of sick time. After that, it was 20 days accruing instead of the five pre
loaded plus the 15. There were increases by five at certain intervals. Mr. Kartchner noted that there
had been discussions that an additional week be allowed for the City Manager and Directors as
those employees were often handling different aspects of City work even when on vacation.

Historically, at the end of a career in the City, whether through retirement or leaving for another
reason, sick time would be forfeited and vacation time would be paid out. Mr. Kartchner reported
that when someone was about to leave their employment, a lot of those sick days were used, so the
value of the employee was not received during that time. Rather than dealing with that issue, it
made sense to pay out both the vacation and sick days. Since there was an accrual, the maximum
number of hours there could be at the end of any given year would be maxed out at 520 hours. The
bulk of the amendments would reflect the change in vacation being called PTO and eliminating the

                               COMBINED MEETING MINUTES

sick leave language. Section 130 would now become the PTO Section and incorporate the changes.
The sick leave section had been deleted in its entirety because it would no longer apply.

Mr. Kartchner discussed the hiring process. There are times when the new hire might have a
significant amount of experience somewhere else. Those people were not expecting to shift from
four weeks of vacation back to two. As a result, language had been added to state that the City
Manager or designee could negotiate or modify the initial allocation of PTO hours to reflect that.
Mr. Reid reported that it had become a negotiation point. A candidate might insist on having what
