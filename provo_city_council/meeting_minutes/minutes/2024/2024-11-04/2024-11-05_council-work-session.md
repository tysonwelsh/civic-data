Item 2: A discussion regarding the Stormwater Management Program and SWPPP
changes (24-090) 0:14:12

Carlos, Public Works Engineer, presented on the Stormwater Management Program and
upcoming changes to the Storm Water Pollution Prevention Plan (SWPPP) requirements.

Carlos explained that a new law in Utah, effective January 1, 2025, introduces updated
regulations for municipalities concerning stormwater pollution prevention. He provided an
overview of both state and city-level adjustments necessitated by these new regulations,
highlighting areas of potential impact on Provo City’s stormwater management practices.

Carlos noted that despite numerous violations observed in this area, no fines are currently
imposed for non-compliance. Chair MacKay inquired about Carlos's opinion on the absence of
fines, to which he responded that he did not favor this approach. Chair MacKay then asked if
non-compliance with stormwater regulations was a significant issue in the city. Carlos responded
that, while non-compliance exists, there are additional tools and strategies in place to ensure the
city achieves the necessary compliance without fines.

Item 4: A discussion regarding the Provo Airport Snow Removal Equipment (SRE)
Building (24-086) 0:24:44

Brian Torgersen, Public Works Division Director - Airport, presented a proposal for an interfund
loan to fund a new Snow Removal Equipment (SRE) building at the Provo Airport and provided
background on the airport’s snow removal program. Mr. Torgersen explained that airport
runways must be cleared within 30 minutes to avoid delays, but much of the equipment is
inadequate to meet this requirement. He showed images of the outdated equipment and their
functions, as well as recent acquisitions, including a new $2 million vehicle that currently lacks a
storage facility. To address this, a temporary arrangement has been made with a private hangar to
store the new equipment over the winter.

Mr. Torgersen noted that it has been two years since a flight delay occurred due to insufficient
snow removal. He also shared site plans, design images, and maps of the proposed building’s
location. Funding for the building includes $8.7 million from the Federal Aviation
Administration (FAA) and $7.9 million from Provo’s Passenger Facility Charge (PFC) revenue.
Chair MacKay clarified that FAA funding requires individual project applications, and she
inquired about the project’s completion timeline, which Mr. Torgersen estimated at four years.

Mr. Torgersen invited John Borget, Administrative Services Director, to discuss the funding
strategy. Mr. Borget recommended two interfund loans: one supported by selling property in the
Mountain Vista area originally intended for economic development, and another drawing $3.5
million from the Energy Fund Loan. Councilor Handley asked about the current balance in the
Mountain Vista fund. Mr. Borget replied that it contains approximately $4 million, and he added
that an outstanding $2 million loan for a golf course development has yet to be utilized.

Councilor Bogdin asked whether the Mountain Vista and golf course projects were connected to
the medical college. Mr. Borget confirmed, explaining that the additional property tax revenue
from housing development near the medical college would help repay this loan. Councilor
Bogdin also inquired about the Energy Fund Loan. Mr. Borget explained that the Energy
Department would issue this loan, noting that the department’s business approach involves
charging interest on funds to offset potential returns from alternative investments.

The discussion then turned to the Energy Department's reserve levels, as the department’s state
standard is to reach a 365-day reserve goal for operational security. Mr. Bunker, Energy
Director, noted that while a 365-day reserve is ideal, the current reserve goal is at least 120 days.

At present, the department’s reserves stand at 102 days. Mr. Borget indicated that using $3.5
million from the Energy Fund for the SRE building would reduce the reserves to approximately
80 days—significantly below the recommended 120 days. Despite this, Mr. Borget and the
Energy Board continue to support the loan, as interfund loans provide flexibility and can be

Vice-Chair Whipple inquired about the funding required to meet the 365-day reserve goal, which

Vice-Chair Whipple asked about alternative funding options if the Energy Fund were not used.
Mr. Borget recommended exploring external financing, though he had not yet assessed the costs.
Councilor Christensen then asked if interfund loans offer the city greater flexibility, and Mr.
Borget affirmed, stating that interfund loans allow for faster repayment as long as balances
remain adequate.



Item 5: A presentation regarding the Wastewater Project financial update (24-048) 1:03:48

Gordon Haight, Public Works Director, opened with enthusiasm for the progress on Provo’s
Wastewater Project. He introduced Dave Michaelsen, Engineer, to provide background on the
project.

Mr. Michaelsen began by outlining the history of the city’s wastewater reclamation plant,
showing an image of the older facility and explaining how new regulatory standards have
rendered it inadequate. He described the planning process for meeting these standards, during
which the City Council directed the creation of a new plant on the existing site, utilizing a
membrane bioreactor system. He noted that the new plant would also address public concerns
regarding Utah Lake algae by reducing phosphorus and nitrogen outputs.

The initial compliance deadline for the standards was January 1, 2020, but the city requested an
extension to January 1, 2025. In 2020, the Mayor and City Council approved funding for facility
updates, making Provo one of the first cities to commit to meeting these stricter regulations. The



Provo City Municipal Council Work Meeting – November 5, 2024                            Page 4 of 13
original project cost estimate was $120 million, but the Water Quality Board only allocated $15
million. This funding shortfall led to necessary cuts, including the removal of landscape
maintenance, administrative, lab, and collections facilities, and some bioreactor components. Mr.
Michaelsen noted that the state approved these temporary reductions, acknowledging Provo’s
financial constraints. Over time, additional funding allowed for the partial restoration of some
bioreactors, but he expressed concern about the plant’s vulnerability to a catastrophic event due
to the limited resources.

Mr. Michaelsen thanked the Mayor for her role on the Water Quality Board, which provided
some additional funding, though a significant funding gap remains. He highlighted the cost-
efficiency of Provo’s project, comparing it to a similar plant in Spanish Fork, which is two-thirds
the size but equally costly. This accomplishment has drawn interest from other municipalities.

Jimmy McKnight, Public Works Business Manager, then discussed the financial strategy for the
project. He outlined various loans and agreements that minimize interest payments, reducing
overall project costs. He proposed issuing a revenue bond to distribute costs over time, which
would help defer rate increases for several years.

Chair MacKay asked how the bond would impact citizens' bills, and Mr. McKnight confirmed
that the bond would not require a rate increase. Vice-Chair Whipple inquired about the plant’s
anticipated lifespan, to which Mr. McKnight responded that, given the facility’s robust design, it
should last at least the 20-year duration of the bond, noting that Provo’s existing plants are
currently 50–70 years old. Councilor Bogdin asked if savings from the current "pay-as-you-go"
approach would help finance the bond, and she also inquired about the plant’s service life. Gary
Calder, Public Works Division Director – Water, confirmed the plant is designed with a 50-year
lifespan, while Mr. Michaelsen noted that the selected technology supports compliance with
current phosphorus and nitrogen standards, as well as potential future potable water reuse.

Mayor Kaufusi expressed support for eliminating phosphorus and nitrogen discharges into Utah
Lake, reinforcing the environmental impact of the project. Councilor Bogdin further asked how
long the plant could accommodate population growth before requiring expansion. Mr.
Michaelsen indicated that the current three bioreactors are designed to support Provo’s
population, with capacity for an additional 25% growth.

Vice-Chair Whipple asked for details on the bond. Mr. McKnight assured that the bond structure
enables Provo to pay current prices rather than deferring costs until construction is more
expensive. Dan Follett, Finance Director, then elaborated on the $29 million bond's structure,
noting its tax-exempt status, lower interest rates, and maturity date of September 1, 2044. Vice-
Chair Whipple asked if the bond could be paid off early, and Mr. Follett confirmed that a call
option is available after 10 years, allowing for early repayment.

Item 10: A discussion regarding solar rates (24-094) 2:49:01

Scott Bunker, Energy Director, presented options for adjusting the compensation rate the city
pays to solar customers for surplus energy they generate and sell back to the city. He outlined
three proposed options for council consideration:

    1. Option 1: Sunset the current rate of $0.10 per kilowatt hour (kWh) for grandfathered
       customers, lowering it to $0.067 per kWh.
    2. Option 2: Sunset the $0.10 rate for grandfathered customers to $0.067 per kWh hour and
       reduce the rate to $0.04 per kWh for all other customers.
    3. Option 3: Lower all rates to $0.04 per kWh, with no grandfathered rates.

Mr. Bunker provided a history of Provo’s solar rate discussions and reiterated the goal of
maintaining a reliable and affordable utility service. He presented data showing that Provo’s
energy rates are among the lowest in Utah and well below national averages. Using a timeline
from 1990 to 2023, he illustrated how electricity prices have remained relatively stable,
increasing only slightly, in contrast to other goods like gasoline and food.

To further illustrate Provo’s energy mix, Mr. Bunker showed two pie charts comparing energy
sources in 2020 and 2023. Solar energy now accounts for about 20% of Provo’s energy mix, and
natural gas has increased to 25%. He emphasized that even solar customers still rely on the city’s
grid, as solar systems alone are insufficient for uninterrupted energy needs.

Councilor Handley requested clarification on the percentage of an average $80 bill dedicated to
fuel acquisition, which Mr. Bunker answered. He then shared historical data on solar adoption in
Provo, showing an increase influenced by city and national incentives, along with the financial
impact of solar buyback programs on the city. To demonstrate potential impacts, Mr. Bunker
showed various rate scenarios and projected customer bill estimates under each option.

Chair MacKay asked for the recommendation of the Energy Board. Mr. Bunker reported that the

grandfathered customers. Councilor Christensen voiced support for reducing subsidies across

Councilor Handley inquired about how other utilities, such as Rocky Mountain Power, handle

Item 12: A discussion regarding vacant properties in the city (24-096) 4:49:00

David Pyle, Assistant Director of Development Services, presented on issues and potential
solutions related to vacant properties in Provo. He highlighted the main concerns surrounding
vacant properties: they pose safety risks, detract from neighborhood aesthetics, and represent an
unproductive use of land.

Mr. Pyle outlined several common causes for properties remaining vacant, including owners'
financial inability to renovate, unwillingness to sell due to sentimental attachment, or the desire
to hold onto the property for future market gains. He defined a vacant property as a developed
property with at least two of the five following conditions persisting for more than 180 days:

       Unlocked doors or open access points
       Inactive utilities
       Visible signs of neglect or lack of maintenance
       Absence of active marketing efforts to sell or rent the property
       Delinquent property taxes

To address these concerns, Mr. Pyle proposed several solutions:




Provo City Municipal Council Work Meeting – November 5, 2024                             Page 11 of 13
    1. Code Amendments – Modify city codes to require securing properties against entry,
       addressing pits and potholes for safety, and enforcing the replacement of damaged fences
       or walls.
    2. Renovation Loans – Provide loan options for owners needing financial assistance for
       property renovation.
    3. Registration Program – Implement a registration requirement for vacant properties,
       helping the city track and address vacant homes in need of attention.

Councilor Bogdin questioned whether property owners would participate in voluntarily notifying
the city about vacancies. Scott Johnson, Zoning Administrator, expressed support for the
registration program, though he noted challenges in identifying owners of properties registered
under LLCs, as these entities are sometimes dissolved.

Councilor Christensen asked how the registration program would address difficulties in locating

would assist with properties where ownership is known.

Mr. Johnson responded that these enforcement issues are complex and suggested that additional
staffing could be beneficial. Bill Peperone, Development Services Director, recommended
assigning existing code enforcement officers to specific geographical areas as a potential solution
before hiring additional staff.

Councilor Handley supported the registration program, noting that while it may not be a
complete solution, it could be a helpful tool. Chair MacKay asked Mr. Jones if the registration
idea would be viable. Mr. Jones agreed that it could be effective, though enforcing the 180-day
vacancy threshold could be challenging.

Motion:

Vice-Chair Whipple motioned to move forward with establishing a vacant property registration
program, and Councilor Bogdin seconded the motion. Councilor Bogdin further asked if Mr.
Johnson would be involved in the program's setup, to which Mr. Johnson confirmed his
involvement.

The motion passed with a unanimous vote of 7-0.

Vice-Chair Whipple questioned the feasibility of Azora’s capabilities, asking whether it seemed

Mr. Johnson also reviewed potential staffing needs, outlining the additional full-time employees
(FTEs) required to handle increased compliance demand.

Councilor Bogdin asked about funding availability for these technology and staffing options.
Councilor Christensen emphasized the city’s commitment to promoting owner occupancy,
expressing that a robust, data-driven system could prove invaluable for managing compliance in
the long term. He indicated a willingness to invest significantly in effective technology if it could
provide comprehensive oversight, assisting home buyers and addressing illegal rentals.

Councilor Bogdin suggested that Councilor Christensen’s expertise could provide valuable
insights in discussions with these data-scraping vendors. Councilor Hoban advocated for a cost-
effective approach, suggesting that achieving around 75% compliance at a lower cost could be
more practical. Vice-Chair Whipple reinforced the importance of addressing rental compliance
for the community, while Chair MacKay acknowledged that current enforcement processes are
insufficient.

Councilor Handley observed that many illegal rentals are spread by word of mouth, posing an
enforcement challenge that may not be fully solvable through technology alone.
