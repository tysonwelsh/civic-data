7.1 Discussion- Electric Bikes, Motorcycles, Golf Carts, UTVs and ATVs

(Presenter, Fred Donaldson).
City Attorney, Fred Donaldson, reported that there are many different types of e-bikes, e-
motorcycles, and other electric vehicles in the City. The presentation was intended to provide
information on current State and City laws regarding these vehicles.
Electric vehicles are regulated under Utah State Code §41-6a. Bluffdale City Code does not have
a specific section related to these vehicles but Bluffdale City Code §7.60.040 indicates the
following in regard to City parks:
e Unauthorized motor vehicles are prohibited.
e Rollerblades, bikes, scooters, and skateboards are prohibited in or on pavilions, plaza areas,
grass, tables, tennis courts, pickleball courts, membrane surfaces, and benches.

e Damaging the grass is prohibited.
Utah State Code defines e-bikes as electric-assisted bicycles that have working pedals and electric
motors up to 850 watts. Riders are required to follow all bicycle traffic and safety laws, and
sidewalks and trails that are prohibited to bicycles are also closed to e-bikes. They must be labeled
Class 1, Class 2, or Class 3, with different age and supervision requirements by class. Helmets are
only required for Class 3 riders under 21 years of age. Mr. Donaldson then reviewed the different
e-bike classes.
Class 1 and Class 2 e-bikes
e Equipped with a motor that may be used exclusively to propel the bicycle but stops
providing assistance at 20 miles per hour.
e Must be eight years old or older to ride with the motor engaged.
e Riders under 14 years of age are required to have adult supervision on public property,
including sidewalks.
e Can be ridden on roads, sidewalks, and trails.
e Cannot be ridden on park property or other city-owned open space.
In response to a question raised by Mayor Hall, Mr. Donaldson clarified that Class 1 e-bikes cannot
be ridden on most City property, but they can be ridden on City trails.
Class 3 e-bikes
e Provides assistance when the rider is pedaling and stops providing assistance at 28 miles
per hour.
e Equipped with a speedometer.
e Riders must be at least 16 years old, but an endorsement is not required.
e Can be ridden on roads, sidewalks, and trails.
e Cannot be ridden on park property or other city-owned open space.
e Riders under 21 years old are required to wear a helmet.

Mr. Kartchner reported that the City Council had previously discussed the possibility of reutilizing
water from the Jordan Basin Water Reclamation Facility. It is very clean water but has slightly
elevated salts. They were now at a critical point in the decision-making process.
Bluffdale is in a desert and as the City grows, its need for water will increase. The City has taken
advantage of its ability to secure various water rights in the past and maintains a balance in the
Water Fund for this purpose. Water purchased from the Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District
comes from the Central Utah Project (“CUP”), which is a water source outside the Great Salt Lake
Basin that was diverted into the Salt Lake Valley.
The City’s current water rights are 1,100 acre feet. WaterPro was moving forward on its project
to bring water into Draper and was ready to begin work on final plans for the pumphouse.
Bluffdale had the opportunity to join the project, which would save money because costs for the
electricity, water lines, and facility would be shared. Bluffdale would build its own pump room in
the building. The total project cost would be approximately $4 million, or $3,600 per acre-foot.
Mr. Kartchner reported that the City typically spends between $2,500 and $5,000 per acre-foot on
irrigation water, depending on the source and delivery method. The effluent water was of better
quality than most of that water, with the exception of the additional salts.
It was noted that the City previously purchased 200 acre feet of water rights from shallow wells
along the Jordan River, but that was not currently being utilized. Well locations could potentially
be identified near the infrastructure for this project.
Mr. Kartchner asked for Council input on the project. A bond is usually obtained for projects of
this size because future users will receive the most benefit. The Zone 2 water tank would be funded
through Impact Fees and bonding; the road to the site had been completed and was ready for
construction. Mr. Kartchner proposed that the tank project be delayed to coincide with the effluent
water project so a single set of bonds could be issued in 2026 and the projects could be completed
concurrently. The total bond was estimated to be between $10 million and $14 million, depending
on how much of the project was Impact Fee eligible.
