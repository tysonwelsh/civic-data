8.         A resolution approving the appropriation of $7.5 million in the Airport Fund, a $3.5 million
           interfund loan from the Energy Fund to the Airport Fund, and a $4.0 million interfund loan
           from the Mountain Vista fund to the Airport Fund. (24-086) 0:48:59

     Motion:       An implied motion to approve Resolution 2024-53, as currently constituted, has been

Brian Torgersen, Division Director of Public Services, provided an update on the airport's snow removal
operations and the need for improved equipment and storage facilities. The FAA requires the airport to
clear snow from priority surfaces, including the runway, taxiway, and terminal apron, within 30
minutes—covering the equivalent of 46 lane miles. Historically, the airport relied on outdated
equipment, including a 1957 army surplus snow blower. During an FAA inspection, Provo was warned
that its snow removal capabilities were insufficient, putting its 139 certification at risk. In response, the
airport has invested in new snow removal equipment, funded by excess airport revenue. However, the
equipment currently lacks proper storage, and the FAA mandates a dedicated facility to protect it from
the elements. To address this, the airport has designed a new snow removal equipment storage
building. Construction is set to begin immediately, with the project funded 50% by the FAA and 50%
through Provo Airport’s Passenger Facility Charge (PFC) revenue. Torgersen requested approval for a
$7.5 million loan to continue construction quickly. He assured the council that adjustments had been
made to prioritize repaying the energy department first and reaffirmed that the airport plans to take
over the annual property payment by FY 2027 to minimize the impact on the general fund.

Councilor Garrett asked Mr. Borget for insight into the solvency of the interfund loan and the ability of
the Mountain Vista fund to support these transfers while ensuring repayment within four years.

Gordon Haight, Public Works Director, presented. Gordon Haight, Public Works Director, expressed
appreciation for the opportunity to address the city council again. He mentioned that Dave Torgersen
would provide a brief explanation to address any questions, followed by Mr. Hunter for further details.
He also noted that Mr. McKnight would be available to handle any more challenging questions, ensuring
they were well-prepared.

David Torgersen, Water Reclamation Manager, explained that the Water Quality Board's new rule,
effective January 1, 2015, required phosphorus removal at the treatment plant, which the existing
facility could not meet. After extensive analysis, the decision was made to build a new facility at the
same location. In 2017, Provo was granted a variance extending the deadline for phosphorus removal to
2025 and received a $75 million loan and $2 million in principal forgiveness from the Water Quality
Board. Provo then funded the remaining $100 million, leading to cuts in the project. In 2022, Provo
sought additional funds from the Water Quality Board and received $15 million, including $10 million in
loans at 0.5% interest and $5 million in principal forgiveness. Despite this, the project was incomplete,
requiring the use of both new and old facilities, which put the system at risk of failure. As part of the
city's budget process, it was noted that Provo would seek additional funds to complete the project. The
state has mandated that the entire project, including three bioreactors, be completed within 10 years,
with only two bioreactors funded so far. Given challenges with self-funding due to inflation and other
factors, Provo approached the Water Quality Board again and received authorization for an additional
$7 million, leaving a shortfall of approximately $30 million. To close this gap, Provo intends to request a
revenue bond to complete the project.

Councilor Bogdin asked for clarification on the decision to take on a loan for the project, despite Provo
having been saving for a "pay as you go" program. She explained that costs had risen significantly, and
the city determined that taking on the loan would not result in higher utility rates for residents, as they
were already collecting funds for the program. She asked for an explanation of how the bond approach
would be cheaper eventually compared to continuing the "pay as you go" method.

Mr. McKnight explained that the wastewater fund covers the entire wastewater system, including the
collection system and the treatment plant, using a combination of funding options such as "pay as you
go," grants, and low-interest loans from the state. While "pay as you go" has been used for collection
system improvements, he noted that funding the current projects through this method would require an
