Item 1: A discussion regarding the proposed repeal of Provo City Code Chapter 9.41
(25-047) 0:08:25

Bob Trombly, Deputy City Attorney, presented this item. He explained that Provo City Code
Chapter 9.41 previously adopted an older version of the Utah Alcohol Beverage Control Act by
ordinance. Under that older version, municipalities were required to formally adopt the act
before they could enforce its provisions. However, that version of the law has since been
repealed and replaced with a newer version, referred to as 32B, which mandates municipal
enforcement without requiring formal adoption. Mr. Trombly characterized this repeal as a
cleanup action to align the City's code with current state law.

Item 2: A presentation regarding the FY2026 Budget: Public Works (25-025) 0:12:35

Jimmy McKnight, Public Works Business Manager, presented on the FY2026 budget for the
Public Works Department. He provided an overview of the Water Resources Division, which
includes both the water fund and wastewater fund. Mr. McKnight stated that the water fund

streets and engineering divisions.

Councilor Whipple inquired about administrative costs from the water division being allocated to

requested positions were funded due to the need to balance operational and capital expenditures.

He also reviewed appropriations made outside the standard budget process, including funds for
property acquisitions and service line improvements. Finally, Mr. McKnight presented the
proposed FY2026 supplemental budget request, which includes a $486,000 increase in the
operating budget for the new water reclamation plant.

Item 5: A discussion regarding property tax (25-046) 2:02:04

Justin Harrison, Council Executive Director, provided an in-depth overview of property tax
regulations with a particular focus on their application within the State of Utah. He explained
that tax rates are based on property values and the previous year’s revenue. He emphasized that
unless a taxing entity proactively proposes an increase, the total property tax revenue remains
constant, regardless of changes in property values. This statutory mechanism ensures revenue
stability for municipalities and predictability for property owners.

Mr. Harrison detailed how property tax revenue is distributed among various governmental
entities. Approximately 57% of every tax dollar goes to the local school district, followed by
17% to Provo City, 15% to the State of Utah, 8% to Utah County, and 4% to the Central Utah
Water Conservancy District.

Historical data was presented to illustrate trends in Provo City's property tax rates and overall
revenues. These data showed that, despite significant increases in property values over time, the
total tax collected has remained relatively stable, consistent with Utah’s revenue-neutral taxation
framework. This stability has been beneficial to the City’s budget planning.

A key point of discussion was whether the City should consider a cost of living adjustment to
property tax rates in response to inflation and rising economic pressures. Councilor Handley
expressed interest in exploring such an adjustment to make the City less dependent on sales tax,
which currently accounts for 33% of revenue and is more susceptible to economic fluctuation.
By contrast, property tax is a more stable revenue stream and comprises a smaller portion of the
City’s income.

Council members voiced mixed reactions. While some acknowledged the long-term benefits of a
modest rate adjustment to align with inflation, others, including Councilor Christensen,
expressed concern over the cumulative financial impact on residents. He noted the strain already
created by recent increases in water, energy, and transportation fees. Councilor Bogdin
commented that a property tax increase might reduce public support for the upcoming
Recreation, Arts, and Parks (RAP) tax renewal.

No formal action was taken during the discussion. Council members agreed to continue
evaluating the issue and revisit it as part of future strategic planning efforts. The potential for a
property tax increase remains under consideration, pending further analysis and public input.

Item 7: A discussion regarding an ordinance amending Provo City Code regarding the
Transportation Utility Fee dedication of funds and amending fees on the Provo City
Consolidated Fee Schedule (25-028) 2:51:45

Jimmy McKnight, Public Works Business Manager, presented proposed changes to the
Transportation Utility Fee (TUF) ordinance. He outlined a plan to increase annual TUF funding
from $2.7 million to $4 million over the next three years. The goal is to fully fund road
maintenance through the TUF without relying on other capital improvement funds, under the
guiding principle that maintaining high-quality roads now avoids more costly full repaving in the
future.

The Council engaged in a detailed discussion on how TUF funds should be utilized. A key issue
was whether to allow TUF funds to be used for traffic signal replacements. Councilors debated
whether expanding the use of TUF funds beyond road surfaces might undermine the legal
foundation and public understanding of the fee. Concerns were raised that such a change could
expose the City to legal challenges or misalign with prior legal rulings and legislative intent,
which have traditionally focused TUF usage on road surface maintenance.

Mr. McKnight clarified that the majority of TUF funds would remain allocated to resurfacing,
crack sealing, and related maintenance tasks. However, he acknowledged that including traffic
signals could potentially divert existing capital improvement funds away from those purposes.

Councilor MacKay made a motion, seconded by Councilor Handley, to strike Section
5.08.060(ii) (line 34) of the proposed ordinance and consider the revised version at the April
22nd Regular Meeting. The motion passed unanimously, 6-0, with Councilor Hoban excused.

Item 10: A discussion regarding data centers and self-serve power plants (25-049) 4:14:19

Scott Bunker, Energy Director, presented on the topic of data centers and self-serve power
plants. He clarified that Provo Power does not oppose the development of data centers but
emphasized the importance of ensuring such facilities are located in zones with sufficient
infrastructure and do not pose risks to existing utility customers. Key concerns include avoiding
stranded investments and maintaining equitable distribution of utility costs.

The Council discussed the environmental and infrastructure implications of data centers,
particularly in relation to whether these facilities should be allowed to operate self-serve power
plants or be required to draw power solely from the city’s grid. Legal obligations under Provo’s
arrangement with the Utah Municipal Power Agency (UMPA) were noted, specifically the
requirement to meet energy demands through shared agency resources rather than independent
generation.

Additional concerns were raised about potential emissions from on-site power generation and the
regulatory challenges involved in managing these facilities. Councilors stressed the need for a
clear policy framework to address zoning, environmental, and utility infrastructure
considerations before permitting further development.

Councilor Whipple made a motion, seconded by Councilor MacKay, directing Council Staff to
work with the Administration and appropriate outside partners to develop a comprehensive
policy regarding data centers. The motion passed unanimously, 6-0, with Councilor Hoban
excused.

Item 12: An ordinance amending Provo City Code to add data centers as a permitted use in
Airport Zones. (PLOTA20240373) 5:26:49

Nancy Robison, Planner, presented a review of the proposed ordinance amendment that would
add data centers as a permitted use in Airport Zones. The original proposal, which had the
support of the Planning Commission, was intended to expand zoning permissions to allow data
centers in airport-specific areas.

Robison explained that after further internal review and interdepartmental consultation, staff
revised its position. The revised recommendation is based on the finding that data centers are
already permitted in several other zones throughout the city that are better suited to
accommodate their infrastructure needs. These existing zones were found to have the necessary
capacity to support the operational demands of data centers, making the expansion into Airport
Zones less practical and potentially inefficient.

Concerns about infrastructure stress, environmental impacts, and overall efficiency informed the
updated staff position. Robison emphasized the need for careful zoning to ensure that data
centers are located in areas where they can operate effectively without overburdening local
systems.

The Council discussed the proposal and acknowledged the reasoning behind the staff's updated
recommendation. They expressed appreciation for the thorough analysis and recognized the
importance of aligning zoning permissions with Provo’s broader infrastructure and development
strategy.

Although staff now recommended against the proposed amendment, the ordinance was
scheduled to proceed to a formal vote during the evening Council meeting. The outcome of that
vote would determine whether additional analysis and zoning adjustments will be needed to
better align future data center developments with the city’s sustainability and infrastructure
goals.
