dwelling will continue to be zoned A and will be approximately 27,750 square feet in size. The
property is located at 5990 West 3500 South on 1.99 acres for the zone change and 3.4 acres for
the General Plan change.

Properties to the west and north are zoned residential including R-1-8 (single family with a
requirement for 8,000 square foot lots and R-1-10 (single family with a requirement for 10,000

Development Proposal

As shown in the concept plan, the applicant is proposing to develop a subdivision with 11 new
single-family lots with a minimum size of 8,000 square feet. As noted earlier, the existing dwelling

with the exception of lots 5 and 6 will meet the 80-foot frontage requirement of the R-1-8 zone.
Lots 5 and 6 are approximately 75 feet in width and will need an exception regarding the frontage
requirement. Since those lots are less than 80 feet in width, the applicant is asking for an exception
that a 2-car garage be allowed with a pad built on the side to comply with the city’s parking
requirements.

All streets are public as required by City ordinance. The developer has prepared a concept plan
showing a 54-foot right-of-way with the required improvements including a cul-de-sac to
accommodate emergency and other services. The cul-de-sac will extend to the east property line

anticipated that the developer will need to acquire any land from that owner to make the right-of-

cobble or other landscaping. 2. Deed that portion over to lot 506 if that property owner is willing
to accept it.

property mentioned above is part of lot 11, or deeded to lot 506, a fence will provide a buffer from

In reviewing this application, staff suggests that there are two primary questions to answer:
    1. Should the City allow apartments on the mall site?
    2. Assuming the location is appropriate, does the quality of the project achieve City goals?

Each of these questions is explored below.
Should the City allow apartments on the mall site?

General Plan Considerations

The quotes below from the City’s General Plan can help answer this question (italics added for
emphasis):

       “When considering rezone requests for high density residential development, the
       proximity to transit, the size of the project and the proposed level of quality (i.e. exterior
       finishes and amenities) should be primary factors for evaluation along with other site-
       specific considerations.”

       “In areas where retail development is aging (at least 30 years old) and underperforming
       and where transit is readily accessible, consider the addition of some multi-family
       residential as part of a redevelopment strategy to add more retail demand.”

When it comes to proximity to transit, no other location in the City has access to more public
transit options than this location. In addition to light rail service, the bus hub at the West Valley
Central Station by City Hall is serviced by 9 local bus routes. One of those routes stops on the
south side of the mall along 3800 South. Another route stops on the west side of the mall along
2700 West. In addition, UTA is currently constructing a new bus rapid transit (BRT) line called
the Midvalley Connector that will connect the West Valley Central Station to Salt Lake
Community College and the Murray Central Station that is serviced by Frontrunner and the
TRAX Red and Blue lines.

The General Plan comment about the size of the project was made because larger apartment

developments (those with 200 units or more) tend to have more amenities, on-site management,
better security, and owners that reinvest in and update the units. If approved, the three proposed
apartment buildings would represent the largest apartment development in the City under one
owner.

The proposed level of quality is addressed later in this report.

All of the retail space proposed for demolition was constructed in 1970 as part of the original
mall building and is either vacant or underperforming. As an example of the vacant or
underperforming space, when Hobby Lobby moved into the space vacated by Macy’s, they left
the eastern 40% of the building vacant. This is one of the areas proposed for demolition.

While certain tenants within the mall are doing well, the mall overall is underperforming relative
to the City as a whole. From 2019 to 2024, sales tax collected at the mall (excluding Costco)
increased by about 20%. However, inflation during this same period increased by 23%. By
comparison, sales tax collected within the City as a whole from 2019 to 2024 was up by about
40%.

Adding residential to mall sites is a national trend to replace vacant retail space like empty
department stores, chip away at the housing shortage, and bring people closer to the remaining
retail space. A CNBC article on this topic is accessible here.

Parking (Sections 7-9-104 and 7-9-105)

The applicant’s proposal includes a request to reduce the amount of required parking. This
request is supported by a parking study conducted by Hales Engineering. In summary, the
applicant is proposing to reduce the existing amount of parking from 3,000 stalls to 2,800 stalls
though the City ordinance would require 3,656 stalls with the proposed changes. The parking
study justifies the 2,800 stalls through three steps. First, by counting actual parking demand over
Black Friday weekend, they determined that the parking demand rate for the overall shopping
center was 3.08 stalls per 1,000 square feet of occupied space, yielding 3,020 stalls. Second, they
applied time-of-day adjustments from the Institute of Transportation Engineers (ITE) for the
various land uses within the project. Third, they added a buffer of 10% to account for occasional
demand surges, yielding 2,784 stalls.

Staff is supportive of the parking reduction request for the reasons described in the parking study
and the following two additional reasons:

   •   Over the many years the mall has operated, staff does not recall a time when there has not
       been a significant oversupply of parking at the mall.
   •   The City’s minimum parking requirements for commercial use were largely established
       using the latest edition of ITE’s Parking Generation Manual. For example, ITE data
       shows that the average parking demand during the busiest time of the week for a
       shopping plaza of 40,000 to 150,000 square feet is 3.11 stalls/1,000 square feet of
       building and the City’s parking requirement for general retail is 3 stalls/1,000 square feet.
       However, for shopping centers over 150,000 square feet, ITE’s average parking demand

Traffic

The applicant’s proposal includes a traffic impact study performed by Hales Engineering. In
summary, the traffic impact study evaluated the level of service of 17 intersections with and
without the proposed project using traffic modeling data for 2024, 2029, and 2050 and made
recommendations to improve the level of service where needed. Pages 2 through 4 of the traffic
impact study summarize the findings and recommendations from Hales Engineering.

In addition to the issues identified in the traffic impact study, staff discussed two other traffic
issues with the applicant and Hales Engineering. First, southbound traffic on 2700 West turning
left into the mall at the Lehman Avenue access regularly backs out onto 2700 West because
vehicles entering the mall site stop at the first intersection. The applicant has asked Hales
Engineering to evaluate solutions to this problem. Second, the drive aisle just east of Wells Fargo
does not align with the drive aisle between In-N-Out Burger and Panda Express. The applicant
has indicated that they will realign the drive aisle east of Wells Fargo to align with the drive aisle
between In-N-Out Burger and Panda Express.

The applicant is proposing to implement the on-site mitigations recommended by traffic impact
study. The applicant is also proposing to install the recommended traffic signal on 2700 West
just south of Wells Fargo with a request that the City pay half of the cost. The recommended
traffic signal at 2400 West and 3800 South is not addressed by the applicant because, in the
applicant’s words, “This recommendation impacts the entire block, not just our property. Given
that, we see this as a broader planning matter rather than a site-specific obligation.” Since the
traffic study identifies this traffic signal being needed because of the proposed project, staff is
not recommending that the City pay half of the cost.

While not addressed by the applicant, the traffic study also recommends a new traffic signal at
2400 West and 3800 South by 2029 as a result of background traffic. Public Works is
recommending that this traffic signal be installed by the developer. Since the traffic study
recommends this improvement in 2029 due to background traffic that is not a result of this
project, staff is reluctant to make this a requirement in the development agreement.

Neighboring Land Uses

In some instances, apartments in the City are built next to existing single family residential
neighborhoods. In this case, the proposed apartments are located between 3500 South on the
north, I-215 on the east, the main mall building on the west, and Costco to the south. Apartment
residents would be next to shopping and services including Costco, banks, restaurants, shops at
the mall, Planet Fitness, Megaplex, All-Star Bowling, and Springhill Suites. Including a mix of
uses in close proximity to each other can reduce automobile trips that would otherwise occur
when uses are segregated. In fact, the traffic study referenced above estimated a 20% reduction
in trips due to trips that stay within the development given the mix of land uses. An additional
trip reduction of 5% was applied due to the proximity of the transit at West Valley Central
Station.

Landscaping and Recreation Space (Sections 7-6-302 and 7-11-414)

There are two standards for landscaping and recreation space that would apply in this situation.
First, the MXD zone has a minimum landscaping percentage of 20% that would apply to the
entire area being rezoned to MXD. By comparison, the minimum landscaping percentage under
the current C-2 zone is 15%.

When the mall site was originally constructed in 1970, there was very little landscaping installed.
It appears that when the mall was built, the County only required 5% of the parking area to be
landscaping. When the mall site was renovated in the late 2000s, significant landscaping was
added to strategic locations like along 2700 West and in the mall plaza. However, the entire mall
site was not required to come into compliance with the C-2 zone requirement of 15%
landscaping.

Staff recommends a similar approach with this project. Instead of applying the 20% requirement
to the entire mall site, this standard would be applied to the areas that are being modified.

The second standard is found in the multi-unit residential design standards that have a minimum
recreation space percentage of 40% for developments with a density over 30 dwelling units/acre
that apply to just the residential portion of the mall site. For reference, recreation space is defined
as:
        “a. Outdoor space shared by residents of a residential Development for passive and
        active recreation. Examples include terraces, Courtyards, plazas, shared roof decks,
        sports courts, sports fields, open lawn areas, private parks, swimming pools, hot tubs, tot
        lots, picnic areas, fire pits and trails.

       b. Indoor space shared by residents of a residential Development for passive and active
       recreation. Examples include fitness rooms, libraries, computer labs, swimming pools,
       hot tubs, sports courts, lobbies, clubhouses, social rooms, classroom space, community

       c. Private outdoor space provided only for the use of the residents of the Dwelling Unit
       to which it is attached. Examples include private Yards, front porches, patios, balconies,

Based on calculations from staff, it appears that 40% of the area devoted to Phase 1 would

The applicant, Blake Hansen, was present. He addressed concerns about parking, stating that the
site is underutilized, and the proposed development would provide better use of the land. Blake
also discussed the traffic study, acknowledging that the project would have a small impact on
traffic. The Planning Commission raised questions about the on-site flow of vehicles and the
potential impact on nearby streets, to which the applicant responded, stating that the project
would help reroute traffic internally and reduce the impact on nearby streets.

Commissioner Drozdek asked Blake if the aesthetic of the buildings could be improved. Blake
responded that they do have plans to continue designs of the exterior features of their buildings,
especially when they submit for building permits. Blake assured the Commission that the designs
will meet Code, and they are committed to the open spaces, as an urban environment it creates
offers a better feeling for the community.

Vice Chair Winters asked Blake about separating JCPenney from the mall. Blake responded that
the idea would be to open it up and have connectivity in the mall, so shoppers can walk through
the mall to the other side, inside and out. He doesn’t know the exact location of the JCPenney
doors. Vice Chair Winters then asked if all the traffic lights could be timed better if they weren’t
already. Eric Brondum, Traffic Engineer for West Valley City Public Works Department, stated
all the signals in Utah are on the same central system. There is an effort to time them all together.
The complicating factor is the Trax line. Whenever a train comes, it changes the signal
automatically and that can throw timing plans off, depending on their headways.

Michele Porter, a property manager in California, encouraged the increase of parking for the
apartment buildings. Blake explained that providing more structured parking would significantly
increase costs and rents. Commissioner Matagi added that she’s not concerned about the parking
because a lot of the current generation of young adults don’t have driver’s licenses, they use
Uber and take public transportation. Blake added that rents in the State of Utah and West Valley
don't justify costs for structured parking without significant public assistance. The amount of
structural support to provide those additional parking structures is roughly $50,000 to $60,000 a
stall. To add just another level underneath a building is roughly 10 million dollars, which
ultimately is passed on to the renters.
