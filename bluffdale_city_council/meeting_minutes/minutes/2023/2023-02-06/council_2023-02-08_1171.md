         2.1    Discussion Regarding Detached Accessory Dwelling Units (Staff Presenter^
                Grant Crowell).


City Planner/Economic Development Director, Grant Crowell shared information related to
Detached Accessory Dwelling Units("DADU"). He noted that a number ofquestions were posed
to the Council previously and there would be discussions on each one. Mayor Hall asked that all
of the questions be reviewed during the Work Session. Lengthier discussions could take place
after each ofthe questions had been addressed. The first question was as follows:

   • Should the City limit DADUs by lot size? What if there is also an Internal Accessory
     Dwelling Unit("lADU")on the property? lADUs are permitted by State law.

Mayor Hall noted that everyone in Bluffdale is already allowed to have an lADU according to the
State. The question was whether lot size should impact whether or not a DADU is permitted as
well. Mr. Crowell explained that there is not currently a lot size limitation on lADUs. For DADUs,
it is possible to allow them only on 10,000-square-foot lots. He reported that 10,000 square-foot
lots are largely found in the R-1-10 and R-1-43 zones. Setbacks and parking availability would be
limiting factors. Council Member Kallas thought the 10,000-square-foot lot size limitation was a
good starting point. Council Member Crockett agreed and liked there being some sort oflimitation.
However,she acknowledged that the setbacks and parking spaces will ultimately determine where
DADUs are permitted. Council Member Hales did not have an issue with DADUs on any size lot
as long as parking requirements and setbacks are adhered to.

The second question related to zoning and building permits and was as follows:

   • Zoning and Building Permits are required to build a DADU. Does the City want to have
        an annual and reoccurring registration fee in place as well?

The Council Members were not in support ofan annual and reoccurring registration fee. The next
question pertained to limiting Accessory Dwelling Units("ADU")and was as follows:

   • Where allowed by State Law,should the City limit ADUs to one per parcel?

Mayor Hall was not certain that this was allowed. Council Member Kallas suggested that the
Council discuss this matter in greater detail once all of the other questions have been reviewed.
He had outstanding questions related to this matter. Mayor Hall also had some questions because
she was not certain what the law would permit the City to do in that instance. The next question
was shared with the Council Members and was as follows:


   • Owner occupation is currently required in the primary unit. Should the City allow the
     property owner to live in the ADU and rent out the primary unit?

Some Council Members did not believe it mattered whether the property owner lives in the primaiy
unit or the ADU but felt it was important for the owner to live on the property. Council Member
Kallas noted that an ADU is an accessory use, so it made sense for the homeowner to live in the
primary unit. That being said, he was open to the property owner living in the ADU. Mayor Hall
reported that there had been some feedback from residents on that issue. If there is a primary
residence, an lADU in the basement, as well as a DADU and the owner does not live on the
property,there were concerns that the uses would not fit the intention ofthe language.

that the property owner live on the property still made sense. There needed to be some level of
care for the home. Council Member Kallas believed owner occupation needed to stay because
otherwise, it was essentially a rental property. Other Council Members agreed.

   • Should the City allow separate utility metering?

    • ADUs are not allowed to be used as short-term rentals in the draft. Through a separate

Mayor Hall believed it was an important future discussion item but currently the Council felt it
was best to focus on the existing DADU discussions. The next question was:

    • Should DADUsfollow the typically required accessory building setbacks and lot coverage
      requirements ofthe underlying zone?

Mr. Crowell explained that in most instances, R-1-10 and R-1-43 zoned lots have setback
requirements for 10 feet on the side and 10 feet on the rear for a detached structure. In a few other
zones, such as the special districts where there are a few larger lots, there were some slightly
smaller setbacks. He wondered if the Council wanted there to be an additional setback created
because ofthe DADU unit. Council Member Kallas asked for additional clarification.

Mr. Crowell reported that for a detached garage in the R-1-10 or R-1-43 Zone, the requirements
were 10 feet in the rear, 10 feet on the side, and 10 feet from the main structure. The current draft
states that DADUs are only permitted in rear yards and not side yards. Council Member Kallas
wanted to better understand the residential setbacks versus the detached setbacks. If a detached
building is being turned into a residential unit, it might make sense to have the same setback.
Mr. Crowell clarified that a home in the R-1-43 Zone is 30 feet on the firont, 20 feet on the side,
and 30 feet in the rear. With those limitations, it was unlikely there could be a DADU.

Mayor Hall asked what would happen in the case of an oddly shaped lot. Mr. Crowell noted that
it would be necessary to make a determination based on the facts, the location of the roads, the
property lines, and the fi-ontage. The definitions in the ordinance provided some guidance but did
not address every possible scenario. When there is an unusually shaped lot, it is necessary to look
at the context. Lot lines will be determined and City Staff would see what makes the most sense.
Council Member Kallas felt that the setback should be behind the plane ofthe fi-ont ofthe house.
There could then be an exception for odd-shaped lots where the Zoning Administrator can examine

    • Should DADUs be allowed in side yards?

Council Member Aston did not see an issue with DADUs being built on the side of the property.

    • Should there be a minimum size or maximum size for DADUs?

Council Member Kallas was not sure if a minimum size was necessary but he did believe it was
important to have a maximum size in place for DADUs. Mayor Hall asked if smaller DADUs
would count toward the housing requirements for the State. Mr. Crowell reported that anything
the City does in any given year to further one of the menu items that had been selected would
ensure that the City maintains compliance. As long as the DADU discussions continue, he did not

item option. He noted that there are micro ADUs that are 500 square feet or smaller that have been
seen in other parts ofthe county.

instance, whether the living space was above a garage or on its own. Council Member Kallas noted
that to be a dwelling unit, there needs to be a restroom and a kitchen. Any DADU would need to
meet the Building Code.

Mayor Hall believed the Council Members were not concerned with a minimum or maximum size.
Council Member Kallas clarified that he had an opinion on a maximum. It was determined that
the DADU maximum could not be more than 50% of the size of the main dwelling. Council
Member Aston wanted to have additional discussions about the minimum size in the fiiture.

this was a question the Council would come back to once all of the other questions have been
reviewed as there were differing opinions. The next question was read aloud, which was as
follows:

Mayor Hall explained that the Council could determine that DADUs should be required in a garage.

apartment. It did not matter to him ifthe DADU is built on top ofa garage but it does matter that

believe the DADU needs to be within a garage and could be separate. The next question related
to permanent foundations and was as follows:

   • Are permanent foundations required for a DADU?

The Council Members believed a permanent foundation should be required. There cannot be
cinderblocks or trailers and the DADU needs to be anchored onto some type offoundation system.

Calls for service were further reviewed. ChiefBurton reported that the increase in calls for service
is more or less what is expected and predicted. However, what is not predicted is the increase in

6,000 require a report. This year,there was a 33% increase fi-om the previous year and the amount

Chief Burton explained that a call for service that requires a report means there was additional
work. The increase in those kinds of calls means additional officers are required to handle those
calls. Out of the 11,786 calls, approximately 3,350 required more than one officer. As a result,
there were a little over 16,000 call responses, which meant the total number of officers on calls.
Approximately 30% ofcalls required more than one officer. When considering the calls for service
per officer per year, there were just under 1,100. The number ofincidents was approximately 600
per officer per year.

Based on his years of experience, when there are 1,000 to 1,075 calls for service per officer per
year, that is acceptable. More could create issues. Chief Burton noted that without adding any
officers next year ifthere is an 8% increase, it could start to reach a point where additional staffing
is needed. Council Member Crockett asked if the 11,786 was based on the call number and not
the officer nximber. Chief Burton clarified that the number is based on individual calls. Each call
had a responsible officer. There are responding officers as well in instances where more than one
officer is needed. If it is a one-officer call, that officer is both the responsible and responding
officer. There were approximately 4,200 officer call responses. He did not know the exact number
ofofficers who were there in those instances. The simplified way to reach a number is to take the
number of calls for service and divide it by the number of available officers to respond to calls.
Occasionally, a detective takes a call, but the numbers presented were fairly accurate. The average
officer in Bluffdale handles 1,071 calls for service per year. He noted that some departments use
a population density calculation.
