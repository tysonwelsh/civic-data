City Manager, Bruce Kartchner, reported that in 2026, the City will have its first Strategic
Implementation Plan. Tens of thousands of dollars were spent creating and updating the General Plan

outreach through open houses and polls to better understand what is important to Bluffdale residents,
why it is important, and what they would like the City to become. When he first moved to Bluffdale,
there were 4,500 residents, and what most of them wanted was to escape the rat race of larger cities.
The City now has a diverse population of 20,000 residents with differing opinions as to what the City
should look like and what its priorities should be. The General Plan aggregates that information and

The yearly budget season can be painful or easy depending on a variety of factors. Its primary purpose
is to create a budget for the current year and determine how City resources will be expended. The
Strategic Implementation Plan is a bridge between the General Plan and Budget. If properly prepared,
it will make the budget process easier because it outlines where the City is going and what is needed
to get there.
Mr. Kartchner reported that the draft Strategic Implementation Plan would be adopted by the City
Council once finalized. He was excited to present the plan as it provides guidance Staff had requested
to help them do their jobs more efficiently and get the City where it wants to be.
The strategic vision that drives the plan balances growth, preservation of existing neighborhoods,
expanding commercial development, etc., based on the General Plan. The land use vision focuses on
strategic locations, such as what areas of the City should be commercial and why, and where density
is focused and why. These aspects are important to meet State mandates while maintaining the City’s
character.
Economic development in Bluffdale includes a lot of home occupations, including some borderline
industrial businesses that are problematic. Job development is also part of economic development,
and a mandate of Redevelopment Agencies (“RDA”) and Economic Development Agencies (“EDA”)
is job creation. In the past six years, the concept of “retail commercial” has changed to Bluffdale’s
benefit; private homes have become one of the biggest economic drivers in the City due to Amazon
and other retail deliveries.
Reducing commute times and transportation costs benefits both society and individuals, but that
requires proper design and construction of infrastructure. Mr. Kartchner finds it frustrating that
required infrastructure often must come before the revenues that will support it. The understaffed
Engineering Department must try to determine how to fund projects when the revenue has not started
coming in. Property taxes are delayed by 18 to 24 months, and it can take a while for new commercial
projects to develop and fill with tenants. The costs are front-loaded. How to handle that is addressed
in the Strategic Implementation Plan.

The General Plan defines the City’s long-term vision. Residents have explicitly stated that they love
trails, parks, and recreation, and they want to strengthen the police and fire departments. Bluffdale’s
demographics are changing. Over 50% of residents live on the east side of the river, and their age,
lifestyle, and priorities differ from the original one-acre neighborhoods to the west. Older residents
express the desire for their children and grandchildren to live nearby. The City Council needs to
consider how to accomplish those goals.
The final concept outlined in the plan is resiliency. It is important to build things that will last a long
time and be sustainable. If the City builds 50 acres of parks but does not plan for the personnel to
maintain them or funding for maintenance and upgrades, that asset will become a burden. Resiliency
is important because it allows the City to move ahead with strength and stability despite challenges.
That includes things like economic and fiscal resiliency, taking care of historic places, and ensuring
that everyone is on board.
Mr. Kartchner finds it interesting that every election cycle, candidates say that there needs to be better
communication and outreach. He agrees that if people are better engaged and more involved, it is
easier to determine the City’s priorities. However, the residents who speak to him or the Council are
not necessarily representative of the City as a whole. He proposed that the Council consider allocating
funds for statistically valid polling to determine what residents really want.
The Strategic Implementation Plan converts the General Plan’s vision into action. It provides a clear
framework that acts as the basis for the Budget and various Capital Improvement Plans and provides
a way for future decisions to be evaluated consistently. It is also a shared understanding between the
City Council and Staff. It is not a project list, capital plan, or funding commitment, and it does not
replace the Council’s analyses or decisions. It is also not a static document as it will be updated at
least annually.
Mr. Kartchner stated that after the Strategic Implementation Plan was reviewed and discussed at that
meeting, he would forward an updated draft to the Council for consideration. He has also developed
a Financial Strategic Plan that supports the main plan. The individual goals and implementation
strategies were then discussed.

The Fire Department is working to improve the average dispatch time which is 2.21 minutes. The
National Fire Protection Association (“NFPA”) recommendation is 1.25 minutes. The average arrival
time is approximately six minutes, two minutes higher than the NFPA recommendation.
NFPA 1710 recommends a minimum of 15 firefighters be dispatched at the first alarm for a low-
hazard, single-family house fire; 20 for a strip shopping center or apartment fire; and 43 for high-rise
buildings of seven stories or more. Additional firefighters should be sent in situations that are beyond
the capability of the initial alarm. In low-hazard response areas, the required number of firefighters
per engine is four. In areas with higher incidences or geographic restrictions, a minimum of five
firefighters is required. In dense urban areas, the minimum is six.
Mr. Kartchner stated that the NFPA requirements show the importance of mutual aid agreements.
Chief Evans stated that each station is currently staffed with four employees, two on the engine and
two on the ambulance. The units respond in tandem so that there are always four people on calls.
All cities within the mutual aid agreement have committed to sending no less than three personnel per
engine.
The Salt Lake Valley Emergency Communication Center (““VECC”) recommends that 17 personnel
respond to a house fire, including three engines, one ladder truck, one ambulance, and two battalion
chiefs. Bluffdale and UFA have one battalion chief each. For apartment fires, the VECC recommends
21 total personnel, including one additional ladder truck. For commercial fires, their recommendation
is four engines, two trucks, one ambulance, and one heavy rescue vehicle that can perform
extrications. High-rise fires require 50 personnel, including six engines, four trucks, three
ambulances, one heavy rescue vehicle, and one air and light truck, as well as four battalion chiefs.
In 2024, Bluffdale fire stations were closed 62 times. As a result of bringing in full-time staff, there
were only four closures in 2025.
Chief Evans reported that fire trucks are very expensive, and that cost has increased exponentially
since 2022. A standard engine can cost between $500,000 and $1 million, depending on its equipment
and capabilities. The City currently has a mixture of new and old equipment, and replacements need

2.3. Engineering.
Mr. Fazio reported that the Engineering Department works very hard to keep everything in-house.
Most cities contract out much of their engineering work. They work together with other departments

interest in developing the Bringhurst area, so they have been working on a road extending south into

The City Council receives departmental budget requests and determines their order of importance

The City’s population has increased from approximately 8,000 in 2010 to 23,000 in 2025. Additional

Services, can take on more work before they need to expand.
Revenues are categorized based on stability. Sales tax is hard to estimate and unstable as it can
decrease significantly if the economy declines and consumer spending habits change. Building
permits and development fees decrease as the City is built out.
Ms. Thayer stated that she does not believe there is an appetite for a property tax increase, but one
will be necessary in the future if other revenue sources are not found. Breaking up the land into
smaller zones would bring in development fees, and she enjoys hearing proposals like that which can
bring in revenue without a major negative impact. She hopes to be as good at finding money for
projects as Mr. Kartchner was, but that will be challenging.
Expenditures are categorized as primary and discretionary, recurring and non-recurring. They always
try to match revenues and expenditures in a sustainable and balanced way. Recurring revenues like
property tax and sales tax are matched to recurring expenses such as salaries and day-to-day
operational costs. One-time revenues like grants, building permits, and development fees fund one-
time costs like capital projects and major equipment purchases.
Different funds are used for different purposes, and the funds cannot be intermingled. For example,
salaries and wages cannot be paid from the Class C Roads Fund. The General Fund includes sales,
energy, and hotel taxes. All property tax revenues go directly to the Fire and Police Protection Fund,
but they do not cover those costs, so they are supplemented with a transfer from the General Fund.
The Water Fund is treated differently from other funds, with equipment purchases depreciated over
time.
