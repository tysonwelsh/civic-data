                                     Traffic calming incorporates the combination of physical measures to reduce the negative
                                     effects of motor vehicle use, alter driver behavior, and improve conditions for non-motorized
                                     street users such as pedestrians and bicyclists (ITE 2016). Traffic calming measures
                                     encourage vehicles to obey posted speed limits as they move through different roadways
                                     (Parham 1998). The objective of traffic calming is to reduce motor vehicle speeds, noise,
                                     and collision frequency and severity; increase the level of safety for non-motorized users
                                     such as pedestrians and bicyclists; and enhance the street environment (ITE 2016, New
                                     England Section 2000). In addition to these objectives, speed reduction measures serve to
                                     increase quality of life, create safe and attractive roads, help to reduce the negative effects
                                     on the environment due to motor vehicles, and promote walking, cycling, and transit use (ITE
                                     2016).

                                     The purpose of this manual is to provide general information about several speed reduction
                                     measures used in the United States. This manual presents several speed reduction
                                     measures with their proper definition, effectiveness, relative cost, applications, and
                                     advantages and disadvantages. The manual also includes two decision matrices: one to
                                     illustrate the effectiveness of each measure and the other to show the potential impacts of
                                     installing each measure. This manual also includes a suggested planning process to
                                     address speeding issues, as well as a flow chart and a project prioritization form to help
                                     decide the appropriate traffic calming measure.

VERTICAL SPEED REDUCTION MEASURES
Vertical speed reduction measures are applications that adjust pavement elevation over
short distances and cause vehicles to slow. The vertical changes in elevation are always less
than six inches at the street centerline (DelDOT 2012). Motorists who travel over these
speed control measures above the appropriate speed limit will experience “unpleasant
bumping sensations” (PennDOT 2012).

Vertical speed control measures are effective in reducing speed and diverting unwanted
traffic from neighborhood streets. If the location receiving vertical measures is used by
transit or emergency vehicles, or regularly requires snow removal via snowplows, it is
important to plan and design the placement of vertical measures to appropriately
accommodate those vehicles (DelDOT 2012).

Bluffdale is willing to implement the following vertical speed reduction measures:

   •   Raised Crosswalks
   •   Raised Intersections
   •   Textured Pavement

Information regarding these measures can be found in the appendix.

Due to the negative impacts of speed bumps/humps, they are not suitable for what
Bluffdale City is trying to accomplish with traffic calming on its roads. Information regarding
the negatives of speed bumps/humps can be found in the appendix.

                                     Horizontal speed reduction measures are applications that cause drivers to slow by altering

Narrowing speed reduction measures are applications that narrow the width of the travel
lane and in so doing, reduce the amount of available driving surface which causes vehicles
to slow. These measures are effective because they create shorter crossing distances for
pedestrians, they provide landscape opportunities, reduce speeds, and can accommodate
emergency vehicles moving through areas with narrowing measures (PennDOT 2012).

Stop Signs should not be used for speed control
Definition: Stop signs are primarily used to manage right-of-way at an intersection. In the
past, they have also been used as a traffic-calming measure (or also called speed reduction
measures). For example, stop signs have been used to slow down traffic in certain areas to
make them less attractive to through traffic (NCCHPP 2011). However, MUTCD states that
“STOP signs shall not be used for speed control” (MUTCD 2023).
Effectiveness: A study of nine residential two-way stop sign installations in Portland, Oregon,
indicated that stop signs are not very effective in reducing speeds. Before-and-after studies
on all 36 legs of the intersections indicated that the 85th percentile speeds remained the
same on 19 legs, (53%), increased by 2 mph or more on four legs (11%), and decreased by
2 mph or more on 13 legs (36%) for an average decrease of 0.5 mph (Parham 1998).
Application: Stop signs should only be used when warranted by the MUTCD. As with any
other traffic sign, drivers need to recognize the need for a sign, or they will eventually begin
to disregard the sign that they feel is unnecessary. This problem is especially dangerous with
regards to stop signs. The Utah MUTCD states the following regarding the excessive use of
stop signs (MUTCD 2011):
       Section 2A.04 Excessive Use of Signs: Regulatory and warning signs should be
       used conservatively because these signs, if used to excess, tend to lose their
       effectiveness.
       Section 2B.04 Right-of-Way at Intersections: Yield or Stop signs should not be
       used for speed control.
       Section 2B.07 Multi-Way Stop Applications: Multi-way stop control can be
       useful as a safety measure at intersections if certain traffic conditions exist.
       Safety concerns associated with multi-way stops include pedestrians,
       bicyclists, and all road users expecting other road users to stop. Multi-way
       stop control is used where the volume of traffic on the intersecting roads is
       approximately equal.

POTENTIAL IMPACT MATRIX
Speed reduction measures are used to address speeding issues; however, they can have
other effects in the surrounding area. These effects depend on the type of measure used
and its physical characteristics such as size and material. Based on the research done,
seven potential impacts were determined that apply to all measures: available shoulder
space, emergency response vehicles, drainage, noise pollution, pedestrian protection, snow
removal, and bicycle safety. This section explains each potential impact and shows a final
decision matrix including all the measures and potential impacts.
In general, the symbols used in this matrix consist of hollow and black circles.

   ○     Negative impact

   ●     Positive impact

Using this symbology, it is easy to see which measures are better in each category. For more
detailed information about the impact of a specific measure, refer to the vertical horizontal,
narrowing, and non-construction speed reduction measures sections in the beginning of this
manual.
Available Shoulder Space
The available shoulder space category refers to whether a road has sufficient space to place
a specific speed reduction measure in an area. In other words, it refers to the space that a
specific measure will need to be built on a particular roadway/intersection. Available space
is often a limiting factor since some measures require a considerable amount of road space
to be put in place. Chokers and bolb-outs, for example, require far more road space than
most traffic calming measures; they increase the sidewalk width and decrease the road
width to slow drivers and allow for safer pedestrian crossings.
The symbols used to reflect available shoulder space are a hollow circle and a black circle.

   ○     Measure needs extra space
                                                                                                  Traffic Manual | City of Bluffdale
   ●     Measure does not need extra space

Emergency Response Vehicles
The emergency response vehicle category refers to the effect that a specific speed reduction
measure will have in emergency response vehicle routes (delays). For example, some
measures will slow down emergency vehicles due to impediments in the roadway itself such
as speed humps. However, there are other measures that only cause a visual effect to
reduce speeds rather than physical changes that emergency could not avoid.

                                     1. Initiate the Study
                                     A study should be initiated involving all parties that would be affected by the implementation of a
                                     speed reduction measure in the area of study. In this stage of the process, parties involved, study
                                     area, the scope of work, and any constraints (budget, schedule, site conditions, etc.) should be
                                     identified and agreed upon. At the study initiation, the following tasks should be done:

                                            Identify the need for a speed reduction study: The need for a speed reduction study could be
                                            identified by neighborhood residents or organizations, and they should submit a formal
                                            petition to ensure consistency and objectiveness. A speed reduction study could also be
                                            identified by the city and municipal staff assigned to work on speed reduction issues. If the
                                            reported issue has already been identified and studied previously, and site conditions and
                                            traffic patterns have not significantly changed in the study area since the previous study, the
                                            concerned resident is notified of the results of the previous study. No further study will be
                                            performed unless study area conditions have changed, as determined by the City Engineer.

                                            Define study scope and study area: The study area should be clearly defined before any data
                                            collection and further investigation occurs to ensure that key objectives, constraints, and
                                            deliverables are identified and agreed in advance. The study or project area should include
                                            the study street, cross streets on either side of measure, any street that relies on the study
                                            street for access, the two parallel local service street, and any other local streets that may be
                                            affected by the speed reduction measure to be implemented.

                                            Develop a Team: The City Engineer may choose to form a team consisting of City Staff and/or
                                            a consultant engineer with experience in speed reduction measures depending on the extent
                                            and nature of the project.

                                            Meet with the team: The City Engineer and team should meet to ensure that all the parties
                                            agree and are aware of the issues and scope of work. In this meeting, the team should
                                            develop a detailed work program, schedule, and budget for the study.


                                     2. Identify Problems and Issues
                                     Data collection and analysis will aid in identifying whether an issue exists, as well as what measures
                                     may be appropriate to address such issues. The following steps are recommended to identify the

                                     problems and issues in the study area.

                                            Collect Data: Site specific data collection should be done to identify, confirm, and quantify
                                            the severity and urgency of the reported issue. The data collected will be used to identify the
                                            appropriate speed reduction measures and to establish existing conditions for comparison
                                            with conditions after any measure has been implemented. The data includes traffic volumes,
                                            speed data, crash data, roadway context information, pedestrian and bicycle activity,
                                            emergency service routes, and any other data that the team determines necessary for the
                                            study.


   17
       Quantify Problems: An analysis of the data collected needs to be done by the team to
       quantify the magnitude of the problems reported. Examples of this include the number of
       vehicles per hour or per day, duration of the problem such as only peak periods or all-day,
       which routes and directions of travel are affected, etc. This information will be used to
       identify the appropriate speed reduction measure to be implemented. A prioritization form
       has been included in the appendix to help rate the study results and assist in determining
       which type of traffic calming measures should be implemented. This prioritization form
       evaluates the data mentioned above. If during this stage it is identified that the problem
       extends to other neighborhoods or communities, the team should get in contact with that
       group and coordinate further efforts. If the study determines that no problem exists which
       warrants correction by traffic calming measures, then the speed reduction planning process
       is concluded, and documentation kept of the results. The resident(s) who reported the issue
       are contacted and notified.


3. Develop a Plan
Once any issues have been identified and quantified, the next step is to develop a speed reduction
plan according to what the area needs. The following steps present a layout for a successful plan:

       Examine arterial streets: Traffic operations of adjacent arterial streets should be examined to
       ensure there are no operational deficiencies which might be negatively affecting traffic in the
       neighborhood. If there are problems on the adjacent arterial streets, then modifications
       should be made to eliminate or reduce the traffic problems that occur on neighborhood
       streets. Once the arterial streets traffic concerns are addressed, the speed reduction
       planning process can reach its objective.

       Examine neighborhood streets: Consider the effects of speed reduction measures on
       adjacent streets to ensure that problems do not just shift from one street to another. All
       measures need to work together to eliminate or minimize problems and achieve the desired
       balance between safety, residential access, cost and acceptable traffic conditions.

       Review and select potential speed reduction measures: Potential measures should be
       selected using the Speed Reduction Flowchart found in Figure 1.

       Develop alternative speed reduction plans: Using the Speed Reduction Flowcharts will lead
       to the most effective speed reduction measures. Non-construction measures can be installed         Traffic Manual | City of Bluffdale
       in addition to the measures suggested by the Speed Reduction Flowcharts.

       Hold a community meeting to discuss alternative speed reduction plans: A meeting with the
       community should be held to present the speed reduction plans and receive input from all
       affected and interested parties. Consulted groups may consist of nearby residents,
       businesses, and emergency and maintenance service providers.

       Communicate with the appropriate parties: Communication with those that have ownership
       of the roadway (e.g., local government officials and police, fire and maintenance
       departments) in order for them to review and approve the plan that will be implemented.

                                            Failure to get the appropriate approvals could result in cancellation of projects after
                                            implementation.

                                            Evaluate the alternatives and select the recommended speed reduction plan: Evaluate the
                                            alternative speed reduction plans according to the needs of the street being assessed.
                                            Evaluation can be done by referring to the Potential Impact Matrix found on page 15.

                                            Present the recommended speed reduction plan: Present the speed reduction plan and cost
                                            to the public at a City Council meeting. The presentation should provide the opportunity to
                                            receive feedback and address questions and comments about the speed reduction plan.

                                            Develop an implementation strategy: If the City Council approves the proposed plan,
                                            determine the priorities for timing and staging of the plan implementation. Such timing
                                            includes consideration of when funding would be able to implement the plan, timing of other
                                            nearby planned projects, clustering of projects to reduce costs/impacts, economy, etc.

                                            Finalize report: Prepare a report or memo to summarize the issue, the data collection/study,

                                            way acquisition or permitting.

                                            Implement measures: If necessary, procure a contractor through the City’s procurement
                                            process and construct the project.

Negatives of Speed Bumps/Humps
Speed bumps/humps are a well-known traffic calming measure. While they can be effective
in reducing speeds, there are several drawbacks to installing speed bumps/humps,
including:

   •   Discomfort for vehicle occupants: Speed bumps/humps can cause discomfort to
       passengers inside the vehicle, particularly when driven over at higher speeds. This
       can be particularly bothersome for those with back or spine issues.
          o Munjin MA;Zamorano JJ;Marré B;Ilabaca F;Ballesteros V;Martínez C;Yurac
              R;Urzúa A;Lecaros M;Fleiderman J;García N; (n.d.). Speed hump spine
              fractures: Injury mechanism and Case series. Journal of spinal disorders &
              techniques. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21150664/

   •   Vehicle damage: Repeatedly driving over speed bumps/humps can lead to increased
       wear and tear on vehicles, potentially damaging the suspension, tires, and other
       components.
          o Jiffy Lube. (n.d.). Can speed bumps ruin your car?. Jiffy Lube.
              https://jiffylubesuncoast.com/can-speed-bumps-ruin-your-

              20to%20a%20car

   •   Noise pollution: Vehicles driving over speed bumps/humps can create noise,
       especially when larger or heavier vehicles traverse them, which can be bothersome
       to residents in quiet areas.
           o Haroon, M. J., Kharbeche, M., Alhajyaseen, W., &amp; Alhawari, A. (2022).
               Impact of Speed Hump on Noise Pollution. Australasian Transport Research
               Forum. https://australasiantransportresearchforum.org.au/wp-

   •   Reduced fuel efficiency: Vehicles need to slow down and then accelerate again when
       navigating speed bumps/humps, leading to decreased fuel efficiency and increased       Traffic Manual | City of Bluffdale

       fuel consumption.

   •   Difficulty for emergency vehicles: Speed bumps/humps can slow down emergency
       response vehicles that need to navigate them carefully. This delay in response time
       can reduce the chance of saving lives.

         In this case, the Salt Lake County District Attorney’s Office charged Mr. Roberts with six
         offenses: one count of misuse of public money, a second-degree felony, and five counts of
         falsifying or altering government records, class B misdemeanors. These charges were filed
         after a thorough review of the evidence, which indicated a reasonable likelihood of proving
         the following beyond a reasonable doubt, as outlined in the probable cause statement.

         Lieutenant Andy Halulic, with the Salt Lake County District Attorney's Office, conducted
         an investigation at the request of the City of Bluffdale into payroll documents related to
         Bluffdale Fire Department employees between March 1, 2018, and March 31, 2020.
         During this investigation, Lt. Halulic reviewed hundreds of daily reports signed by Mr.
         Roberts in his capacity as Fire Chief. Lt. Halulic identified several hundred daily reports
         containing handwritten modifications, which either added employees to the logs or altered
         the reported hours for those already listed. Upon comparing these reports with

fire department to generate payroll, and that the altered reports resulted in multiple
firefighters receiving pay for times they had not worked. The estimated overpayment of
wages to these firefighters is approximately $128,609.51.

During the investigation, Mr. Roberts admitted to Lt. Halulic that he had intentionally
altered the time logs to generate additional pay for the affected employees, and that he
knew his actions were wrong.

Throughout the case, our office engaged in discussions with both the Bluffdale City
Administration and Mr. Roberts’ defense attorney. As a result, Mr. Roberts entered a guilty
plea on June 18, 2024, to attempted misuse of public money, a third-degree felony. The
elements of his plea, as articulated in his plea form, state: “as a public servant, I
appropriated public money to the benefit of another without authority of law.” The facts
Mr. Roberts admitted to committing were: “On or about 3/1/2018, while employed by
Bluffdale City, I diverted public funds to my employees without permission or authority to
do so.”

The plea agreement terms include holding the guilty plea in abeyance for 36 months,
dismissing the remaining counts, placing Mr. Roberts under court supervision, and
requiring him to pay $10,000 in restitution to Bluffdale City. Additionally, the prosecution
agreed to consider early dismissal of the case upon full payment of restitution.

                                        MEMORANDUM

From: Fredric Donaldson, Bluffdale City Attorney
Date: August 30, 2024
Re:   Code Amendment to Bluffdale City Code 1.70.030


       Earlier this year (2024), the City Council decided to do away with the requirement to
approve an organization chart that specifies positions in each department of the City.
Instead, the City Council decided to give the City Manager the discretion to determine the
employment and staffing needs and positions for City departments. The organization chart
previously included a position of Assistant City Manager that was not filled.
       There is a need to clarify that the position of Assistant City Manager is intended to exist
and that the position is considered an appointment similar to other appointments in the City,
such as the City Attorney, City Recorder, and Treasurer.
       This code amendment creates the position of Assistant City Manager, indicates that
the duties will be specified by the City Manager, and that the person is selected by the City
Manager and then appointed by the Mayor with the consent of the City Council.

as designated by the City Manager. The Assistant City Manager shall be selected by the City
Manager and appointed by the Mayor with the consent of the City Council.

SUMMARY

The Applicant owns approximately 20 acres of property at the intersection of 1300 West and Porter Rockwell
Boulevard, which is in the SD-X The Bluff at Point Crossing Zone. This Zone was enacted on January 25, 2023,
through Ordinance 2023-03 (attached as Exhibit B) and entitled this property to develop up to 50 townhome
units and at least 62,000 square feet of commercial space. Following additional site work and soil analysis,
the Applicant determined a reduction in the developable area of the property is necessary because
additional space is needed to stabilize the surrounding slope. The Applicant has brought this Text
Amendment before the City to reduce the number of townhome units from 50 to 40 and to reduce the square
footage of commercial space from 62,000 to approximately 40,000, along with associated housekeeping
changes.

ANALYSIS

The SD-X The Bluff at Point Crossing Zone was created through Ordinance 2023-03 to develop a mixed-use
community adjacent to Porter Rockwell Boulevard and Heritagecrest Way (approximately 15600 S Porter
Rockwell Boulevard). As required by Section 11.110.080.050, the Applicant met with the City Council in a
Work Session on February 23, 2022 (Minutes, p. 1-4) to discuss the proposal. The City Council expressed
support for the commercial component of the project and urged the Applicant to develop the commercial
pads before, or concurrently with, the residential component. At that time, up to 50 townhome units and a
minimum 62,000 square feet of commercial space were anticipated.

The Applicant submitted an SD Zone Application to the Planning Department on September 26, 2022. The
Planning Commission reviewed the proposal on December 7, 2022 (Minutes, p. 8-14) and discussed the
                                                    Application 2024-17 – SD-X The Bluff at Point Crossing Text Amendment
                                                                            August 21, 2024, Planning Commission Meeting
                                                                                                                    Page 1
amount of proposed commercial space and proposed land uses. The Planning Commission continued the
item to January 4, 2023 (Minutes, p. 2-7) with revisions to the amount of commercial space entitled in the
Zone. The Planning Commission forwarded a positive recommendation of the proposal to the City Council on
January 4, 2023, and the City Council approved Ordinance 2023-03 on January 25, 2023 (Minutes, p. 13-16),
enacting the new Zone.

The Applicant proceeded with a geotechnical study of the site and found a layer of silt in the area that would
not be stable enough for development. The Applicant regraded the site to remove this instability and
establish a 3:1 slope across the property, however, this resulted in the developable area of the site shrinking.
The developable area has been reduced by roughly 30% and the Applicant has proposed the amendment of
the zoning ordinance to reflect this reduction in the entitlements. The Applicant proposes the reduction of
the residential component by 20% and the commercial area by approximately 35.5%.

BACKGROUND:
During the 2023 Legislative Session, the Utah State Legislature passed Senate Bill 43 with the intent of
streamlining the system for providing public notices. This legislation created two classes of notices: Class A and
Class B.

During the 2024 Legislative Session, the Legislature passed House Bill 256 which requires municipalities and
counties to provide a notice to military installations for new land use permits within 5,000 of a boundary with
military land. Staff has proposed the attached amendments to Section 11.30.060 of Bluffdale’s City Code to
incorporate the changes made by the State.

On August 28, 2024, the Utah Department of Transportation (UDOT) requested the City of Bluffdale provide a
notice of land use applications that could potentially impact high priority corridors in accordance with Utah
Code § 10-9a-206. These corridors are defined by UDOT and the intent of this notice is to allow time for the
agency to coordinate on right of way needs with the property owner(s). The City of Bluffdale will provide this
notice in a timely manner upon receiving a relevant land use application. UDOT’s request came to the City after
these proposed amendments received a recommendation from the Planning Commission, however, because

Re:    State Code Ordinance Updates: Noticing Requirements

       Application No.: 2024-29
       Applicant:       City of Bluffdale
       Request:         Consider proposed amendments to Section 11.30.060 of Bluffdale’s City
                        Code to incorporate changes made by the Utah State Legislature related to
                        noticing requirements.

SUMMARY

During the 2023 Legislative Session, the Utah State Legislature passed Senate Bill 43 with the intent
of streamlining the system for providing public notices. This legislation created two classes of
notices: Class A and Class B.

During the 2024 Legislative Session, the Legislature passed House Bill 256 which requires
municipalities and counties to provide a notice to military installations for new land use permits
within 5,000 of a boundary with military land. Staff has proposed the attached amendments to
Section 11.30.060 of Bluffdale’s City Code to incorporate the changes made by the State.

ANALYSIS

Land development entitlements in the State of Utah are divided into two categories: Legislative and
Administrative. Some examples of Legislative and Administrative items are included in the table
below:

                           Legislative                          Administrative
                 Zone Map Amendments                    Site Plan Reviews
                 General Plan Map Amendments            Subdivision Plats
                 Ordinance Amendments                   Conditional Use Permits

                                             Application 2024-29 – State Code Ordinance Updates: Noticing Requirements
                                                                           August 21, 2024 Planning Commission Meeting
                                                                                                                 Page 1
The City has broad discretion when it comes to the consideration of Legislative items since these
items are centered around the creation of policy. Administrative items, however, are tied to
standards that have already been adopted. If the proposal meets the standards, an administrative
application must be approved.

Public notice and input are important to the public process, and both Bluffdale City and the State of
Utah have enacted minimum requirements to provide public notice of pending applications. Over
the past few years, Bluffdale has updated its noticing requirements to comply with legislation and
adjust the noticing provisions to match the legislative/administrative categorization of each type of
application. Routine administrative applications that meet all standards proceed through a more
streamlined process. Bluffdale even exceeds the State’s requirements by mailing out notices to
property owners within 1,000 feet of certain land use applications; these applications are typically
legislative and public input is highly encouraged.

Section 11.30.060 of Bluffdale’s City Code currently shows a table depicting the noticing
requirements for each specific application. The proposed ordinance amendment would modify this
table to include labels for “Class A” and “Class B” notices and list where each type is required.

Class A notices include all of the following:
   - Posting on the Utah Public Notice website;
   - Posting on the City of Bluffdale’s official website;
   - Posting at City Hall; and
   - Posting on or adjacent to the public street/easement (if applicable; e.g. Vacation of a Public
       Street or Right of Way)

Class B notices require:
   - Mailing to Affected Entities;
   - Mailing to a specified geographical area; and
   - All items required for a Class A notice

Staff has also proposed the following modifications:
- Remove newspaper notice requirement; a newspaper notice is no longer required by State
   Law for land use applications.
- Classified conditional use permits as administrative land use applications, removing the
   mail-out notice requirement.
- Include additional notices provided to State and regional entities when preparing and/or
   amending a General Plan.
- Revise Annexation notice provisions to include a “Class B” notice and to refer noticing
   procedures directly to Utah Statute governing annexations.
