DISCUSSION ITEMS

Murray North Station Area Plan Workshop 1 - Discussion on Preliminary Concepts/Directions
and Implementation Steps for the Murray North Station Area Plan

Christine Richman, principal planner with GSBS Consulting, introduced herself and project
planner Erika Chmielewsk. GSBS was hired by Murray City, Millcreek City, and the Wasatch
Front Regional Council to complete a station area plan for the area around the North Murray
TRAX station. She explained that state law requires stationary plans for jurisdictions with fixed
rail stations to address development within a half-mile radius of the platform. The purpose is to
identify a shared community vision and implementation steps, focusing on opportunities,
constraints, affordable housing, and short and long-term strategic recommendations. She
showed a map of the half-mile radius around the station, encompassing parts of Murray and
Millcreek. She pointed out the major roadways and transportation infrastructure, such as I-15,
surrounding the area that impact connectivity. She noted there has already been a new
development, so the plan aims to build on that momentum and identify further improvements.
Once complete, the plan must be adopted by the city councils and get certified by the Wasatch

Front Regional Council and Utah Transit Authority. The timeline is a 20-year vision but with an
emphasis on actions for the next five years.

Ms. Richman confirmed the half mile radius circle on the map represents the area encompassed
by the plan, extending into Millcreek since the radius overlaps both cities. She explained the
planning process has three main phases:

1. Gathering information - They spent a lot of time walking the area, talking to stakeholders,
reviewing existing plans, and conducting the first phase of community engagement to understand
what's working well and what could be improved.

2. Identifying the preferred approach - Based on the input gathered, they are starting to identify
the preferred direction moving forward, which is why they are meeting with the Planning
Commissions now.

3. Creating the plan - The next stage will involve developing the graphics, maps, and
implementation plans to provide a roadmap for achieving the shared vision of the future.

In terms of what has been done so far, Ms. Richman said they mapped and analyzed data on
existing land use, transportation, crime statistics, demographics, and zoning. They also
conducted stakeholder interviews and public open houses. Key takeaways were desires for safer
and more walkable neighborhoods, a mix of housing options, new development that fits the
neighborhood character, and utilizing the station as an asset. She noted crime data showed this
area has higher crime rates than other parts of the cities. The early feedback will help shape the
preferred approach as they move into creating the final plan.

Vice Chair Milkavich asked what the timeframe was for the data used in the analysis.

Ms. Chmielewski said they used one year’s worth of data in the official analysis, but they did look
at around five years, noting that they’ve seen an increased trend in crime for the area.

Vice Chair Milkavich said she thinks there was less crime before the area was developed. With
the new development there is more crime. She says that more recently, crime has been
addressed differently, so they’re hoping to see a dip in crime.

Ms. Richman noted that crime statistics lag since they are not reported in real-time. The team
also analyzed other safety factors like crash data, pedestrian and bike safety issues, and
community assets. They launched a website to communicate with the public, conduct surveys,
gather input on maps, and enable conversations. They held several in-person events including
open houses, outreach at the climbing gym, and giving away coffee/food on the TRAX platform.
Stakeholder meetings included city staff, police, UTA, residents, business owners, and regional
experts. One-on-one interviews were also conducted. She showed the existing land use map,
noting the area's industrial history. When the station first opened, this was one of the first areas
to see new housing and transit-oriented development, transitioning away from industrial uses.
That development momentum has continued to spread outward over time.

Commissioner Hacker asked how much property within that half mile is owned by individuals or
businesses, Mill Creek, Murray City or UTA.

Ms. Richman said that most of it is owned by private landowners. UTA owns a small portion of
parcels, but Salt Lake County owns a large portion.

Ms. Richman provided a comprehensive overview of the ongoing discussions regarding the
development plans for Birkhill Boulevard. This area is currently being considered for
redevelopment by Murray and Salt Lake County in collaboration with the Utah Transit Authority
(UTA). The proposed plan involves extending Birkhill further south and redirecting it towards
Main Street, a move aimed at improving traffic flow in the vicinity. Notably, there is an existing
approval for a new apartment complex on a parcel owned by Evergreen, and Salt Lake County is
actively engaged in negotiations with potential developers to introduce housing on their own
parcel. This endeavor may also entail the acquisition of part of UTA's property, which currently
contains contaminated soils requiring cleanup, along with potential environmental concerns due
to its prior use as a public works facility. She emphasized the importance of careful land use
planning to minimize conflicts arising from adjacent industrial activities. This involves ensuring a
smooth transition from industrial to transit-supportive uses to enhance overall livability in the
area. Additionally, the plan aims to improve the connectivity of the grid network, addressing the
numerous roadways and railways that currently act as barriers hindering access to the station for
neighboring communities. The presented map showcased the age distribution of housing in the
area. Lighter shades represented older single-family homes, remnants of the pre-industrialization
era, while darker reds signified newer multifamily developments that have emerged since the
introduction of the tracks line. Ms. Richman noted a scarcity of ownership opportunities, with a
predominant presence of rental options. Striking a balance between renters and homeownership
was identified as an area of concern. Furthermore, there was an urgent need to enhance access
to social and economic opportunities, particularly groceries and services, especially for families
with children residing in the area. Ms. Richman highlighted the importance of providing access
to recreational facilities, especially for children and youth. Additionally, she emphasized the
necessity to expand transportation choices and connections, enabling various means of reaching
the station, such as biking and walking, in order to reduce reliance on conventional driving and
parking. Environmental considerations were also brought into focus, with a call to address
contamination and enhance Big Cottonwood Creek, creating more space for recreational
activities and trails. Additionally, there was a proposal to augment the tree canopy in the area, as
it was comparatively sparse compared to other parts of Murray and Mill Creek, which would
further contribute to overall livability. Ms. Richman discussed safety and security concerns in the
area, as depicted in the crime heatmap. Darker areas on the map represented higher crime
rates, which, although partially influenced by the concentration of households, were notably
elevated compared to other regions within the two cities. This underlined the need for careful
attention to safety measures and community policing efforts.

Ms. Richman expressed concern over Brick Gate Apartments, noting that area has experienced
crimes, particularly those related to traffic. They clarify that not all the incidents in these locations
are traffic-related, but they emphasize that there is a distinct issue in certain areas, particularly
evident in specific spots they point out. She stresses the importance of forward-thinking urban
design strategies. This includes implementing measures to enhance visibility, ensuring adequate
lighting, avoiding the creation of blind alleys, and establishing seamless connectivity within the
area. She also highlights the necessity of access control and regular maintenance to foster a
safe environment. Ms. Richman mentioned the dead-end areas within a portion of the location,
indicating that these may warrant attention. She addressed the problem of vacant storefronts,
which can give the impression of low activity levels despite a significant residential population.
She expressed a desire to explore methods to revitalize and enhance these aspects of urban
design, particularly in light of potential new developments.

Vice Chair Reid asked about responsibility for adding more trees along the creek, whether that
falls to developers, cities, or grants.

Ms. Richman responded it depends on whose property it is. For private property, requirements
could be incorporated into development approvals. For public trails, it could be funded through
capital improvement plans. Grants are also available from organizations like TreeUtah to
increase tree canopy.

Vice Chair Reid followed up asking specifically about Millcreek's ownership along Big
Cottonwood Creek and whether it's private or industrial.

Ms. Richman clarified that Millcreek does not own any of the land along the creek in this area.

Ms. Richman stated that connectivity is a major consideration in the station area. In addition to
the obvious barriers like major roads and rail, there are also gaps in the sidewalk network and
insufficient safe crossings of barriers like State Street. Millcreek's general plan includes a
potential new mid-block crossing on State Street that UDOT seems receptive to currently. More
crossings are needed to safely access bus stops like the common buses used by the
neighborhood south of the station. Riders going south currently have no safe crossing options to
legally access the bus stop traveling southbound. UTA has committed to adding more bus stops
to reduce the distance between stops and crossing opportunities. Other connectivity priorities
include improving bike and micro-mobility networks which are currently challenging. Urban
design and placemaking could better identify the station location which is hard to find currently.
Enhancing the identity and edges of the station area would make it more visible and welcoming.

Vice Chair Milkavich asked who would be responsible for improving the station's visibility - the
city or the state, since placement on state property or right-of-way poses challenges. She noted
potential limitations around municipal sign codes and expenses. She inquired about barriers if
the city wanted to place something on private property to better identify the station location.

Mr. Smallwood responded that Murray could place signage or other identifiers on city right-of-
way, but noted State Street would pose more challenges since it is under UDOT’s jurisdiction
However, he acknowledged there are opportunities to enhance station visibility that they can
continue to explore.

Ms. Richman noted that enhancements don't all have to happen immediately. There are already
small blue signs on State Street pointing to the station, but they get lost among other visual
stimuli. Parking has also been a frequent complaint from the area west of the tracks developed
under old regulations. Newer parking rules applied in the Birkhill area have not drawn as many
concerns.

Mr. Smallwood shared an anecdote from a Technical Advisory Committee meeting where they
probed about parking problems in Fireclay. When asked to identify specific locations with issues,
the committee members all pointed only to the west side where earlier development occurred
under old regulations. They indicated no parking problems on the newer east side like the Birkhill
area. Mr. Smallwood found it enlightening but discouraging that people associate the whole
Fireclay area with parking challenges based solely on the older west side projects. He felt it was
important to clarify the parking issues are isolated and not district wide.

Commissioner Soule asked why it’s so complicated to navigate getting out of the parking area,
stating that you have to go onto Main Street instead of directly onto 4500 South, making multiple
turns. She asked why that property can’t be accessed.

Vice Chair Milkavich said that the property is owned by Salt Lake County, who will not sell it.

Commissioner Nay also pointed out the grading issues with the property.

Mr. Smallwood pointed out that it’s also a UDOT-owned right-of-way, who limits access to their
property and would not allow a new road there.

Ms. Richman acknowledged there are parking issues specifically with older developments on the
west side of the station area, while parking elsewhere is generally well-utilized without being
overwhelmed. More parking options are desired for the west side. Market analysis shows strong
regional demand for retail, housing and eventually office. The goal is attracting investment
internally focused between State Street and 4500 South rather than spread along the major
thoroughfares. This requires creating an appealing environment, not just adding housing units,
since existing residents alone likely can't support significant retail. Residents, especially longer-
term, want a complete community with amenities, social interaction, and basic shopping within
walking distance. They value increased connectivity, high-quality urban design, safety
improvements, and an enhanced sense of place. Alternatives focused on either the station
platform or Main Street as the nucleus, but the preference is expanding the successful core
outwards to create a cohesive transit-oriented neighborhood. The connectivity vision includes
greenway trails along the rail, creek, and 4500 South, Main Street becomes a bikeway linking the
station to Salt Lake City and pedestrian bridges improve south access. Major moves are
extending Fireclay under the Union Pacific tracks to reach 300 West, and improving the Central
Ave underpass for cars, bikes, and pedestrians. This dramatically improves access to unlock

connectivity framework supports a more unified district.

Vice Chair Milkavich asked how realistic these concepts are.

Ms. Richman said there are factors that could help with implementing the ambitious connectivity
proposals, such as the federal Connecting Communities grant program. A grant application
focused on overcoming the railroad barriers could be promising, especially since the federal
Department of Transportation has more leverage engaging Union Pacific's cooperation.

Vice Chair Milkavich asked about the realism of the proposed north-south and east-west
transportation bypasses shown on the plan, including how much land purchase might be
required in the existing developed areas.

Ms. Richman explained the cities currently expand the Creekside trail through development
requirements as parcels redevelop. So, acquiring large swaths of land along the creek likely isn't
needed. The cities mandate creek setbacks that can then incorporate trails, so redevelopment
provides opportunities to implement the trail vision incrementally.

Commissioner Nay asked about the feasibility of a trail system along the creek on the east side
outside the development area, where there is significant hardscaping installed for flood control.
He inquired whether a trail along the creek is realistic in that area or if the hardscaping prevents
it.

Mr. Smallwood responded that continuing the creek trail eastward from the development area
would be very difficult since the land along that portion of the creek is largely residential
backyards in Millcreek and Murray. He suggested it's not realistic to extend it farther east in the
next 10-15 years. However, west of I-15 the land is owned by Salt Lake County, so connecting to
the Jordan River Parkway on that side could be more feasible. He recommended focusing efforts
on opportunities to go west once able to get under I-15.

Ms. Richman said that using the creeks, that a creek system is a sort of regional goal to help
with connectivity.

Chair LaMar asked about the idea of putting a park above 4500 South.

Ms. Richman clarified she was referring to the idea of "capping" 4500 South by covering the
below-grade highway to connect the areas on either side, similar to projects in other cities like
Dallas, Boston, and Phoenix. She acknowledged this is possible but would be very expensive.
From an infrastructure investment perspective, they want connectivity projects to unlock
sufficient development potential to justify the cost. A highway cap across 4500 South would
require more intensive development on both sides to warrant that level of investment. However,
in community input residents expressed a desire for maintaining the medium density character
rather than much higher intensity uses. So, while additional pedestrian bridges over 4500 South
are proposed, the plan does not currently recommend a full highway cap based on community
goals and the density needed to rationalize the expense.

this expanded right-of-way is not a short-term project but will take 10+ years. However,

request dedication to incrementally achieve the vision over time.

Ms. Richman noted they are thinking about phasing since this is a big plan covering a large area.
There is already strong momentum in the core that can be built outward from over time. The full
vision likely won't be realized for 10-15 years but documenting it now provides direction as
redevelopment allows incremental progress. She pointed out the Millcreek portion of the creek
trail was identified in a plan from 12 years ago, reinforcing the long timeline but the importance of
memorializing future hopes. It would be ideal to implement the full connectivity vision
immediately, but financial realities require a phased approach growing out from the existing
vibrant core. She explained the area currently has a high concentration of affordable housing
units. To attract desired services, amenities, and businesses, there needs to be greater housing
diversity including market rate and ownership options to increase disposable income. Affordable
housing should continue but in balance with other types. Public amenities and gathering spaces
are also key, like potentially repurposing part of the UTA lot for an urban plaza lined with
restaurants and activities. Urban trail concepts could incorporate adjacent cafes and patios. An
urban central plaza adds greenspace and a safe enclosed feel. The Main/4500 intersection is
ideal for a grocery store to serve the broader area, not just the immediate neighborhood. A public
market concept with fresh foods and vendors may be more feasible than a large store. Housing
above would boost units.

their residents' private use. She asked how to better interconnect everything and share amenities
between buildings beyond just trails and greenspace. She inquired whether certain public
amenities could be required of developers or incorporated to promote more cohesion.

Ms. Richman responded there would likely need to be some public investment in shared
amenities. She noted multifamily projects are required to provide amenities for their residents,
which is equivalent to a private backyard for a single-family home. But cities also furnish public
parks available to all residents, even those with backyards. Just because developments have
private amenities doesn't remove the need for public parks and spaces in the neighborhood for
broader interaction.

Commissioner Patterson referenced discussions in the MCCD about enclosed yet publicly
accessible spaces like plazas where amenities face a central gathering spot. She felt this
concept would be helpful in the station area so required development amenities interconnect
rather than remain restricted. The goal would be expanding access so residents aren't limited in
which amenities they can utilize within the broader community.

Ms. Richman clarified that it’s publicly accessible private space and stated that they do that when
they have a setback and are required to do a seating area.

Vice Chair Reid believed cities cannot collect park impact fees from developments to fund future
public parks and amenities. She mentioned coming from a place where large projects had to
contribute money for park dedication, which over time allowed the city to accrue funding to
purchase public parks.

Ms. Richman clarified that both Murray and Millcreek do impose park impact fees on new
development, exactly for the purpose Vicky Reid described. Each city charges a set amount per
housing unit that goes into a park impact fee fund. This money is then used to invest in new
public parks and amenities to maintain a consistent level of park service as the population grows,
rather than eroding service for existing residents. She confirmed there is existing funding
accruing that can help provide new public spaces in this area through the fees collected.

Commissioner Soule pointed out they have an opportunity with former industrial areas
transitioning to residential to designate new public park spaces, not just private amenities. She
questioned whether, for instance on the west side where new road connections are proposed,
they could identify a specific location for a new public park in the redevelopment areas while it is
still undeveloped. She felt with the conversion of old industrial properties, the cities should
consider designating some portion as new public open space rather than just allowing full
residential redevelopment without a park component.

Mr. Smallwood agreed with Commissioner Soule's point and said he understands the desire to
designate public park spaces, especially with former industrial areas transitioning. However, he
noted the west side is still segmented by the railroad barrier, so that portion will likely evolve over
10 years as connectivity improves. He explained the plan won't get to the granular level just yet.
The next phase will involve implementation details where they can specify code requirements for
a certain percentage of new development areas to be public open space. So, setting aside
specific park locations can happen in the more unified detailed planning the cities will continue
refining. However, the vision plan starts the process and intention of ensuring public spaces
amidst the redevelopment.

Ms. Richman acknowledged Mr. Smallwood raised a good point that the plan needs to address -
the existing park space per population is already below the level of service goals in both cities,
even before recent housing growth. While they have included some new park spaces, she

Chair LaMar suggested an idea for future consideration by the planning staff of both cities.
Rather than having a developer put all required amenities within their own project, perhaps they
could split them with another nearby project to concentrate investments in larger shared
amenities when strategic. For instance, a single consolidated dog park rather than small dog
runs at each site, then paired with another more substantial amenity. This could produce better
overall outcomes through synergies between projects versus having repetitive amenities in
isolation.

Ms. Richman noted that was a good idea and worth exploring.

Main/4500 location makes sense for visibility and regional access now, in 20 years an additional

walking who want to grab items after work. The plan should incorporate improving walkability
over time.

Mr. Smallwood discussed trying to identify feasible interim grocery locations. Using software
analyzing cell phone data, their economic development specialist explored where Fireclay
residents currently shop and eat. They learned State Street is such a major barrier that access to
the south side is very limited. Potentially a small location north of Parris RV, by the D I thrift

Ms. Richman clarified the public market concept they envision is not a full-scale grocery store,
which would require more parcel depth. The Parris RV site likely has enough depth, but smaller
parcels like the DI thrift store probably do not. In terms of size, the DI property could potentially
work but likely couldn't accommodate a full grocery store footprint.

Commissioner Soule suggested considering a smaller-format grocery like a Walmart

potentially fit in the neighborhood and attract people stopping by after work.

Ms. Richman noted certain grocery formats like Harmons Market target different demographics
than currently present in the neighborhood. And Walmart needs extensive parking. She
explained the next steps are to incorporate the commission's feedback into the preliminary plan
and then bring that back through staff review. They will start drafting goals, strategies and
implementation steps. They also loop back with developers and landowners to get their feedback
on the draft plan. The intent is to bring a more refined version back for additional guidance on
shaping the final plan. She expressed that she felt this was a great conversation and thanked
the commissioners for their time.
