STAFF RECOMMENDATION:

Based upon the information submitted in the application, supporting documentation, and analysis of the
relevant code sections and general plan goals, Staff recommends the Planning Commission move to
approve the preliminary subdivision plat to plat the two (2) parcels into one lot located at 3303 S 460 E,
based on the findings of fact, conclusions of law and conditions of approval for the subdivision in the staff
report.


Findings of Fact:

     1. On October 29, 2024, Jeremiah Larson submitted a Plat Application to plat two (2) parcels to plat
          parcels 16-30-453-0010-0000, 16-30-453-0020-0000.

     2. The purpose of the proposed subdivision plat is to plat and combine the two parcels to meet the
          6,000 sq ft R-1 district lot size minimum.

     3. The subject location 3303 S 460 E is in the R-1 Land Use District.

     4. The total development area of the one (1) lot is 0.212 (9210 sq ft) acres after consolidation.

     5. Staff has reviewed this subdivision application for compliance with South Salt Lake Municipal Code
          Sections 17.10.120, 17.10.130, 17.10.140, 17.10.150, 17.10.160, 17.10.170, 17.10.180, 17.10.190
          and 17.10.200, and found that this subdivision application is compliant with this section, as
          outlined above.

Conclusions of Law:

     1.    The plat, as specifically conditioned below, is consistent with the South Salt Lake City General Plan.

     2.    The plat, as specifically conditioned below, is compatible in use, scale, and lot size in the R-1
           district.

     3.    The plat, as specifically conditioned below, does not compromise the health, safety, or welfare of:
           Persons employed within or using the proposed development; Those residing or working in the
           vicinity of the proposed use or development; or Property or improvements in the vicinity of the
           proposed use or development.

     4.    The plat, as specifically conditioned below, does not impose disproportionate burdens on the
           citizens of the city.
                                                        9
Conditions of Approval:

     1.   The applicant will continue to work with City staff to make all technical corrections necessary prior
          to recording;

     2.   Prior to plat recordation and any additional development of the subsequent lot, the applicant must
          provide city staff the final plat mylar to include notarized signatures of owners’ consent to
          dedication; obtain signatures of all entities indicated on the subdivision plat attached hereto;

     3.   The applicant shall complete (or post an adequate improvement completion assurance), warrant
          and post required warranty assurance for all required landscaping and public infrastructure
          improvements including street lights;

     4.   The applicant shall ensure that all site improvements are installed consistent with the requirements
          of the South Salt Lake City Municipal Code;

     5.   The applicant shall dedicate an easement to the City of South Salt Lake for purposes of storm water
          management practices and ensure the plat includes language of the dedication of such easement,
          in a form as approved by the City Attorney’s Office;

     6.   The proposed development shall meet all requirements of the South Salt Lake Community
          Development Department, South Salt Lake Fire Marshal, the South Salt Lake Building Official, and
          the South Salt Lake Engineering department;

     7.   The applicant shall comply with all applicable International Building Codes and International Fire
          Codes and must obtain and complete a South Salt Lake building permit;

     8.   The applicant shall record the plat with the Salt Lake County Recorder’s Office within 2 years of
          receiving final plat approval or the applicant elects to install Infrastructure Improvements prior to
          Plat recordation, the applicant shall have applied for the proper permits, deposited a 10% Warranty
          Assurance and be actively working on the issued permit;

     9.   All items of the staff report.

SYNOPSIS:
Based on the direction staff received from three City Council Work Meetings, held on 9/13/23, 6/5/24, and
11/13/24, staff recommends adding certain regulations for Short-Term Rentals (“STR”) to the South Salt Lake
City Municipal Code (the “Code”), including requirements for hosts in the R1 District to be primary residents in
South Salt Lake, limitations on maximum occupancy, and minimum on-site parking requirements. The proposed
ordinance amendment includes adding STRs to Title 17 as well as correcting technical errors within Title 4 to
increase clarity and eliminate inconsistencies within the ordinance.

The Planning Commission is the recommending body for amendments to Land Use Ordinances and the City
Council is the Land Use Authority for amendments to Land Use Ordinances.

Staff have provided a summary and analysis of the proposed amendments below. For the full amendment to
Title 4, please see the attachments provided herein that include the full redlined proposed amendment to the
ordinance.

RECOMMENDATION:
Staff recommends the Planning Commission forward a recommendation of approval to the City Council for the
ordinance amending multiple sections of Title 4 and Title 17 of the South Salt Lake City Municipal Code to
correct technical errors, to add general regulations for Short-Term Rentals, and to update definitions. Staff has
also drafted alternate recommendations specific to the residency requirements for existing licensed businesses
and to the on-site posting requirements to less-conspicuous locations for the Planning Commission’s
consideration.

BACKGROUND:
The growth of the STR market has precipitated a host of neighborhood challenges, including noise complaints,
late-night parties, disruptive guests, parking, and trash issues, all of which disturb the quality of life for the
residents. Furthermore, the replacement of long-term community members and families with short-term renters
erodes the feeling of “community”, and diminishes alternatives for home ownership, where the cost of home
sales has increased through competition driven by investment and business purposes. A FlashVote survey was
conducted on STRs by city staff to gather statistically valid community input, a summary of the results is below:

When provided with a list of statements on STRs (select all that apply), residents responded:
  • 59% said they believe STRs should be subject to reasonable regulations on occupancy, parking, noise,
      etc.
  • 42% said STRs do not have significant negative impacts on my quality of life in South Salt Lake.
  • 35% said STRs have a negative impact on the affordability and availability of long-term housing in South
      Salt Lake.
When asked if SSL should regulate STRs and how (select up to three statements), residents responded:
  • 38% said STRs should be required to get a permit from the City.

   •   33% said to restrict the total number of STR permits in the City.
   •   51% said to cap the number of nuisance violations an STR owner is allowed to receive before their
       permit is revoked.

properties listed for rent, we believe there are approximately 200 STR listings in SSL. We currently have 45-

                                 Chapter 4.04 GENERAL PROVISIONS

4.04.010 Purpose.
       The city council City Council of South Salt Lake finds that a large number of dwelling Dwelling units within the
city City of South Salt Lake are occupied on a rental tenancy basis. The city council City Council of South Salt Lake
also finds that pursuant to constitutional and legislative authorization, the city City is permitted to license, regulate
and enact ordinances governing rental dwellings Rental Dwellings, mobile Mobile home parks and trailer courts.
The city council City Council finds that regulation is necessary due to a history of nuisance activities by tenants
Tenants, unsanitary conditions and unfit premises. As such, a rigorous housing program is necessary in order to
improve the quality of life for residents of the city City abiding in rental dwellings Rental Dwellings and mobile
Mobile home parks.

4.04.020 Definitions.
      As used within any chapter in this title:
    "Apartment complex" means a parcel of real property, or a series of contiguous parcels sharing a common
owner Owner, containing ten or more rental dwelling Rental Dwelling units.
     "Background check" means a printed history of the tenant's criminal activity, either prepared by the Utah
Bureau of Criminal Identification, or a service which provides the same data.
      "Business license official License Official" means an employee, official or agent of the city City authorized to
review, issue, suspend and revoke business licenses and applications for licenses.
      "City" shall mean the City of South Salt Lake, Utah.
      "City Attorney" shall mean the duly appointed City Attorney.
      "City official" means any employee, official or agent of the city City authorized to administer or enforce the
provisions of the City Code.
      "Community Development Director" shall mean the duly appointed Community Development Director.
     "Complainant" shall mean a Person, including the Community Development Director, who files a complaint
under this chapter.
      "Conciliation" shall mean the attempted resolution of issues raised in a complaint filed under this chapter, or
raised in the investigation of the complaint, through informal negotiations involving the Complainant, the
respondent, and the Housing administrator.
      "Conciliation agreement" shall mean a written agreement setting forth the resolution of issues by
Conciliation under this chapter.
     "Criminal conduct" refers to both "serious Serious criminal conduct" and "minor Minor offenses" as those
terms are defined in this section.
      "Design professional" means an engineer or architect, duly and currently licensed under the laws of the state

                                 Chapter 4.05 SHORT-TERM RENTALS
4.05.010 – Purpose.
The purpose of this Chapter is to establish regulations and design standards for residential Short­Term Rentals
(STRs) located in single family and multi-family neighborhoods. These standards seek to allow for STRs while also
protecting the safety and general welfare of South Salt Lake residents and preserving the residential character of
South Salt Lake single family and multi-family neighborhoods. In allowing STRs, it provides existing property
homeowners economic relief who might otherwise be forced to leave a neighborhood, thus promoting, and
preserving affordable housing in South Salt Lake. This chapter also intends to stabilize neighborhoods by
promoting home ownership and preserving long term rental housing in the market.

4.05.020 – License Requirements
It is unlawful for any Person to Engage in the Business of Short-Term Rental unless that Person has first obtained a
business license from the business license office.

                            Chapter 4.12 GOOD LANDLORD PROGRAM

4.12.010 Purpose.
      The cost to provide municipal services to rental dwellings Rental Dwellings is greater than the cost to provide
services to owner-occupied Owner-Occupied dwellings Dwellings. Due to this added cost, disproportionate rental


                                                      Page 11 of 52
fees are assessed to pay the incremental cost of the disproportionate services. Pursuant to state law, however,
landlord Landlord who actively participate in the good landlord Landlord program are permitted to pay a reduced
fee, so long as they continue to comply with the provisions of this chapter.

4.12.020 Good landlord program.
A.   Every landlord Landlord who is "certified" by the city City pursuant to its "good landlord program" shall be
     entitled to a reduction in its per unit license fee in the amount set forth in the consolidated fee schedule.
B.   An exempt Exempt residential rental business is not required to pay a disproportionate impact fee. License
     fees for exempt Exempt residential rental businesses in the good landlord program are limited to the base
     licensing fee. Exempt businesses may still participate in the good landlord program and receive a discount in
     their base licensing fee.

4.12.030 Good landlord certification.
     To receive a "good landlord" certification from the city City, a landlord Landlord shall do the following:
     A.    Provide proof that the person Person or persons Persons responsible for the management of the rental
           housing has/have: (1) made arrangements with an entity authorized to complete background
           Background checks; (2) procured a lease form that allows prompt eviction for criminal acts or
           maintaining a nuisance; (3) completed good landlord training provided or approved by the city City
           prior to certification and once every three years thereafter. A property is exempt from the training
           requirement if the manager Manager or landlord Landlord maintains a designation as a certified
           property manager Manager with the Utah Division of Real Estate or is otherwise exempt pursuant to
           state law.
     B.    Within thirty (30) days of a change in the person Person or persons Persons responsible for the
           management of rental housing, provide proof to the city City that the new manager(s) Manager(s)
           has/have completed good landlord training provided or approved by the city City.
     C.    Enter into a good landlord agreement on a form prepared by the city City to institute the following
           practices:
           1.    Require a written lease for every rental unit.
           2.    Require that all adult occupants of the unit be listed on the lease.

                              Chapter 4.20 APARTMENT NUISANCES

4.20.010 Purpose and intent.
      It is the purpose and intent of this chapter to impose a legal duty upon apartment Apartment complex
owners to properly manage their properties to prevent them from deteriorating into havens for crime to the
degree that a nuisance is created, ruining the safe, peaceful and quiet enjoyment of property of innocent tenants
Tenants and of neighboring properties. The chapter creates a system to notify owners of the problems reasonably
attributable to their properties and imposes civil fines upon owners who neglect to take reasonable actions to
address problems.

4.20.020 Acts include causing, attempting, aiding and abetting.
     Whenever any act or omission is referred to or made unlawful in this chapter, it shall include causing,
attempting, permitting, aiding or abetting such act or omission.

4.20.030 Duty to properly manage.
      Every owner Owner of an apartment complex has a duty to provide adequate management to prevent the
creation of a nuisance to innocent tenants Tenants and to neighboring properties, resulting from multiple incidents
Incidents of criminal Criminal conduct by tenants Tenants and guests within a six-month period of time.

4.20.040 Nuisance declared.
A.   Pursuant to Title 10, Chapter 8, Section 60, of the Utah Code, the city City of South Salt Lake city council City
     Council declares that the quiet enjoyment of property is intolerably impaired and a public nuisance exists
     when, despite giving notice to the owner Owner and/or manager Manager of an apartment Apartment
     complex:
     1.    Seven or more incidents Incidents of serious Serious criminal conduct occur at an apartment
           Apartment complex within a six-month period of time. In any six-month period, each incident Incident
           of serious Serious criminal conduct in excess of seven shall constitute a separate nuisance and violation
           of this chapter;
     2.    Fifteen (15) or more incidents Incidents of minor Minor offense occur at an apartment Apartment
           complex within a six-month period of time. In any six-month period, each minor Minor offense incident
           Incident in excess of fifteen (15) shall constitute a separate nuisance and violation of this chapter; or
     3.    Five or more incidents Incidents of serious Serious criminal conduct in combination with ten or more
           incidents Incidents or minor Minor offenses occur at an apartment Apartment complex within a six-
           month period of time. In any six-month period, each subsequent incident Incident of serious Serious
           criminal conduct in excess of five and each subsequent incident Incident of a minor Minor offense in
           excess of ten shall constitute a separate nuisance and violation of this chapter.

4.32.010 Purpose.
A.    Every individual in the city City has the right to seek housing. Discriminatory housing practices are
      detrimental because they impede the social and economic progress of the city City by preventing all of the
      city’s City’s citizens from contributing to or fully participating in the cultural, spiritual, social and commercial
      life of the community, which is essential to the growth and vitality of the city’s City’s neighborhoods and
      businesses.
B.    The Utah Fair Housing Act, Utah Code Section 57-21-1 et seq., addresses housing related discrimination
      Discrimination based on race; color; religion; sex; national origin; familial status; source of income; and
      disability, but does not address discrimination Discrimination based on sexual Sexual orientation or gender
      Gender identity.
C.    South Salt Lake City has found that discrimination Discrimination in housing on the basis of sexual Sexual
      orientation and gender Gender identity must be addressed. The denial or deprivation of access to housing
      because of an individual's sexual Sexual orientation or gender Gender identity is detrimental to the health,
      safety, and welfare of the city’s City’s citizens and damages the city’s City’s economic well being. The purpose
      of this chapter is to provide a clear and comprehensive mandate for the prevention and elimination of
      discrimination Discrimination in housing in the city City against individuals based upon sexual Sexual
      orientation or gender Gender identity and this chapter shall be liberally construed to achieve that purpose.

4.32.020 Administration.

Person has a sexual Sexual orientation and a gender Gender identity.

4.32.090 4.32.080 Procedures for filing complaints.
A.   Any person Person who claims to have been injured by an unlawful housing practice may file a complaint
     with the city City recorder. A complaint may also be filed by the Community development director
     Community Development Director if the city City has reasonable cause to believe that a person Person has
     committed an unlawful housing practice. A complaint must be filed within one hundred eighty (180) calendar
     days after an alleged unlawful housing practice has occurred.
B.   A complaint must be in writing on a form provided by the city City recorder, made under oath or affirmation,
     and contain the following information:
     1.    The complainant’s Complainant’s name, address, and signature;
     2.    The date the alleged unlawful housing practice occurred;
     3.    A statement of the facts upon which the allegation of an unlawful Unlawful practice is based; and
     4.    The respondent's Respondent’s name and address.


                                                    Page 34 of 52
C.   Promptly after the filing of a complaint, the housing Housing administrator shall:
     1.   Provide the respondent Respondent named in the complaint written notice that a complaint alleging
          the commission of an unlawful housing practice has been filed against the respondent;
     2.   Furnish a copy of the complaint to the respondent; and
     3.   Advise the respondent Respondent of the respondent's Respondent’s procedural rights and
          obligations, including the right to file a written, signed, and verified informal answer to the complaint
          within fifteen (15) days after service of notice of the complaint.
D.   Not later than the fifteenth day after service of the notice and copy of the complaint, a respondent
     Respondent may file an answer to the complaint with the housing Housing administrator. The answer must
     be in writing, made under oath or affirmation, and contain the following information:
     1.   The respondent's Respondent’s name, address, telephone number, and signature of the respondent
          Respondent or the respondent's Respondent’s attorney, if any; and
     2.   A concise statement of facts in response to the allegations in the complaint, including facts of any
          defense or exception.

4.32.100 4.32.090 Investigation.
A.   Upon the filing of a complaint, the housing Housing administrator shall commence an investigation to
     determine the facts behind the complaint and whether there is reasonable cause to believe the respondent
     Respondent committed an unlawful housing practice, except that no investigation may commence if, after
     reviewing the allegations of the complaint, the housing Housing administrator determines that the complaint
     does not come within the scope of this chapter. Upon determining that a particular complaint does not come
     within the scope of this chapter, the housing Housing administrator shall dismiss the complaint, notify the
     complainant Complainant and the respondent Respondent and take no further action.
B.   In connection with any investigation of a complaint filed under this chapter, the housing Housing
     administrator shall seek the voluntary cooperation of any person Person to:
     1.   Obtain access to premises, records, documents, individuals, and any other possible source of
          information;
     2.   Examine, record, and copy necessary materials; and
     3.   Take and record testimony or statements of any person Person reasonably necessary for the
          furtherance of the investigation.
C.   The Community development director Community Development Director may request the city attorney City
     Attorney to issue an executive branch subpoena or subpoena duces tecum to compel the attendance of a
     witness or the production of relevant materials or documents.
D.   The Community development director Community Development Director may dismiss a complaint during the
     investigation if the housing Housing administrator determines that:
     1.   The complaint was not filed within the required time period;
     2.   The location of the alleged unlawful housing practice is not within the city’s City’s jurisdiction;
     3.   The alleged unlawful housing practice is not a violation of this chapter;
     4.   The complainant Complainant refuses to cooperate with the housing Housing administrator in the
          investigation of the complaint or enforcement of an executed conciliation Conciliation agreement;
     5.   The complainant Complainant cannot be located after the housing Housing administrator has
          performed a reasonable search; or




                                                    Page 35 of 52
     6.    A conciliation Conciliation agreement has been executed by the complainant Complainant and
           respondent.

4.32.110 4.32.100 Conciliation.
A.   During or after the investigation, but subsequent to the mailing of the notice of the complaint to the
     respondent Respondent, the housing Housing administrator shall, at the request of the Community
     development director Community Development Director, attempt to conciliate the complaint if it appears
     that the respondent Respondent has committed an unlawful housing practice. In conciliating a complaint,
     the administrator shall try to achieve a just resolution and obtain assurances that the respondent
     Respondent will satisfactorily remedy any violation of the complainant’s Complainant’s rights and take action
     to ensure the elimination of both present and future unlawful housing practices. A conciliation Conciliation
     agreement may include: sensitivity training for the respondent Respondent and/or the respondent's
     Respondent’s employees; the respondent's Respondent’s agreement to adopt and pursue a policy of
     nondiscrimination in its practices; and the respondent's Respondent’s agreement to not engage in
     discriminatory practices in the future.
B.   A conciliation Conciliation agreement executed under this section must be in writing in a form approved by
     the city attorney City Attorney and must be signed and verified by the respondent Respondent and the
     complainant Complainant, subject to approval of the Community development director Community
     Development Director who shall indicate approval by signing the agreement.
C.   If a respondent Respondent voluntarily enters into a conciliation Conciliation agreement, the Community
     development director Community Development Director shall immediately dismiss the complaint.

4.32.120 4.32.110 Disposition of a complaint.
A.   If, upon completion of an investigation of a complaint, the Community development director Community
     Development Director determines that an unlawful housing practice has occurred and is unable to secure an
     acceptable conciliation Conciliation agreement from the respondent, then the Community development
     director Community Development Directorshall refer the case to the city attorney City Attorney. The
     Community development director Community Development Director shall refer the entire file to the city
     attorney City Attorney, who shall determine how best to pursue further action, if any, on the complaint.
B.   If the city attorney City Attorney determines that cause exists that an unlawful housing practice occurred and
     the facts are sufficient to warrant the initiation of an administrative action, then the city attorney City
     Attorney shall provide written notification to the respondent Respondent and the complainant Complainant
     that an action to enforce this chapter may be submitted to the administrative law judge. If the city attorney
     City Attorney determines that there is no cause that an unlawful housing practice occurred or that the facts
     are insufficient to warrant the initiation of an action by the administrative law judge, the city attorney City
     Attorney shall provide written notification to the respondent Respondent and the complainant Complainant
     and notify the Community development director Community Development Director, who shall then dismiss
     the complaint.

4.32.130 4.32.120 Offenses and penalties.
      A person Person violates this chapter if the person Person intentionally or knowingly violates a provision of
this chapter or if the person Person intentionally or knowingly obstructs or prevents compliance with this chapter.
An offense committed under this chapter by a respondent Respondent owning or operating twenty (20) or fewer
dwellings Dwellings is punishable by a fine of not more than five hundred dollars ($500.00). An offense committed
under this chapter by a respondent Respondent owning or operating twenty-one (21) or more dwellings Dwellings
or by a real Real estate broker or salesperson is punishable by a fine of not more than one thousand dollars
($1,000.00).

 Dear Planning Council Members,


 I am writing to express my concern regarding the proposed short-term rental (STR) legislation in South Salt
 Lake. My name is Emmanuelle and I own and manage one STR in South Salt Lake. My husband and I
 purchased the home in late 2024 and legally operate the STR with a city-approved business license. I
 understand that the city’s intention is to keep the spirit of South Salt Lake alive with caring local citizens,
 provide safe and affordable housing, and stimulate the local economy. However, creating this prosperous
 environment does not have to come at the expense of the STR community – at least to the extent that is
 being proposed. I recognize that a balance must be achieved and with that, I share my concerns and
 solutions below:
         · Requiring STR owners to reside in South Salt Lake – this is of greatest concern as it directly
         affects out of state homeowners such as myself. In fact, it penalizes those of us who went through
         the proper channels to obtain a business license and legally operate a STR.
                 o My husband and I made a major decision to invest in South Salt Lake – we have been
                 visiting the area for many years and not only purchased a home, but spent significant money
                 to renovate it. This home purchase and renovation increases our property taxes, which
                 directly benefits the city and the state. By excluding out of state owners, you are removing a
                 pool of hardworking people who are not only beautifying neighborhoods, but directly
                 contributing to the local economy. When we purchased this home, we made a promise to be
                 dutiful owners who operate a safe, friendly STR. We have upheld this promise and hope the
                 city will return the promise it made to us when it granted us the business license.
                 o In addition to the economic impact we provide through our property taxes, we also
                 contribute to the local economy every day. We employ local cleaners, handymen, hot tub
                 service technicians, landscapers, and more. Additionally, our guests visit local restaurants
                 and shops, which helps to support local businesses. As you can see, an STR directly
                 contributes to the local economy in many ways.
                 o We care deeply about our home and neighborhood. The STR market is incredibly
                 competitive and just one bad review can terribly affect our business. Accordingly, we ensure
                 that our guests are properly screened, our lawn is clean, and the home is orderly.
                 o Lastly, there is legal precedent against the proposition that would require owners to reside
                 in South Salt Lake. In this article, you can see that the city of New Orleans in 2019 passed a
                 law restricting licenses for STRs to city residents and excluding out of state owners. In 2022,
                 the 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that the provision unconstitutionally restricts
                 interstate commerce. With similarly worded language in the new STR provision in South Salt
                 Lake, the U.S. Court of Appeals would very likely rule again against the city and in favor of
                 out of state owners.
                 o SOLUTIONS:
                          § grandfather existing, licensed out state owners
                          § require a local contact to ensure orderly guest conduct and home maintenance
                          and/or
                          § institute a cap for out of state owners, thereby limiting the number who may obtain
                          an STR license

         · If the goal is to ensure that all licensed STRs are licensed, we are currently discussing the state
         of 40 units, which represents a tiny fraction of the overall housing supply.
                o While the number of housing units in South Salt Lake is not publicly available, U.S. Census
                data states that between 2019-2023, there were 10,707 households. If we divide the 40
                licensed STRs by the 10,707 households, it shows we are discussing the state of 0.37% of
               households in South Salt Lake. While I completely agree with the Council’s stance that we
               need prioritize owner-occupied homes, clearly 0.37% is a small minority of the overall
               housing supply.
               o SOLUTION: if the concern is that the number of licensed STRs could balloon beyond the
               existing 40, I recommend instituting a maximum number of permits at any given time. This
               could be an absolute number (for instance, 250 STRs) or a percentage (for instance, only
               20% of a given neighborhood can be STRs).

       ·   The ~40 properties that are currently operating STRs legally with permits are being punished.
             o For the ~40 of us properties that are licensed units, we are potentially going to be

Thoughts on New STR Regulations in South Salt Lake
Jeremy Jareckyj                                                                         Tue, Jan 7, 2025 at 3:04 PM
To: "planning@sslc.gov" <planning@sslc.gov>


 To the Planning Members with the City of South Salt Lake,
 I would like to share my thoughts regarding the requirement for short-term rental (STR) owners to live
 specifically within South Salt Lake (SSL). While I understand the intention behind this proposal—ensuring
 owners are connected to the community—I believe it presents significant challenges and unintended
 consequences for STR operators and the local economy.
 Requiring STR owners to reside in SSL would force many existing properties, both licensed and unlicensed,
 to transition to mid-term rentals (30-day minimum stays). This is because a significant number of STR
 owners likely do not live in the immediate area. Forcing such a shift could have negative effects, including
 reduced housing flexibility for visitors, decreased tourism options, and economic losses for the community.
 The council has suggested that living in SSL would help owners better understand and align with community
 values and goals. However, I respectfully disagree with the idea that someone from Northern Utah, or even
 further afield, inherently cares less about the community than a resident of SSL. The vast majority of STR
 owners take pride in their properties and the broader state of Utah. For us, maintaining a clean, safe, and
 visually appealing property—both inside and out—is not only a reflection of our care but also a necessity.
 Positive reviews are the lifeblood of any successful STR, and neglecting property standards quickly leads to
 poor reviews, fewer bookings, and business failure.
 Many responsible property owners—particularly those who do not live locally but remain fully compliant
 and licensed as short-term rental (STR) operators—purchased their properties with the explicit intent to use
 them as STRs. These owners have made substantial investments in purchasing, improving, furnishing, and
 maintaining these properties, with financial calculations predicated on their legal use as STRs.

 For many of these families, including those who reside outside the immediate area, their properties serve
 as both an investment and a personal retreat. They often use their homes for days, weeks, or months
 throughout the year while relying on STR income to offset the significant costs of ownership. These families
 also contribute to the community by paying property taxes, maintaining the appearance of their homes, and
 supporting the local economy through hiring staff such as cleaners, landscapers, pest control providers,
 handymen, and other service professionals. Revoking the ability to operate legally licensed STRs would have
 a devastating financial impact—not only on these hardworking families but also on the locally employed
 professionals and small businesses that depend on this sector.

 Moreover, such a decision would likely lead to costly litigation that could drag on for years, as seen in other
 cities such as Dallas, Texas. In that case, ongoing lawsuits regarding STR restrictions have now surpassed
 three years and have consumed significant public time and resources. If South Salt Lake were to take similar
 action, it could face challenges both from individual property owners, the Airbnb legal department, and
 from organizations like the Utah Association of Short-Term Rental Owners (UASTRO), which has grown
 substantially in membership and resources. This association is already aware of South Salt Lake's proposed
 changes and takes these matters very seriously.


 City Of Dallas Example -
I am writing to share a relevant case from Dallas, Texas, which underscores the legal challenges and financial
implications cities can face when enacting restrictive ordinances targeting short-term rental (STR) operators.
In December 2022, the City of Dallas attempted to enforce an ordinance banning short-term rentals in
single-family residential neighborhoods, even for operators who had been legally running their businesses
for years. This ordinance, which was approved by the City Council in June, faced immediate legal challenges.
STR owners, represented by the Dallas Short-Term Rental Alliance, filed suit, arguing that the ordinance was
unconstitutional and caused irreparable harm to their livelihoods.
Dallas County District Judge Monica Purdy granted an injunction, halting enforcement of the ordinance, and
emphasized that STR owners had made substantial financial investments in reliance on their legal rights to
operate. The judge’s ruling pointed out that the ban could be seen as “unconstitutionally oppressive” and
highlighted the significant economic impact on property owners. She noted that the rights of STR operators
are tied to property ownership in Texas, creating vested interests that cannot be easily overridden.
This case demonstrates the legal and financial risks associated with retroactively limiting previously lawful
activities. If South Salt Lake City pursues similar measures, there is a high likelihood of prolonged litigation
and substantial costs, as well as economic disruption for property owners who relied on their business
licenses in good faith.
I encourage the Council to consider the potential consequences of such actions and to explore balanced
regulations that respect the rights of property owners while addressing community concerns.



Proposed Solution
Rather than imposing a strict residency requirement, I suggest a more flexible and balanced approach:
     1. Owner Residency Range: Allow STR owners who live within a reasonable distance—such as a
        specific mileage or time-based radius of the property—to hold a valid business license. This ensures
        that owners remain close enough to monitor, manage, and care for their properties effectively.

Additionally, all currently licensed units (approximately 40 properties) should be grandfathered under the
existing regulations. This means that while they would be required to comply with new occupancy limits,
signage requirements, and parking guidelines, they would not be subject to new zoning or owner-
occupancy restrictions. Any new permit applications submitted after the notice is sent out (e.g., 170+
currently unlicensed properties) would need to adhere fully to the new regulations. This approach provides
a fair transition, ensuring compliance while protecting legally operating owners from unnecessary hardship
and avoiding potential lawsuits.


     2. Local Property Managers/Hosts: For STR owners who reside outside the approved range, require
        the appointment of a local property manager or responsible party who lives within the designated
        area. This individual would serve as the primary point of contact for the city and the community,
        ensuring swift responses to issues, ongoing compliance with local regulations, and proactive
        property oversight. This model has been successfully implemented in cities like Heber City, Utah,
        where it has proven effective in balancing community interests and STR operations.

Why This Works
This balanced approach achieves the council’s goals while addressing the practical realities of STR
ownership. It:
       Promotes local oversight and accountability.
       Ensures STRs operate responsibly and align with community values.
       Supports tourism and the local economy without unnecessarily restricting property rights.
In conclusion, responsible STR owners already prioritize the quality, safety, and appearance of their
properties—regardless of where they live. Allowing a fair residency radius or requiring a local property
manager strikes an appropriate balance between community interests and property owner rights.
Short-term rentals contribute to the vibrancy of South Salt Lake by supporting tourism, enhancing local
businesses, and providing visitors with unique lodging options. A fair and practical regulatory framework—
such as the one proposed above—will allow STRs to thrive while ensuring accountability, neighborhood
harmony, and community goals are upheld.


By adopting this solution, South Salt Lake can strike a balance between addressing community concerns,
maintaining economic benefits, and protecting the rights of responsible property owners who have
operated ethically and legally. This approach also reduces the risk of prolonged litigation, protects local jobs,
and ensures the continued contribution of STRs to the city’s economy.




---Additional Thoughts on other Proposed Items/Other----


Long Term Rentals:
Many properties that are used for short-term rentals (STRs) are specifically furnished, designed, and
marketed to cater to the unique needs of transient guests. These properties often feature high-quality
furniture, amenities, and flexible booking arrangements, which make them highly attractive to short-term
visitors. As a result, STR owners will be reluctant to transition to long-term rentals, as doing so would
significantly alter the financial dynamics, flexibility, and usage of the property.
In practice, most STR owners facing restrictions in South Salt Lake are likely to first pivot toward Mid-Term
Rentals (MTRs), which are in high demand in the region. These rentals typically cater to professionals,
contractors, and tourists who require housing for stays ranging from 30 to 90 days. The shift from an STR to
an MTR is relatively simple—owners only need to adjust their booking calendars to restrict stays to a
minimum of 30 days. Both STRs and MTRs are generally managed in similar ways, with only slight variations
in terms of lease length and tenant interaction.
There will no longer be the transient /hotel tax collected automatically from every booking, which mean
less money coming in to the local government in this way. This fee will NOT be collected when they are
rented as a 30-night+ mid-term furnished rental.
According to Utah state law and Salt Lake County regulations, any rental that exceeds 30 days is classified as
a long-term rental. If further restrictions are imposed on STRs in South Salt Lake, many owners will simply
convert their properties into MTRs. This transition is an appealing option, as the rental income from MTRs
tends to be significantly higher than that of traditional long-term rentals, which are often capped at lower
market rates.
Moreover, converting to an MTR also provides additional benefits for property owners:
     1. Lower Fees: Once an owner switches to an MTR model, they will no longer be subject to the higher
        STR licensing fees. Instead, they will fall under the standard landlord program, which generally
        carries lower regulatory costs.
     2. Greater Flexibility: With mid-term rentals, owners retain more control over the property, can avoid
        dealing with long-term tenant issues, and still capitalize on the higher rental rates associated with
        more flexible lease structures.
Ultimately, these financial and operational incentives mean that STR owners in South Salt Lake are likely to
transition to MTRs rather than long-term rentals, should further STR restrictions be enacted. This shift could
result in fewer available properties for short-term visitors, limiting tourism and economic activity in the
area, while increasing the supply of mid-term rental options instead.

Short Term Rentals Guests/Economy of South Salt Lake:
       Short-term rental guests significantly contribute to the local economy, especially in areas with a
       vibrant tourism scene. While many of these guests are staying for just a few days, they typically
       spend money at local businesses—restaurants, shops, and breweries—enhancing the overall
       economic activity of the area. Unlike traditional hotel guests, STR visitors often prefer a more
       personalized experience and actively seek recommendations from their hosts. This means that many
       guests make a conscious effort to eat, shop, and explore locally, contributing directly to the
       businesses that rely on tourism for their revenue.
       For example, guests staying in short-term rentals may inquire about local dining options, favorite
       spots for shopping, and nearby entertainment or cultural experiences. Hosts, who often have a deep
       knowledge of the area, provide suggestions that encourage guests to support neighborhood
       businesses rather than opting for chain restaurants or large shopping centers. This type of spending
       has a multiplier effect on the local economy, as money spent at a locally owned business often stays
       within the community, benefitting other local suppliers and service providers.
       This behavior benefits not just small businesses but also local government revenues from increased
       taxes on restaurant sales, retail purchases, and entertainment. As the number of STRs grows, their
       role in driving economic activity becomes more apparent.
       In conclusion, short-term rental guests not only help support local businesses but also create a
       stronger sense of community involvement, where both residents and visitors actively contribute to a
       thriving local economy.


Another important factor to consider is how many local small businesses rely on short-term rentals (STRs) to
stay in business. In South Salt Lake, numerous service providers—such as handymen, landscapers, cleaners,
hot tub companies, and others—are able to thrive because they directly support the operation and upkeep

Without the constant turnover of STRs, many of these small businesses would face a significant loss of
revenue, putting their operations at risk. These service providers form a critical part of the STR ecosystem,
and without the STR market, their businesses might struggle to survive, which could lead to job losses or
even closures.
By supporting STRs, we are not only helping property owners but also supporting a wide network of local
small businesses that depend on them to sustain their operations and contribute to the local economy.

Dallas Restricts Short Term Rentals
• In December twenty twenty-three, the City of Dallas attempted to
  enforce an ordinance banning short-term rentals in single-family
  residential neighborhoods, even for operators who had been legally
  running their businesses for years.
• This ordinance, which was approved by the City Council in June, faced
  immediate legal challenges. STR owners, represented by the Dallas
  Short-Term Rental Alliance, filed suit, arguing that the ordinance was
  unconstitutional and caused irreparable harm to their livelihoods.
Effects of New Regulations in Dallas
• Dallas County District Judge Monica Purdy granted an injunction,
  halting enforcement of the ordinance, and emphasized that STR
  owners had made substantial financial investments in reliance on
  their legal rights to operate. The judge’s ruling pointed out that the
  ban could be seen as “unconstitutionally oppressive” and highlighted
  the significant economic impact on property owners.
• Judge Purdy noted that the rights of STR operators are tied to
  property ownership in Texas, creating vested interests that cannot be
  easily overridden.
City of New Orleans Restricts Short Term Rentals
• In August of 2019, City Council voted 7-0 to ban the short-term
  rentals of whole houses not occupied by the owners. It also puts
  limits on the number of short-term rentals on commercial properties
  and bans all short-term rentals in most of the historic French Quarter
  and the Garden District after previously being permitted
Effects of New Regulations in New Orleans
• The 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruled months later that the
  provision unconstitutionally restricts interstate commerce and
  claimed “the burden it imposed was not ‘clearly excessive in relation
  to the putative local benefits.’”
• Appeals court judges said the city must look at alternatives that don’t
  eliminate out-of-state property owners, such as limits on the number
  of licenses issued in a given area while allowing existing operators to
  continue.
A sudden ban/restriction not only undermines these investments but also
risks being deemed "unconstitutionally oppressive" by infringing on vested
property rights. In Utah, property ownership confers certain rights that cannot
be easily overridden without violating constitutional protections. Cities must
honor their prior permissions to maintain legal integrity and protect the
economic interests of their residents. This precedent serves as a strong
warning against arbitrary regulatory reversals that jeopardize lawful business
operations.
