V.      Consideration of Resolution 2025-23 - Providing for the Creation of a Local Building
        Authority by the City Council of the City of Holladay and Related Matters.
Council Member Paul Fotheringham believed the Local Building Authority (“LBA”) allows there to
be flexibility with regard to how the bond is issued. This retention of flexibility does not relate to an
expansion of scope, but there would be some flexibility available in case the revenues do not come in
as projected.

It was reported that under Utah Law, there are several ways cities can choose to finance their projects.
The role of the Fiduciary is to look at what is best for the City. This is not just what is best for today,
but what is best in the long term as well. There are two likely ways this could be financed, including
through a pledge of sales tax bonds or using an LBA. The structure of an LBA works similar to a
home mortgage. The building and park will act as a security for the bondholders. Sales tax and
property tax will not be pledged. The interest rate is a bit higher, but after discussion with the City
Council, it was determined that there are future needs to consider, such as road reconstruction. It
makes logical sense to pursue the City Hall building through an LBA. This retains the capacity to
pledge sales tax and franchise tax revenues to other bonds, like a road. An LBA is a non-profit and
the City will have the right every year to determine whether or not it will make the lease payment,
which is similar to the structure of a home mortgage.

An LBA is an entity that is created and required by State Law under the Local Government Bonding
Act as well as the Local Building Authority Act. It is a not-for-profit that is able to conduct these
types of financing activities. Every State is different in how it allows for this mechanism, but most
States do allow for it. In Utah, the case law as well as the State Statutes require it to be done through
an LBA. The LBA cannot do much without t approval from the City Council. It was clarified that
the LBA is where the assets remain and those assets are then leased to the City. The City makes
payments to the LBA and the LBA makes payments to the bondholders.

City Attorney, Todd Godfrey, noted that there has been some discussion about the collateral that
could be pledged to secure bonds. He clarified that it will not be secured with the property of the
park, but with the leasehold interest that the City has. Council Member Durham asked about the
collateral for that. Mr. Godfrey explained that it is all one bond and City Hall is being used as the
primary asset, with the leasehold interest that the City holds for the park, but not the ground itself. It
was added that the pledge of the collateral is a leasehold pledge. In the event that the authority does
not appropriate those payments, there would not be a traditional foreclosure where the assets were
sold off to creditors. There would be a foreclosure where the trustee came in and would sublease the
property for the remainder of the lease term to recoup as much as possible in terms of the lease
payments. There was additional discussion about payments and the role of the LBA.

       a.        Water Service in Holladay Discussion with Salt Lake City Public Utilities, Holliday
                 Water, and Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District.
Mayor Pro-Tem Gray reported that there are different representatives at the Work Meeting who will
share information about water service. Ms. Chamness explained that the presentation will start with
a review of a map that explains the boundary areas and then Jordan Valley Water Conservancy
District will start with their presentation. She reviewed the map and pointed out the areas served by
Holliday Water, Salt Lake City Public Utilities, and the Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District.

Shazelle Terry - Assistant General Manager with the Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District.
She currently oversees operations and maintenance. Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District is
primarily a wholesale water agency. There are 17 member agencies that are wholesaled to and that
is approximately 90% of the deliveries. There are approximately 8,000 retail connections, some of
which are in the City of Holladay. Those retail connections account for approximately 10% of the
deliveries. The number of connections in the City of Holladay are approximately 300. As far as the
infrastructure is concerned, there are three water treatment plants. The largest water treatment plant
is in Herriman and it can treat 180 million gallons per day. There are also treatment plants in Sandy
and West Jordan. In addition, there are water wells and water is purchased from other entities. The
sources are primarily the Central Utah Project and the Provo River Project. In addition, there is some
from the mountain streams south of Little Cottonwood Creek.

Ms. Terry shared a map that highlights the City of Holladay portion that the Jordan Valley Water
Conservancy District serves. The darker portion identifies the retail. When the district was set up in
1951, the mission was to serve unincorporated County areas in Salt Lake County, so there are still
some pockets of retail. In the 1960s, the district was young and most of the water supply was
purchased from the Metropolitan Water District of Salt Lake and Sandy. Jordan Valley Water
Conservancy District was looking into its own water sources. Part of the deal made was that
infrastructure and water would be provided to those who were part of the irrigation company.

Ms. Terry shared additional background information with the City Council, including when changes
were made to the drinking water regulations. She next discussed the water rates. There are low water
rates, especially for the retail areas. The intention is to keep the base charge low and then there are
four tiers. Those tiers are based on the meter size. In the summer, most people should be able to
remain within Tier 2 or Tier 3. If excessive watering is taking place based on the meter size, it would
fall into Tier 4. The rate structure is designed to encourage conservation. That is something the
district continues to look at. Rate updates are typically done every year or every other year. Ms. Terry
reported that there is currently a Rate Study underway.

Capital improvements were discussed. Ms. Terry reported that for the next four fiscal years, there
will be an intense period of capital spending. There are notable projects and Jordan Valley Water
Conservancy District is looking at bonding for approximately $230 million over the next four years.
Half of that will be used to expand the largest water treatment plant from 180 million gallons per day

to 255 million. This increase is to keep up with demand and the growth in the southwest portion of
Salt Lake County. Additional projects were reviewed with the Council.

Ms. Terry discussed some of the conservation goals and programs that are in place. The Jordan Valley
Water Conservancy District became serious about conservation in 2000. Efforts will be made to save
in terms of water delays projects until a future date. The plant expansion mentioned earlier was
originally planned for the year 2000, so the plant expansion has been put off for 25 years because of
the amount of water that was conserved. There is a three-pronged approach to conservation consisting
of education, incentives, and structural/regulatory changes. She shared images of the Localscapes
exhibit and reported that free classes are held throughout the year, including online classes.
Additional information about the conservation programs was shared. There are rebates and landscape
incentives. Ms. Terry reported that there is a $3 per square foot rebate for any lawn that is taken out
and replaced with waterwise landscaping, but certain criteria must be met. Utah Water Savers allows
anyone in the County to enter their address to learn about the incentive programs available to them.

Council Member Brewer asked for additional information. Ms. Terry reported that there are four
large water conservancy districts in the State that include the Jordan Valley Water Conservancy
District, Weber Basin Water Conservancy District, Central Utah Water Conservancy District, and
Washington County Water Conservancy District. Council Member Brewer mentioned the 300
connections in the City of Holladay and asked if those would continue in perpetuity. Ms. Terry
reported that Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District has approached the City of Holladay and Salt
Lake City a few times about taking over that water system. It might be worth having that conversation
again.

Council Member Brewer wondered if the rates take lot size into account. Ms. Terry explained that a
lot of places focus on lot size but someone might have a one-quarter-acre lot that is all lawn, while
someone else might have a one-quarter-acre lot with efficient landscaping. It is not necessarily fair
for the same amount to be paid when the circumstances are different. That is the reason meter size is
used. Council Member Brewer mentioned an area west of Olympus Junior High School and asked if
that is a case where water is being purchased from another company. Ms. Terry reported that there
are several connections. Council Member Fotheringham asked about the timeline for Casto Springs.
Ms. Terry explained that the design is currently underway, so it will be between 18 months and two
years before the project is finished. The objective is still to scale back on the retail service provided.

Council Member Fotheringham mentioned the significant capital improvements plan. He wondered
whether there would be an impact on City of Holladay retail customers. Mr. Terry explained that
there is an economy of scale associated with the district. In addition, the retail rates are competitive.
Council Member Brewer asked if there is free consulting available for Localscapes. Ms. Terry
confirmed that there is free consulting available and there is also a lot of information available online.

Jesse Stewart identified himself as the Deputy Director at Salt Lake City Public Utilities. Salt Lake
City Public Utilities is the biggest provider in the City of Holladay and one of the oldest water
providers in the west. He reported that Salt Lake City Public Utilities provides water to approximately
360,000 people across Salt Lake City, Millcreek, the City of Holladay, Cottonwood Heights, Midvale,
South Salt Lake, and others. There are EPA and DEQ regulations that are taken into account.
Mr. Stewart discussed why Salt Lake City Public Utilities serves the City of Holladay. He reiterated
that Salt Lake City Public Utilities is the historic water provider along the east bench and a lot of this

Holladay City Council Work Meeting Minutes 09/11/25                                            6
stems back to the exchange agreements from the late 1800s. In the 1800s, the farmers wanted a
constant supply of water for the irrigation season, and Salt Lake City wanted to have more pristine
water to treat. Utah Lake water was exchanged for irrigation, so it was possible to use the more
pristine water for culinary purposes.

Water-related infrastructure was discussed. Mr. Stewart reported that there are three water treatment
plants, with one in City Creek Canyon, one near the golf course in Parleys Canyon, and one at the
mouth of Big Cottonwood. Salt Lake City Public Utilities is also a member agency of the
Metropolitan Water District of Salt Lake and Sandy. There are seven water storage dams and
reservoirs, 1,300 miles of water lines, 650 miles of sewer, and 350 miles of stormwater. Mr. Stewart
shared information about water lines. Replacements are completed each year because some of the
water lines are older than others. There are a number of different pipe materials used.

As far as asset management, there is a fairly robust asset management program in place. Every piece
of infrastructure has a plan. Mr. Stewart explained that all of the assets are considered based on
criticality, which takes into account how critical something is to the system. There is a criticality
condition assigned and then a plan is created. That plan is updated each year when the budget cycle
takes place. There is an annual plan, a five-year plan, a 10-year plan, and a 30 to 50-year plan in
place. Certain factors are taken into account, including condition and criticality, as well as growth.
Different regulations are also considered. There is road work and agency coordination as well. For
example, there are meetings with Salt Lake City, Millcreek, and the City of Holladay to make sure
there is no project planned to take place shortly after a city project. There are then discussions about
financing. Sometimes, there are capital improvements, and other times, there are repairs that are
prioritized.

Mr. Stewart reviewed upcoming capital projects in the City of Holladay. The presentation slides
show near-term projects, which are one to three years, and short-term projects, which are three to five
years. For the Tanner Reservoir, the entire reservoir is being replaced. That directly impacts where
the water pressure comes from for a lot of Holladay. As for Heughs Canyon, there was a fairly
significant issue there recently and it is in the final stages of being addressed. Water is back in service.
The whole neighborhood will be looked at for replacements. In the short term, there is the 3900 South
pump station and pipelines. There is another area that will be looked into as well due to a history of
breaks.

Council Member Quinn mentioned Lisa Drive and it was confirmed that it is being examined. There
was additional discussion about Lisa Drive and some of the Council Member concerns. Mayor Pro-
Tem Gray asked for more information about the Tanner Reservoir replacement. Mr. Stewart
explained that there will be a replacement of the reservoir itself, but there is also valving associated
with the work. Mayor Pro-Tem Gray questioned whether this will improve the water pressure.
Mr. Stewart does not believe it will improve the water pressure. There will be a similar water pressure
but it will be a more sustainable, long-term project. He shared information about broader projects
that are planned. Details were shared about the water mains replaced in the last 20 years, as well as
information about water main breaks in the last five years. He identified areas on a map.

2025. One of the changes was to a year-round tiered structure rather than a tiered structure only in

Darren Shepherd from Holliday Water Company introduced himself to the City Council. He
explained that the water started with Spring Creek, but as the community grew, water rights were
acquired coming out of Big Cottonwood Canyon as well. Salt Lake City approached Holliday Water
Company and asked if it would be possible to have access to the water coming out of Big Cottonwood
Creek. There was agreement to this as long as some pipelines were built and maintained. There are
some mainlines that run through the City of Holladay, including Holladay Boulevard, Highland Drive,
Wander Lane, and Gunderson Lane. Those lines were built by Salt Lake and are also maintained by
them.

Mr. Shepherd reported that there are 4,000 customers that Holliday Water Company delivers water
to and approximately 3,000 are in the City of Holladay. The remaining 1,000 customers are located
in Millcreek. He described the boundaries and explained that a lot of the water supply comes from
Spring Creek. There is a water treatment plant there. In addition, there are four wells. During the
summer months, when people irrigate, the wells are turned on to provide the additional water that is
needed.

Mr. Shepherd reported that the customers own shares in the company. There is a quarterly billing
process. During the first and fourth quarters, there are 10,000 gallons of water received for free. In
the second and third quarters, 20,000 gallons of water are received for free. After that is the billing.
There is a tiered billing system in place, which was changed this past year. The rates are not promoted
or advertised, but there is communication with the owners. There are 50 miles of pipe in the ground
and there is a policy to replace one mile per year. Mr. Shepherd explained that there are nine
employees and four of them work on a crew. There are two treatment operators at the plant, and there
are two to three office staff members. He discussed the Holladay storm drain system.

Council Member Durham asked for additional information about the conservation efforts.
Mr. Shepherd explained that the shareholders are given information about how to conserve water.
Council Member Durham wanted to understand the rationale for the free water. Mr. Shepherd
explained that when the arrangement was made with Salt Lake City to process that water, there was
one million gallons of water coming in every day. That water does not cost them anything to process
and Holliday Water Company is owned by the customers. As a result, the water is provided to them.
Council Member Brewer asked the water quality reports. Mr. Shepherd explained that even though
they are privately owned, it is a public water system, so Holliday Water Company is subject to the
same regulations. There are certain standards that have to be met and the water has to be tested.
