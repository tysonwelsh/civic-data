Recycling Update
          In March 2020, the City suspended its curbside recycling program after learning the City's provider was not operating in
accordance with its conditional use permit (CUP). The Administration has been working with the provider to resolve the issues.
They have also been making efforts to identify other options to resume curbside recycling. Representatives from the Public
Services Department will provide an update on these efforts.
          Public Services Director Lowder used the aid of a PowerPoint presentation to discuss the City's recycling program; he
first provided a history of the creation and evolution of the program, after which he discussed the program's current status. After
suspending recycling operations with Recycled Earth due to violations of their CUP, the City has been taking all refuse (solid
waste and recyclables) to the Weber County Transfer Station. In 2016, five recycling companies responded to Weber County's
request for proposal for recycling services. Two out of those five are now out of business. In 2018, only two recycling companies
responded to our request for qualifications for recycling services. One of those two is now out of business. He then presented a
chart detailing costs of doing business with Recycled Earth; the chart compares the fees Recycled Earth committed to charge with
the actual charges based upon tonnage increases and cost differences. If the City had not suspended the program with Recycled
Earth, the City would have paid $91,675 more than has been spent on waste disposal to date. According to Recycled Earth,
recycling costs were $84.14 per ton in December 2019; that month the City delivered 327.19 tons to Recycled Earth, which would
have translated to a cost of $27,529.77. There are 22,094 customers with 20,235 recycling cans throughout Ogden City. The media
has indicated that increases in recycling costs would have cost each household with a recycling cart an additional $.09 per month,
but that is incorrect; based on conservative estimates developed before the program was suspended, the cost per cart would have
been an additional $1.25 per month. The cost to take material to Recycled Earth is roughly double what the City is paying for
tipping fees at the transfer station. He then discussed other recycling options; in order to transport the City's recyclables to other
recycling facilities, it would be necessary to hire a company to provide that service. MBI, is the third-party company that
transports refuse for Republic Services out of the Weber County Transfer Station. MBI is contracted through a bidding process.
Weber County pays $24.99 per ton to haul recyclable waste out. Weber County employees load the transport trucks for MBI to
haul to a Republic Waste owned facility in Tooele County. The City considered the Weber County Transfer Station as an option
for operating a City Recycling Transfer Station. After contacting Weber County, the City was told they do not have room in their
building, as co-mingled waste cannot be stored outside. In order to transport, the City needs a transfer station of its own; this
would include a facility, equipment, employees to load the hauling trucks, in addition to other operational costs. The City would
also need to consider the cost per tonnage to be paid to a recycling facility and the added cost per ton the City would pay a hauling
company. Mr. Lowder then discussed other local municipal recycling programs in Logan, North Salt Lake, Brigham City,
Clearfield, West Bountiful, and Salt Lake City. The curbside programs in these cities range from being independently managed by
the municipality to being supported by a contract with a private entity or special service district. He then discussed the recycling
options currently available to Ogden City:
               • Recycled Earth: offers a variable rate which will be very difficult to budget for. Would not need a transfer
                    station nor third party transport. Local business with the least amount of haul distance.
               • Mountain Fiber: 46.8 miles from our Public Services building, or 93.6 miles round trip. They collect
                    recyclables from Cache County. They claim that they receive about 70% good recyclables with 30% waste.
                    They expressed concern about the extra recyclables we would haul to their facility. They declined to provide a
                    quote. Would need transfer and transport.
               • Rocky Mountain Recycling: 39.5 miles from our Public Services building, or 79 miles round trip. They
                    declined to provide a quote as to how much they charge per ton. Most likely negotiated in a contract. Would
                    need transfer and transport.
               • Waste Management: 40 miles from our Public Services building. 80 miles round trip. Estimate of$10.00 to
                    $50.00 per ton. $65.00 per ton is more accurate. Would need transfer and transport.
               • Wasatch Integrated Waste: Davis County has an operating Materials Recovery Facility (MRF). Currently, it
                    processes mainstream household waste, separating the recyclables. As of August 8, 2020, the facility is now
                    accepting recyclable waste. They have set a price of $50.00 per ton to process out of district recyclables.
          Mr. Lowder expounded on the Wasatch Integrated Waste option, noting obstacles include the distance of29 miles per
truck, with two truck trips per day and a roughly 45-minute wait time per truck. Additional staff and trucks would be required as
the City currently has just 10 solid waste trucks available. Each truck currently travels about 70 miles per day including trips to
Recycled Earth; hauling to Wasatch Integrated Waste would increase mileage to 128 miles per truck per day. This would increase
vehicle costs by $24,931.80 per year, not including increased maintenance costs. He then noted that some have suggested other

reduction of service. At present, City Administration recommends the Council consider all operational implications and costs.
Administration proposes to resume recycling operations with Recycled Earth as this is the City's most viable option. However,
their operations must comply with all requirements of their CUP and a new service contract would be required. Once the CUP

Proposed Subdivision Ordinance Amendments
          Road Classifications and Streetlights: The subdivision ordinance, Title 14 of the City code, provides standards for the
subdivision of property, including the subdivision platting process, standards for required public improvement, and the process for
amendment or vacating subdivision plats. The amendments proposed would update the classifications and widths of different
street types to be consistent with the City's engineering standards as well as the recently adopted Transportation Master Plan. The
amendments also provide standards for new residential alleys and add streetlights as part of the required public improvements for
subdivisions.
          Planning Manager Montgomery used the aid of a PowerPoint presentation to discuss the proposed subdivision ordinance
amendments pertaining to road classifications and streetlights. The City's subdivision ordinance serves as the building block for
how development should occur in the City; it sets land patterns defined by circulation and utility locations. Those patterns
anticipate both local, community, and regional needs. The City's Transportation Master Plan was adopted last year and during the
implementation of the Plan, staff found there are four different standards that govern built components ofa subdivision: the City's
Subdivision Ordinance, Engineering Standards, the Fire Code, and the Zoning Code. Staff proposes using the same language in
each of these standards; the subdivision ordinance uses the terms minor street, collector street, and major street when referring to
street classifications. The Transportation Master Plan uses the tenns local street, minor and major collector street, and arterial
street. Staff recommends using the local, collector, and arterial tenns to help to clarify and simplify all standards. Street
classifications should be detennined by considering surface movement, parking, and underground utilities. There are two types of
local streets: 56 foot and 60 foot right of ways, with 26 and 32 feet of asphalt, respectively. Parking is allowed on one side ofa 56-
foot street and both sides of a 60-foot street. Collector streets will have a 72 foot right of way, fonnerly 66 to 80 feet. And arterial
streets will have a 100 foot right of way with 74 feet of pavement. Alleys can be used as a residential access option, must be 20
feet wide with a concrete surface and no utilities underneath. Cul-de-sacs have a maximum length of750 feet and the revised
pavement width will be 96 feet. Possible width reductions will be considered based on unique characteristics or factors. Variations
for special conditions will be considered on a case-by-case basis and would be based upon an increase or reduction in traffic
determined by a traffic study; the decision will be made by the City Engineer, Planning Commission, and Mayor. Relative to
streetlights, the proposed ordinance amendments include the addition of a provision of streetlights and standards as a public
improvement. The Planning Commission considered the proposed amendments and voted unanimously to recommend approval.
          Public Meetings and Small Subdivisions: The proposed changes remove the requirement for a public hearing when
reviewing a preliminary subdivision plat and instead require a public input opportunity at a public meeting. The amendments also
propose to update the process for subdivisions of 10 or fewer lots, classified as small subdivisions, to allow the Mayor to approve
the subdivision rather than the Planning Commission where no street dedication is proposed.
          Planning Manager Montgomery used the aid of a PowerPoint presentation to discuss the proposed amendments
pertaining to small subdivisions and the opportunity for public involvements in the planning process. He provided general
information about the City's subdivision process; the State Land Use laws have been amended to remove the requirement for a
public hearing for subdivision actions. Planning staff would like to incorporate this change into the City's subdivision ordinance,
though public comment would still be allowed. Subdivision actions are administrative in nature, though the requirement to hold a
public hearing gave the public the indication that it was actually a legislative action. It is important to identify unique conditions
with a property and that may not be possible without hearing from the public; however, if a subdivision meets City ordinance, it
cannot be denied. For small lot subdivisions, which is the creation of 10 lots or less, staff recommends that this be an
administrative process with the Mayor being the approval authority. This will save waiting time for applicants, save staff and
Planning Commission time, and reduce costs. He noted that the Planning C01mnission voted to recommend approval of this
amendment.
