Presentations

       Code Enforcement Presentation

Gwen Sayre explained that an Administrative Law Judge (“ALJ”) could help reduce pressure on
the City’s current criminal court by handling code enforcement and minor cases, potentially
lowering costs since ALJs can be contracted and scaled according to caseload. Examples from
Taylorsville showed that hearings grew from three cases per month at adoption to as many as
18 cases, with outcomes perceived as more educational and compliance-driven rather than
punitive. Positives included efficiency, scalability, and more tailored consequences for
violations, while concerns included additional costs, questions of necessity given the current
part-time court judge, and potential challenges with impartiality.

         250th Celebration Discussion

Sheril Garn, Events and Operations Director, presented about Riverton’s possible participation
in the Utah 250 Sesquicentennial Celebration, which would require forming a community
committee, passing a resolution of support, and submitting a commemoration idea. The State
has suggested service hours, walking challenges, potlucks, and a traveling art show, while City
staff proposed incorporating patriotic themes into existing events with banners, concerts, chalk
art, and Youth Council projects, as well as new ideas such as a commemorative coin, a barn
dance, a 1.776-mile run and 17.76-mile bike ride, a flag-retirement ceremony, a possible
production of 1776, a symbolic torch run, and patriotic school assemblies with coloring books.
Most costs could be absorbed into the current budget, though some items may require a budget
amendment, and the City would be eligible for a $1,500 state grant and a $3,000 Rocky
Mountain Power grant.

Mayor Pro Tempore Buroker asked the Council for input and feedback to decide whether to
move forward with funding or not.

Councilmember Haymond said he would be comfortable moving forward with the plans and
seek out grants and other funding to support additional activities.

Councilmember McCay proposed setting a funding cap, such as $20,000, with the option to
revisit the amount if needed. She also recommended moving the carnival to the week before or
after the Fourth of July to keep the holiday focused on celebrating the country’s 250th
anniversary, noting that some residents had expressed similar concerns about the carnival’s
audience and impact.

Councilmember Pierucci emphasized the importance of seeking business sponsorships before
spending taxpayer dollars, noting that local businesses would likely be eager to contribute to
celebrating the nation’s 250th anniversary. He volunteered to help with outreach efforts and
suggested offering businesses sponsorship opportunities for multiple events.

Councilmember McDougal reiterated support for the plan and encouraged seeking
sponsorships to reduce taxpayer expenses.

To summarize, Mayor Pro Tempore Buroker said that the Council appeared willing to support
spending for the celebration but wanted staff to first explore community and business
sponsorship opportunities before making expenditures.

time staff, 80 employees working overtime, and strong vendor and sponsorship support. The

         Riverton City AI Use Policy

Ryan Carter, City Attorney, presented a draft AI policy intended to guide employee use, noting
its importance for future operations, particularly in data analysis and law enforcement. The
policy emphasizes that employees remain responsible for their work products even when
assisted by AI, outlines compliance with statutes such as GRAMA, requires IT oversight for AI
acquisition, and mandates employee training, with violations subject to discipline. He explained
that while most policies evolve from practice, this one is being established proactively to avoid
misuse, with updates expected as AI use grows.

Staff explained that the multi-jurisdictional hazard mitigation plan is reviewed and updated

Public Hearing(s)/Action Items

         Ordinance No. 25-21 – RM-6 Amendment, Proposed Updates to the RM-6 Zone to
         include provisions for using private roads in specific circumstances and clarify
         Development Standards

Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, explained that the RM-6 zone originally
functioned as a multifamily zone but has evolved to apply to individual lots with detached units,
prompting updates to better reflect current use. Key changes include incorporating private lane
standards, such as required widths, parking limitations, and possible road or sidewalk
connections, into RM-6, similar to single-family zones, while clarifying that private road areas
cannot be counted within lot sizes to preserve buildable space and driveway depth. The
Planning Commission also recommended modifying the rear setback, changing the current
requirement of 25 feet when adjacent to single-family zoning to a consistent 15 feet, citing
challenges with smaller lot sizes and the lack of significant public concerns at the 15-foot
standard.

Councilmember McDougal asked whether adopting a consistent 15-foot rear setback in the RM-
6 zone would still allow the Council to regulate individual developments, particularly infill
projects adjacent to homes. Mr. Lethbridge confirmed that the Council could still address such
situations through the development approval process.

Councilmember McDougal then raised concerns about parking overflow in small lot
subdivisions, noting that narrow lots often force residents to use nearby public streets. Mr.
Lethbridge explained that zoning changes would require private lanes serving three or more lots
to allow parking on at least one side, and that the City retained authority to require wider roads
to accommodate additional parking. He also confirmed that the Council could require project-
specific parking solutions to reduce overflow onto public streets.

Mayor Staggs called for public comments; there being none, he closed the Public Hearing.

Councilmember Buroker expressed continued concern that future infill projects would likely
occur in already developed areas, where a 15-foot rear setback might not provide adequate

         New Skate Park Discussion

Cary Necaise, Public Works Director, reported that an RFP had been issued for a new skate
park at Centennial, requesting at least two design concepts in two potential locations: one
near a parking lot with no homes facing it, and another near a parking lot with some homes
nearby. The timeline for initial designs was estimated at 13–14 weeks, after which the
concepts would be reviewed through an open house or council session, and possibly with
local skaters. The current skate park was over 20 years old and in disrepair, with liability
concerns preventing piecemeal fixes. The City has applied for TRCC funding for the concept
drawings, with a September presentation scheduled, and hoped to seek additional TRCC
funding next year for construction, aiming for completion in 2026.

Councilmember Pierucci asked for clarification on whether Centennial Park had been formally
selected for the skate park, noting that other locations had been proposed. Mr. Necaise
explained that while no formal decision had been made, Centennial Park was identified as the
most suitable location after reviewing the City and nearby communities, including Bluffdale,
Herriman, South Jordan, and Draper, to ensure a central location relative to existing skate
parks. The site at Centennial was deemed optimal due to available land, minimal impact on
nearby homes, proximity to a fire station, flat terrain, and no drainage issues. He noted that
Centennial Park was currently the only site with a large enough footprint for the skate park.

Mayor Staggs added that while the new regional park could eventually provide space, the
timeline and exact footprint for that park had not yet been confirmed. He indicated that aside
