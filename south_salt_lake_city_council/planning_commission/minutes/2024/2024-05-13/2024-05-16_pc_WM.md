Development Director, Jonathan Weidenhamer, explained that there is an hour allotted to discuss
housing. The Meeting Materials Packet for the Work Meeting includes the presentation slides. The
intention is to start the discussions on housing. The City Council is looking forward to hearing
Planning Commission input. Mr. Weidenhamer explained that there can be additional discussions
as needed. He encouraged the Commissioners to ask questions and share ideas. The agenda for the
presentation was shared:

     •   Introductions;
     •   Housing Plan;

Housing Consultant, Lily Gray introduced herself. Her background is in affordable housing
development. She moved to Utah approximately seven years ago and now runs a practice where
she works with non-profit and municipal clients on project-specific work as well as funding
strategies and policies. She has been working with Mr. Weidenhamer and his team on the Housing
Plan update. Jason Glidden explained that he is a Housing Consultant and has spent the last 17
years working in government. Most recently, he worked with Park City Municipal to oversee their
housing program and help them re-write their Land Management Code to incentivize and develop
affordable housing. He is now the new Executive Director of Mountainlands Community Housing
Trust. Rebecca Yund stated that she is the Housing Navigator for Promise South Salt Lake and is
also a resident. She does work that helps people navigate the home-buying process. For instance,
connecting people with resources in the community and providing information about the process
of buying a home through educational resources.

Mr. Weidenhamer read the current Housing Vision Statement aloud, which was as follows:

   •    South Salt Lake is a community where residents are housed in safe, cared-for, and healthy
        neighborhoods that are connected to parks, local shopping, services, and regional
        transportation. Residents living in South Salt Lake have a choice of housing types that align
        with their household type, age, lifestyle, and budget to support our diverse families and
        residents. Housing opportunities in South Salt Lake are affordable to a variety of
        households.

The Housing Vision Statement is relevant, but the statement can evolve and is expected to evolve
as the conversations about housing continue to take place. Mr. Glidden reported that the
presentation materials are included in the Meeting Materials Packet for the Work Meeting and can
be referred back to, as necessary. He stressed the importance of establishing a common vocabulary
and understanding of affordable housing. As a result, the presentation materials included several
key terms:

   •    Affordable Housing:
           o Housing costs less than 30% of a household’s income.
   •    Area Median Income or AMI:
           o Mid-point for the household income for a specific area set by HUD each year.
   •    Cost-Burdened:
           o Household pays more than 30% of their income towards housing costs.
   •    Severely Cost-Burdened:
           o Household pays more than 50% of their income towards housing costs.
   •    Deed Restrictions:
           o Recorded restrictions impose purchase or rental eligibility requirements, limit the
               price at which property can be sold, or limit the rental rate an owner may charge.
               Deed restrictions help to keep properties affordable over time.

Mr. Glidden shared additional information about deed restrictions. He explained that these are
recorded against the property and that is what preserves the affordability of a unit. Generally
speaking, the lowest he has seen is 15 years and the highest he has seen is 50 to 60 years with the
opportunity to extend by 10 years. Commissioner George Pechmann asked about the definition
that referenced housing costs. He wondered what housing costs include. Mr. Glidden explained
that this goes beyond a mortgage because there are other costs associated with a home. For
example, Homeowners Association ("HOA") fees and utilities. Ms. Gray added that for renters, it
is rent plus utilities, and for owners, it includes mortgage payment, property tax, insurance,
utilities, and HOA fees. Commissioner Pechmann asked if utilities include internet. Mr. Gray
reported that the internet is not normally included, as it covers common utilities needed for
survival, but the internet is something that could potentially be included in the future.

Commissioner Bellina asked if transportation is included in the 30%. Ms. Gray clarified that it is
not included. Transportation is another cost that is often a meaningful part of the household budget.
The 30% referenced relates to the housing piece as opposed to the transportation component.
Mr. Glidden reported that transportation is usually the second-largest expense for a household.
Commissioner Suzanne Slifka noted that the housing in some of the new developments requires
payment for parking. In those situations, she wanted to know if it would be included in the housing
costs or if that is considered separate. Commissioner Pechmann believed that would still be part
of the transportation portion. Mr. Weidenhamer stated that it would likely depend on how the
developer financed the project. Ms. Gray explained that with affordable housing, there cannot be
an extra parking charge. Typically speaking, the housing and the parking components are
considered separately, but both are meaningful costs.

Commissioner Slifka asked if deed restrictions include limitations to the rent increases that can
occur per year. Mr. Glidden confirmed that this is normally included. He shared the Income Levels
and Affordability slide, which included a chart with different Average Median Income (“AMI”)
percentages. The chart is for Salt Lake County and the data is based on a family of four. 80% AMI
and below is considered affordable, but the target that South Salt Lake sets might be different.
Based on community needs, a lower percentage might be the target. That is where the deed
restrictions would come in. For example, setting rent at 50% AMI. There would then be an
inflationary component built in so there was clarity about future increases. In deed restrictions and
Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (“CC&Rs”), as developments are being approved, it is
possible to limit the percentage of fees that can be charged to an affordable unit. The intention is
to create certain protections for renters and owners to ensure that the units stay affordable.

Mr. Weidenhamer noted that cities often have a variety of AMI percentages. The entire program
does not have to be set at 80% AMI. There will likely be a diversity available. Mr. Glidden
explained that a Housing Needs Assessment was done for South Salt Lake and that data was
considered. He agreed that there will likely be a spectrum of housing types. The affordability levels
depend on the needs of the community. The reason the Housing Needs Assessment was conducted
was to identify those needs.

Mr. Glidden reviewed the benefits of affordable housing, which included benefits to health,
economy, education, environment, and community. There is a lot of research that confirms there
are health benefits associated with secure housing, both physically and mentally. The benefits to

has been conducted to determine how well children score when they have better housing. He
pointed out that when there is no affordable housing in a community, there are fewer opportunities
provided to younger families. When younger families are unable to live in a community,
enrollment levels in schools decrease. That can result in school closures. When it comes to the
benefits to the environment, when people do not have to drive as far to their jobs, that can be

The Housing Needs Assessment was discussed. Mr. Glidden reported that the Kem Gardner Policy
Institute was contracted to complete a Needs Assessment Report for South Salt Lake. That has
been provided to the Commissioners. As South Salt Lake considers potential policy changes and
potential code changes, it is important to consider the needs that exist within the community.
Mr. Glidden pointed out some of the South Salt Lake demographic information. South Salt Lake
has the lowest median income in Salt Lake County at $50,589. Looking at the AMI chart, it is half
of what the AMI is for Salt Lake County. He reported that almost 93% of South Salt Lake residents
work outside of the City while over 97% of employees working in the City live outside. This means
most residents are traveling outside the City for work and most employees are traveling into the
City for work. The demographic information also indicates that home purchases are out of reach
for most residents based on their income levels.

A chart was shared to illustrate the current housing inventory in South Salt Lake. Mr. Glidden
noted that apartments make up the majority of the inventory whereas single-family homes are at
29%. He pointed out that 30% of the single-family homes are renter-occupied rather than owner-
occupied. There are a lot of renters in South Salt Lake. Out of the apartments in the City, only 8%
of those are deed-restricted affordable units. Other highlights of the Needs Assessment were
shared. Mr. Glidden reported that there is not a lot of turnover for the owner-occupied homes. 40%
of the homeowners have a housing cost burden of at least 30% and 10% of homeowners are paying
at least 50% of their income towards housing. South Salt Lake has the lowest median sales price
and is one of the most affordable cities in the County when it comes to housing prices, but South
Salt Lake also has the second oldest housing inventory in the County. He noted that there are also
large increases in sale prices, with a 10% annual increase from 2010-2023.

Mr. Glidden reported that small groups were hosted to gather resident feedback. The first group
included established residents. Some long-time owners liked the community feel, diversity, lot
sizes, and access. Those residents are currently comfortable paying 10-30% of their income toward
housing, but feel stuck, as they cannot afford to upgrade or downgrade. Their perception is that
what is being built is not affordable. There were also discussions with renters. Those residents love
the community and the services the community provides, but because the City has continued to
become more expensive, they may be forced to move. A lot of them are paying 50-80% of their
income on housing. That is a significant issue. Many of these residents are underhoused and are
seeking more space for their families.

Mr. Glidden shared additional data from the Needs Assessment. He reported that 60% of all South
Salt Lake housing units are rentals, which is the highest in the County. It was noted that there is a
high percentage of rental units that are naturally affordable, while a low percentage are deed-
restricted affordable. Mr. Glidden explained that some units are naturally affordable because those
units are older. There is an opportunity to preserve and maintain those. If the market starts to
change and interest rates decrease, the private sector will likely come in and take over. Ms. Gray
explained that there is a perception that the naturally occurring affordable housing properties are
safe, but that is not always the case. When the market turns, those are lost quickly, so it is important
to think strategically about them.

Commissioner Bellina asked for strategies to preserve the naturally occurring affordable housing
properties. At the end of the day, it is up to the owner whether or not they sell the property.
Ms. Gray clarified that some of those tools will be discussed later on in the presentation. It is not
easy, but there are some options available. Mr. Glidden next reported that residents were concerned
about parking. There is support for density around transit and there is support for density in
neighborhoods if those homes are scaled and regulated. There is a way to increase density and still
make it feel like it is part of the neighborhood. Additionally, residents want to be close to
transportation, parks, schools, trails, and retail. Some of the residents expressed a desire for more
walkability and wanted the missing middle to be addressed.

The community loves the services that are provided in South Salt Lake. However, school
enrollment is decreasing, which is a trend State-wide. Mr. Glidden pointed out that Utah was
recently ranked as the third most expensive State in the County, behind Hawaii and California.
Ms. Gray explained that in addition to the Needs Assessment and the resident feedback received,
there were several interviews conducted with developers. During those interviews, there were a lot
of similar themes heard. The development community acknowledged that South Salt Lake is a
desirable place for people to live due to the transit and freeway access as well as the proximity to
Salt Lake City and the local character. The weaknesses relate to infrastructure needs, placemaking,
walkability, streetscape vitality, the lack of retail options, and the limitations for zoning to address
the mid-density residential/missing middle.

currently allows single-family homes and multi-family, which is a minimum of 50 units. Duplexes
are non-conforming in the code. Ms. Gray explained that the tools presented are within the control

2.       Parks Master Plan Update.

Ms. Ungricht explained that the next item on the Work Meeting agenda is the Parks Master Plan
update. She reported that Parks Project Manager, Anthony Biamont, has been the main person
handling the work. Mr. Biamont shared the presentation slides with those present and provided
some context for the Parks Master Plan update. The last Parks Master Plan was written and adopted
in 2015. There has been a General Plan update since then and the community has also changed a
lot. There are also some things happening outside the City as well, such as the Salt Lake County
Parks and Recreation Master Plan and the Jordan River Park Plan. A lot of things are happening
both inside and outside the City.

Mr. Biamont reported that a Parks Master Plan includes the following information:

     •   Assessment of Parks, Open Space, and Trails:

as well. In addition, there have been workshops in schools with elementary, junior high, and high
school students. There have also been some open houses that were available to the general public
as well as an online survey. Since January, work has been done with an Advisory Committee that
includes residents and City employees.

Over the last year, information gathering has taken place. Moving forward, that information will
be organized and presented clearly. Once the plan is adopted, the focus will shift to the Parks
Impact Fee Study, Capital Improvements Plan, Property Acquisition and Agreements, and Park

Key Terms
Affordable Housing: Housing costs less
than 30% of a household’s income.

Area Median Income (AMI): Mid-point for
household income for a specific area set by HUD
each year.

Cost-Burdened: Household pays more than
30% of their income towards housing costs.
Severely Cost-Burdened: Household pays
more than 50% of their income towards housing
costs.

Deed Restrictions: Recorded restrictions
impose purchase or rental eligibility requirements,
limit the price at which a property can be sold, or
limit the rental rate an owner may charge. Deed
restrictions help keep properties affordable over
time.

SSL Housing Trends:
Resident Feedback
● Established residents (long-time owners):
   ○ Like community-feel, diversity, lot sizes and
      access.
   ○ Currently comfortable, paying 10-30% of
      income on housing, but feel stuck and
      cannot afford to upgrade/downgrade.
   ○ Perception that what is being built is not
      affordable.
● At-risk residents (primarily renters):
   ○ Would like to stay in the community - like
      the welcoming feel, access, schools.
   ○ Pay 30-80% of income on housing and
      getting priced out.
   ○ Most fear they will lose their home and/or
      having to move.
   ○ Lack of information to navigate the system
      and need access to first-time homeowner
      programs.
   ○ Many are underhoused and seeking more
      space for their families.
SSL Rental Trends:
Needs Assess. Report
● ~60% of all SSL housing units are rentals,
  highest in the County.
● 10% of all SSL renters face a severe housing
  cost burden, paying more than 50% of their
  income on housing.
● 30% of occupied single-family homes are
  renter occupied.

SSL Rental Trends:
Resident Feedback
● Established residents (long-time owners):
   ○ Concerned about parking with new, large
      apartment developments.
   ○ Concerned about short-term rentals eating
      up housing availability.
   ○ Support for density around transit,
      downtown, and along State St.
   ○ Support density in neighborhoods if scaled

   ○ Want to be close to transportation, parks,

   ○ Would like to see more “missing middle”

   ○ Need help with rental assistance programs
      to simply stay in their current housing and
      navigate a pathway to housing security.

Council Feedback
Key themes have emerged that will inform a future
action plan:
1.   Concerns about affordability and displacement
Historically, South Salt Lake has been a community with affordable options
but there is fear that this is being lost with the escalations in the for sale and
rental markets.

2.   Interest in more diversity of housing options
The community would like to see more housing options, particularly for
owner occupancy, including housing types with less maintenance than a
traditional single-family home.

3.   Need for larger units
Many feel that there are not enough larger units (2 and 3 bedrooms) in the
community and worry that the new supply is mostly studios and 1
bedrooms.

4.   Desire to see improvements to overall housing quality
Several noted that there are many residential properties, both single and
multifamily, that are eyesores and in need of upgrades.

5.   Support for appropriate density
Many noted concerns about preserving the fabric of single-family
neighborhoods and making sure that new development will be at an
appropriate scale when in or near these areas.
