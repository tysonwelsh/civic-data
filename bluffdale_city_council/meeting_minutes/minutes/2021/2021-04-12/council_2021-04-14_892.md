2.     Bluffdale Economic Development Data, Beni Becker, Zions Public Finance.

Benj Becker from Zions Public Finance explained that Zions Public Finance is a subcontractor to
the entity that is doing Bluffdale's General Plan. They help determine if land use decisions are
grounded in economic realities. Mr. Becker reviewed the previous presentation given that focused
on what is moving the market. He presented slides showing U.S. economic statistics. Mr. Becker
was asked if the economic slope shown will continue to increase. Mr. Becker explained that it is
predicted that it will increase more significantly that it has over the past five years.

Mr. Becker explained that since year 2000, the need for retail space has declined due to online
retail. CmTently, 15 square feet per capita is needed for retail spaces. This indicates that there will
be about 140,000 square feet of space that is obsolete as of 2020.

Mr. Becker was asked about sales tax collection and online retail and if the various State and
district taxes will impact online sales negatively for businesses trying to compete online.
Mr. Becker explained that there may be some hindrances in the form of delivery costs and
exchanges, but that competition online is important.

Mr. Becker presented a slide showing the changes in taxable sales from 2019 to 2020. During the
pandemic, many areas lost revenues due to a lack of people coming in from outside to shop and an
increase in online shopping. Communities where people stayed and made purchases within their
communities did well.

A Slide exhibiting the monthly taxable sales from 2019 to 2020 for Bluffdale specifically was
shown. Mr. Becker asked the Council if the number of per capita annual sales in relation to other
cities was surprising to them. It was reported that Bluffdale has enough businesses and home
development to be competitive.

Slides were displayed showing that car and truck sales have decreased since 2018 while boat and
RV sales have increased. Mr. Becker showed an example of retail sales and development in
Cottonwood Heights. He explained that in Salt Lake County in 2020, online sales taxes were
nearly $4,000 per person. He explained that if you multiply that amount by a four-person
household, it would total $16,000. One-half of 1% is what Bluffdale would have received. He
explained that if you consider it for an entire neighborhood, it can offset the loss of brick and
mortar retail sales taxes. Currently, it is recognized that there can be an additional revenue source
from online sales in residential areas and it changes the way development is done.

Mr. Becker explained that secondary locations will be repurposed. Class A locations have healthy
median incomes, strong daytime populations, healthy traffic counts, and a growing residential
populace. He explained that there is a shift in retail from the east side to the west side as they
follow the money and population per acre. Flexible designs for retail are important. This could
include permit pick-up, drive-thrus, multiple use types, and mixed-use. It is also important to be

in proximity to residential and employment support. Many businesses are changing the way their
spaces are designated with more outdoor space, pick-up areas, and convenience in focus.

Mr. Becker exhibited a slide illustrating the crash in hotel occupancy from 2019. There is currently
only about 20% occupancy in Salt Lake City. New hotel development occurs at about 75%. Full
resort and spa hotels have taken the biggest losses while business and economy hotels have
rebounded better.

A slide was shown exhibiting the City's property taxes from different property types/uses and what
is being generated on a per-acre basis. Mr. Becker explained how sales taxes and population
distribution affect this. He cautioned that while this is good infonnation to have on hand when
making development decisions, there are also many secondary factors to consider.

An example of the requirements of a new grocery store was displayed. They include population,
Area Median Income ("AMI"), and population growth. If an area meets these minimum
requirements, the grocer moves to another set of requirements including competitor location and
services, disposable income, location of population growth, and ease of access. A slide was shown
of the estimated population by radius in the greater Bluffdale, Riverton, Draper, South Jordan, and
Herriman region. The population from one to three miles and three to five was shown in green on
a map displayed. In this category, the City would not qualify for this specific grocery store. They
would have to see something significantly better to consider it. The AMI in the area is higher and
may be enough to warrant a second look at the site. Surrounding undeveloped areas are considered
a desert since population cannot be pulled from these areas unless there are significant plans
coming forward. Some retailers, however, may recognize that it may not work for a grocery site
but the potential traffic may benefit a restaurant, convenience store, or fitness studio. Some grocery
stores may be willing to take a risk if they are a niche market.

City Planner/Economic Development Director, Grant Crowell reported that staff spoke to a group
the previous week that is looking at store construction two to three years out. If something were
spelled out in the General Plan showing how an area is planned to be developed, it would allow a
developer to better plan and detennine if there is a fit. Mr. Crowell stated that site selectors are
astute and have access to a large amount of data. His opinion was that they are looking for
entitlements and not just general planning.

Mr. Becker explained that the Independence area is attractive to developers because there are new
homes, good incomes, and families . From a retail perspective, there is a barrier since there is a
Harmons and a Walmart close by in Draper. Removing barriers that could potentially impact
where the key demographics are located is something retailers look for. With the higher density,
residents typically make decisions based on convenience and choice. With respect to grocery
choice, decisions are primarily based on convenience.

Mr. Becker explained that not all grocery stores have the same criteria. He stressed the importance
of removing all possible barriers and exposure characteristics. If economic tools are used, the City

With regard to a question raised about 2700 West, Mr. Crowell stated that if the City decides that

accommodate the uses that are very popular. He suggested that City consider the alternatives and
the impact of waiting a few years as well as the potential secondary benefits.

3.     Update on City-Owned Redwood Properties Request for Proposals ("RFP").

The City previously sent out the Redwood properties Request for Proposals ("RFP") to a few dozen
potential contacts. They received three detailed responses. Staff was working to evaluate them
and get information to the Council.

It was noted that the plan is to vet them with staff and bring recommendations back to City Council.
It can, however, also be done in a different way. The Council was asked if they wanted to see the
raw information immediately. Mayor Timothy stated that it would be nice to have the raw data
and asked that it be sent to the Council. It was determined that more conversation would be needed
later.

4.     Update on Design Standards Ordinance.

Mr. Crowell reported that staff was working with the Planning Commission on the Design
Standards update that began last October. The hope was that at the April 21 meeting, the Planning
Commission can make additional progress and potentially provide a recommendation to the City
Council.
