4.       A presentation from the Kem C. Gardner Institute: "Affordable Housing and
         Housing Affordability" (21-094) (1:07:25)

Jim Wood, Ivory-Boyer Senior Fellow at the Kem C. Gardner Policy Institute, presented. Mr.
Wood shared some comments on the Council’s preceding discussion of ADUs. He shared insight
into the census process for counting these units and other relevant statistics. He then moved into
his presentation. Utah had the highest rate of increase in its population from 2010-2020 at 18.4%.
Utah also leads the country in employment rate of increase (29.6% with Idaho close behind at
25%). In numeric terms, Utah ranks 10th with 350,000 new jobs. Utah ranks 31st in size of the
labor market. This data indicates that Utah’s growth is disproportionate to the size of the State.
One cost of this type of growth is the impacts to infrastructure and housing.

Many factors impact housing prices; municipal factors include regulatory delays, development
costs, and zoning ordinances, while market factors include economic and demographic growth,
land costs, material costs, labor costs and shortages, COVID-19 impacts, housing shortage, and
low interest rates. Although there are record levels of owner-occupied, renter, and condominium
development, there is still a housing shortage. Different entry points to the housing market
continue to show stress, including the rental market, new home market, and existing home market.

Utah has a long history of housing volatility, with price spikes seen in the 1970s, 1990s, and
2000s. In 2020 there was a 28.3% increase from the previous year—that was a major outlier even
over 1994 and 2006 when Utah led the country. Mr. Wood commented on the national economic

5.      A presentation on the housing development process with varied and mixed housing
        types. (21-080) (1:40:34)

John Dester, Georgetown Development, and Dave Gardner, Gardner & Associates, presented.
Mr. Dester shared some details on past projects and said that the issues Mr. Wood addressed in
the previous presentation were very relevant. One of the main issues for development in Provo is
scarce land. Provo faces similar constraints as the Bay Area with its neighboring body of water
and mountains constraining available land. NIMBYism (“Not In My Backyard”) is another issue.
Provo needs all types of housing and any efforts toward affordability will help.

Mr. Dester walked the Council through several case studies illustrating the development process
with real projects that have taken place. For many projects, there are multiple layouts that will
result in desirable end results, but that may simply reflect a different style or type of housing. Mr.
Dester shared several projects where townhomes or smaller, more modest homes were built,
which have attracted long-term families or retirees and have diversified the housing stock in the
broader areas. In many cases, the profit level between the two different scenarios is fairly
comparable for a developer—larger homes often have higher land development costs which
drives up the cost of the lot. With high construction costs, this escalates housing prices further.

Mr. Dester showed a common product that often results in all concrete front yards with rows of
garage doors, no backyards, no fences, and no private patios. This isn’t a housing type—this
simply reflects a poor design choice. He showed that using the same formula, though, of
alternating the garage, entry door, garage, entry door, can be designed with a more appealing and

beautiful design and layout. The housing type does not determine the look and feel—attached
single-family homes can be built to resemble detached residences, thereby reducing costs while
maintaining beautiful aesthetics. The same basic formula can be tweaked to introduce timeless
architecture with varied exteriors, beautiful landscaping, private backyards with fences, etc. for
more affordable prices than a typical single-family detached home.

Mr. Dester reviewed another proposal case study on a larger parcel of land, comparing two site
plans—one with 11 lots and one with 19 pads or pocket lots and a central community park. He
shared some slides and examples from travels around the country with similar examples he has
seen in other places. Some communities have planted orchards and garden plots with beautiful
results. This particular parcel in the example is located next to a city park and offered area for
expansion of the city park with a pavilion and parking. Mr. Dester reviewed the pro forma
calculations for developing the lots in each of these proposals, the market price for the lot, and
profit in each scenario. The profit was slightly higher for the 11 lots but Mr. Dester said in many
similar situations the profit would be closer between the two. In response to a question from the
Council about why a developer would pursue a project with more units if the profit was lower,
Mr. Dester and Mr. Gardner explained that with any development, there is still risk involved.
There is considerably less risk with a more affordable and modest-sized lot or residence, so many
developers would choose to make a lower profit knowing that their investment is more secure.
Mr. Gardner pointed out that the number of buyers who can afford the higher lot price is
typically a lot less; it is easier to sell smaller lots more quickly, and the quicker a developer sells
the lots, the more quickly they reduce the investment risk.

Mr. Gardner outlined another possible project (a real parcel, but the hypothetical project). The
property could accommodate 30 lots or 44 pads. With 44 pads, the units would still be single-
family detached housing and a low-density project at 4.8 units per acre. Mr. Gardner noted that
the 30-lot layout would inherently consume a lot of water; 44 pads was more economical for
water use and in many other respects and lowered the home prices to open up access to another
segment of the community. The 44-pad layout would allow for better integrated open space
throughout the property, with room for trails, community gardens, and more.

Councilor David Shipley asked if lot improvement costs have risen substantially, whether that
would change the market for selling improved lots. Mr. Gardner said typically a developer will
sell a lot or property for what the market is willing to bear; they do not operate on fixed profit
margins since the market fluctuates. He noted that one possibility was to build a slightly smaller
house on the property in order to keep the total cost in the range of what a buyer could afford.

Councilor George Handley commented on some beautiful developments he saw in Maryland.
Bill Peperone, Development Services Director, was familiar with the project Mr. Handley
referenced in Gaithersburg, Maryland and he noted it was a very new urbanist project. Mr.
Handley would love to see more projects like Timpview Drive and he wondered how the Council
could incentivize and encourage that kind of creativity. Mr. Gardner said that starting off with a
lower density and allowing developers to work with staff to develop a better proposal with a
higher density usually worked well. It was difficult to capture everything in one ordinance; it was
more important to set out the goals, such as quality development and good design.

6.      A discussion on towing fees (21-105) (2:31:34)

Brian Jones, City Attorney, presented. He referenced the memo with background information on
the item. The request originated with a towing company in Provo and Mr. Jones outlined the
possible decision points for the Council. Councilor David Shipley asked whether the city was at
risk of the state disallowing the rule. Mr. Jones indicated there may be some risk, but these caps
only apply to patrol towing in apartment complexes and were an effort to curb predatory towing.

Cliff Strachan, Council Executive Director, asked if Provo’s rates stay low compared to state

would discourage patrol tows because it limits the fees operators they can charge for doing it.

many complaints about tows happening in a patrol setting where the general feeling was that the
property owner had not asked for a tow. Mr. Jones indicated that Provo’s rates were previously at
80% of the state level; with the recent change to the state’s rate, Provo’s rate sits at 72% of the
allowed total. Council Vice-Chair David Shipley invited feedback from the Council. This item
could be requested on a future agenda by two or more Councilors. Presentation only.

9.      A presentation on facility and construction updates specifically regarding traffic
        challenges in the Timpview neighborhood from Provo City School District. (21-104)
        (3:11:23)

Superintendent Keith Rittel and Timpview Principal Fidel Montero presented. Spt. Rittel
explained the request for the City to approve a temporary parking restriction for Quail Valley
Drive. With large trucks going in and out of the main construction entrance throughout the
school day, there is potential for accidents. The parking restriction would only need to be in
place from 7 AM to 4:30 PM and the School District was willing to pay for the cost of the signs.

As students are utilizing Quail Valley Drive to park, many students are ignoring proper parking
protocols and not paying attention to traffic. With the bend in this street, there is a bit of a blind
spot. It is also a steep road and cars inherently drive quickly. Kids and students cross and the
School District wants to avoid any incidents. Traffic management has been okay to this point; the
District has tried to communicate extensively with neighbors to provide them with information
for the school if issues arise. It’s busy there, but this particular adjustment would really help with
safety and increased traffic to this area in the coming months as construction proceeds.

Councilors shared comments and asked questions. Councilor George Handley asked about
incentives to discourage students from driving. Principal Montero indicated they have seen a
large increase in bike access and expanded bike racks around the school. They have also
partnered with the neighboring church building to utilize 190 parking spaces. The school also has
concerns about bicycles and foot traffic as winter arrives with shorter days. To this point, they
have seen a large increase in active transit, parents dropping off students, and bus ridership.

Councilor David Harding pointed out that restricting parking might push students to park in cul-
de-sacs and other neighboring streets. Principal Montero explained that staff members would
monitor parking and direct students. They have not yet shared this plan with neighbors in the
area, but they could. Mr. Harding thanked Spt. Rittel and Principal Montero for the presentation;
he pointed out that the City Administration was the more appropriate body to make this call.
Principal Montero said they met with Provo traffic engineers to consider other parking
alternatives and the city staff walked them through pros and cons. They had felt that this was a
significant enough request that they wanted to bring it to the Council. Councilor Shannon
Ellsworth suggested having a 12-hour restriction to simplify. Spt. Rittel said they had identified
the 7 AM to 4:30 PM block of time in order to be conscious of the needs of neighbors in the area.

Councilor Bill Fillmore thanked Spt. Rittel and Principal Montero for being proactive about
these safety concerns. Mr. Fillmore was concerned about the cul-de-sacs and student parking on
event nights. Principal Montero shared additional insight on game night parking, noting that for
