             BUILDING FOOTPRINT COVERAGE ON SINGLE-FAMILY RESIDENTIAL
             LOTS. APPLICANT - RIVERTON CITY

Mr. Lethbridge reported that Code Amendments come before the Planning Commission in
several different ways. Sometimes, the amendments are initiated by an applicant who is
seeking a code change. Other times, Staff brings them forward as an issue that has been
recognized. In this instance, the City Council has been discussing an amendment. It
pertained to the Lot Coverage Standard, which is the maximum percentage of each lot that
can be covered by buildings or structures. The current ordinance in the single-family zones
allow for 30% lot area coverage. That includes the home footprint and accessory structures.
City Code also includes swimming pools in the definition of “structure.” As a result, the area
of a swimming pool currently counts toward that maximum coverage area.

Mr. Lethbridge reported that the current definition of structure is anything constructed or
erected above or below ground. That had been consistently interpreted to include swimming
pools. When the 10% calculation was done for accessory structures, the swimming pool
calculation was considered. That counted toward the allowed 10%. He shared an example
image with the Commission. Measurements are down to the foundation. The home is
measured from the property line to the foundation of the home. For any kind of deck or other
accessory structures that are supported by posts, the measurement was done to the support
structure.

The City Council started to discuss the Lot Coverage Standard in part because of some of
the waterwise landscaping and water use reduction that had been mandated by the State
and had been encouraged by the City. There was concern that by limiting the footprint on
the property, additional lawn space was being forced in some circumstances. At the request
of the Council, City Staff looked at the standards in other cities. In looking at surrounding
and similarly-sized cities, Riverton City's maximum coverage area requirement was one of
the most restrictive. Based on the comparisons, 40% maximum coverage is a median
number. In addition, Riverton was the only place that counts swimming pools in the
calculation for accessory structures. As a result, the City Council directed City Staff to
prepare a code amendment that proposed an increase to 40% coverage and removed
swimming pools from the calculation. The amendments were in the Meeting Materials
Packet, which included changes to the Building Coverage section and an amendment to the
definition of “structure,” which was in 18.05.030 and would remove swimming pools.

Mr. Lethbridge noted that there was more expansion happening in the City, with the
allowance of Accessory Dwelling Units (“ADU”) and infill development. The 30% to 40%
increase in the square footage would reduce the landscaped area and allow for additional
home expansion. This would be proportional to the lot size, which meant that the additional
10% would have less of an impact on a one-quarter acre lot and more of an impact on the
higher acreage sizes. The matter was coming to the Planning Commission for consideration
and a recommendation to the City Council. A sample motion was shared.

Chair Gilchrist wondered if Staff anticipated that this amendment would lead to
developments with larger homes on smaller lots. Mr. Lethbridge referenced some of the
special districts that had smaller lots. The City was already seeing larger homes on smaller
lots, so the impact of this would likely relate more to additions. He explained that this
amendment would not apply to the special districts. It would mostly impact the infill projects.

       B.     Planning Commission Training by Ryan Carter.

Mr. Carter provided follow-up training from the training the Commissioners received several
months earlier from Craig Hall, where information about public clamor was shared.
Mr. Carter explained that he would share an example of when public clamor and the resulting
decision-making collide with Federal Law. In this instance, the Federal Law being discussed
was the Federal Fair Housing Act. The Federal Fair Housing Act was written in a way to
protect everyone. It prevents people from using housing decisions to discriminate against
people based on gender, race, age, and other factors.

The Federal Fair Housing Act also states that decisions cannot be made that discriminate
against a handicapped person. A handicapped person is anyone who suffers from a
condition that is severe enough that it interferes with their ability to carry out ordinary life
activities. That also pertains to addiction. Under Federal Law, if someone is addicted to
something, it is considered a handicap unless that person is actively using. While in
recovery, that person would be considered handicapped for life. However, while actively
using, a person is not considered a handicapped person under Federal Law.

One purpose of the Federal Fair Housing Act was to allow people to have housing when
they are sober but considered handicapped. Group living arrangements were recognized
as a tool that reinforces sobriety amongst alcoholics and drug addicts by allowing them to
live in ways that might not otherwise be allowed under zoning codes. For example, the City
does not allow more than four people who are unrelated by blood or marriage to live in a
single-family residential home. However, under the Federal Fair Housing Act, if that
restriction interferes with the ability of a group of people who were handicapped to obtain
housing, the group could seek Reasonable Accommodation under Federal Law, where the
city would make an exception to the code in recognition of the handicapped status.

Mr. Carter shared information about Boise County. He noted that there is a predominantly
forested area there where people have cabins in the woods. There is not a lot of industry
and property tax was one of the only sources of revenues in terms of a tax base. In the past,
a group of investors decided that they wanted to put together a facility for the disabled that
would also be a boarding school. The focus was going to be on treating handicapped
children. The children would be mostly under the age of 18 and it would be a 72-bed facility.
It had to come to the County government for approval under the Conditional Use Permit
process. The question during the Conditional Use Permit process was not whether the
proposed use should be allowed because it was an allowed use, but whether Conditions of
Approval were warranted to make sure the use did not cause damages to other properties.

In this particular case, the group came in with a Planning Commission application, and the
Planning Commission at the time was confronted by local area residents who objected to
the application on discriminatory grounds. The residents created a website opposing the
application and that website included a lot of discriminatory content. The residents also
placed illegal signage along State Highway 21, which was the highway adjacent to the
planned location. The decision made by the County wound up being ascribed to the
discriminatory actions of the residents and there was a judgment made against them.

Ultimately, the Planning Commission denied the Conditional Use Permit because they felt
the development of the residential treatment center was not appropriate in the proposed
location at the time. The Planning Commission also stated that the County lacked sufficient
infrastructure and money to monitor and enforce the conditions that were proposed for the
approval of the application. However, that would have been true for all of the cabins around
there as well. The Commission created a false crisis and used that as a basis for denial.

The group then appealed the Planning Commission's decision. Mr. Carter noted that the
Planning Commission denied the application without discussing what the Conditions of
Approval would be if the application was approved. When the appeal happened, it went to

the County Council, which served as the appeal body. At that time, the applicant requested

pad and that two bridges be installed. At this point, the Conditional Use Permit had

applicant filed a lawsuit in Federal District Court, which went to trial. It was found that there
had been discrimination in their intent. What the residents did was also noted because their
comments were all part of the public record during the Planning Commission Meetings.

The decision-making was paired with the actions of the community residents and it was very
clearly discriminatory in nature. The elected officials did not have a decent articulated basis
for the denial. There was clear discriminatory intent, which was what the Federal Fair
Housing Act did not allow. Mr. Carter reported that the jury ultimately awarded $4 million in
general damages plus punitive damages to the plaintiffs. The general damage award did
not include attorney fees, which Boise County also had to pay to the applicants.

Since the final judgment was higher than the annual operating budget for Boise County, the
County tried to petition the court for bankruptcy in order to escape the judgment against
them. That failed and a tax levy had to be issued. The County bonded for the amount and
paid it back over a number of years. Mr. Carter explained that there were several takeaways
to consider in the Boise County scenario. In terms of Conditional Use Permits, it is important
to sift through and separate valid information from invalid information shared during a public
hearing. It is also important to have a list of factual findings when a Conditional Use Permit
was approved or denied. Mr. Carter would likely recommend during those considerations
that Staff be directed to come back at a later time with a written opinion.
