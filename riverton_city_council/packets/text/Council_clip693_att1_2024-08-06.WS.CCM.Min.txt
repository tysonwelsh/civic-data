38          General Plan Process Discussion
39
40   Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, noted that while some elements of the
41   general plan are determined at the city level, state code increasingly dictates the approval
42   process and specific elements that must be included. Recent amendments to state code have
43   influenced the general plan, particularly how it integrates with other plans, such as the
44   transportation master plan and moderate-income housing plans.
45
46   Mr. Lethbridge explained that the focus of the Council's recent general plan amendments has
47   primarily been on the land use map, which outlines the desired land uses for different areas of
48   the City. The land use map has been the most utilized and important element of the general
49   plan. However, the text portion of the general plan, which includes definitions and criteria, is
50   outdated and overdue for revision.

 2   road classifications, transit considerations, and other transportation-related goals. The
 3   transportation master plan will need to align closely with the general plan, land use map,
 4   moderate-income housing, and water plans, as required by state code.

29   Sheril Garn, Events and Operations Director, explained that City staff, along with the police and
30   fire departments, held a debrief meeting to review the recent Town Days event and identify
31   areas for improvement. The event, which was organized by five full-time employees, required

35   Key highlights from the event included a record participation of 55 teams in the three-man

 3            Cargo Container Discussion
 4
 5   Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, discussed cargo containers and their
 6   compatibility with current ordinances. The existing ordinance prohibits cargo containers but
 7   allows them under specific conditions if they meet exterior material requirements similar to those
 8   for accessory structures.
 9
10   A key point of discussion was the treatment of agricultural buildings. The ordinance provides a
11   lighter standard for agricultural accessory structures, allowing materials that are appropriate and
12   complementary to the main dwelling, though these structures must be used exclusively for
13   agricultural purposes such as housing animals or storing feed. The flexibility in materials does
14   not extend to general cargo containers, which must still adhere to the ordinance’s requirements
15   for exterior materials.
16
17   The discussion also covered practices from other cities. For instance:
18
19           Herriman City allows cargo containers for living or accessory space but not as accessory
20            dwelling units (ADUs). They require containers to be painted and finished to match the
21            primary dwelling.
22           St. George and South Salt Lake generally do not allow cargo containers except for brief
23            storage periods.
24           Ogden’s standard is similar to Riverton’s, where cargo containers are not allowed unless
25            enclosed within an accessory building.
26           West Valley City permits cargo containers only in agricultural zones.
27           Lehi allows them with restrictions against stacking.
28
29   Councilmember McCay supported potential adjustments to Riverton’s current ordinance
30   regarding cargo containers. She agreed with the Mayor's suggestion to allow cargo containers
31   for “primarily” agricultural use rather than “exclusively,” which could accommodate additional
32   uses such as garden supplies. She also supported allowing cargo containers on lots over half
33   an acre and positioned in the backyard, behind the house, to avoid the issues seen with storage
34   units in front driveways.
35
36   Councilmember Pierucci clarified his preference for allowing cargo containers citywide, rather
37   than restricting them to agricultural uses. He suggested that containers should be subject to
38   similar standards as storage sheds, including placement behind the property and adherence to
39   materials or colors that match the building. He was open to exploring specific size requirements
40   and other details but emphasized the need for a consistent and practical approach.
41
42   Councilmember McDougal expressed support for allowing cargo containers citywide, aligning
43   them with standards similar to those for storage sheds. He recommended that containers be
44   allowed on various lot sizes, but with clear specifications regarding acceptable exterior finishes.
45
46   Councilmember Haymond emphasized the importance of maintaining the aesthetic appeal of
47   the community while allowing cargo containers. He noted that even in large agricultural areas,
48   industrial structures can look out of place, and advocated for careful planning to prevent an
49   industrialized look in Riverton and to keep the overall design inviting and visually appealing.
50
51   Councilmember Buroker expressed strong reservations about permitting cargo containers in

 4            Motion to Reconsider Ordinance No. 24-14 – Firework Restrictions in City Parks
 5
 6   Councilmember Haymond reflected on a previous discussion regarding restrictions on fireworks
 7   in public parks. He acknowledged the reasoning behind the proposed restrictions, particularly
 8   the concern over potential damage to public property. However, he pointed out that there are
 9   already ordinances in place against damaging public property. He emphasized that public parks
10   are ideal locations for fireworks, especially for those who live in areas where space is limited
11   and setting off fireworks could pose risks to neighboring properties. He suggested that the
12   Council reconsider the restrictions, as he believes existing protections are sufficient and do not
13   want to deprive the community of the opportunity to enjoy fireworks in public parks.
14
15   Mr. Carter discussed concerns regarding the potential legal liabilities associated with allowing
16   fireworks in city parks. He acknowledged that while there are existing ordinances against
17   damaging public property, these may not fully address the risks posed by fireworks. He
18   explained that if a venue, such as a park, allows activities like fireworks that could foreseeably
19   cause harm, the City could be held liable, especially if an incident occurs due to negligence.
20
21   Mr. Carter further elaborated that while existing laws prohibit intentional property damage, they
22   may not be sufficient to prosecute cases where fireworks unintentionally cause harm, such as
23   when a garbage can is accidentally set on fire. He suggested that an ordinance specifically
24   prohibiting fireworks in parks could prevent such incidents.
25
26   Councilmember Pierucci expressed understanding of the challenges in proving intent in criminal
27   cases, particularly regarding property damage caused by fireworks. Initially opposed to
28   reconsidering the issue, he explained how discussion with another member led to new insights
29   and concerns that had not been fully explored in previous meetings. These included questions
30   about specific types of fireworks and the broader implications of restricting their use.
31
32   Councilmember Pierucci MOVED that the City Council Reconsider Ordinance No. 24-14 –
33   Firework Restrictions in City Parks. Councilmember Haymond SECONDED the motion.
34   Mayor Staggs called for discussion on the motion. Mayor Staggs called for a roll-call vote. The
35   vote was as follows: Buroker-yes, Haymond-yes, McCay-yes, McDougal-yes, and Pierucci-yes.
36   The motion passed unaminously.
