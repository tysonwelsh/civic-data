Wasatch Front Waste and Recycling District (WFWRD)

               Ms. Pam Roberts, Director, Wasatch Front Waste & Recycling District, delivered
a presentation on the recycling program about what should and should not go into the blue can.
The market has changed, and plastic bags are no longer acceptable. WFWRD has been
grappling with whether to even keep recycling because of the costs associated with it. Rocky
Mountain’s recycling fee is $44.28 a ton, Waste Management’s is $38.42, and the landfill’s is
$32.85 a ton. Based on WFWRD’s 2017 tonnages in recycling, its increased cost to process
recycled trash will be roughly $900,000 to $1 million, about $1 per home per month. The
increased costs at the landfill will be about $750,000 a year, and that is about a 75 cent per home
per month increase. So, the question is whether the community feels the extra 25 cents per
month to continue recycling is worth it. WFWRD has a survey on its website to find out what
community members think.

There are two landfills in the valley – the Salt Lake Valley Landfill and the Trans-Jordan Landfill.
The Trans-Jordan Landfill’s life expectancy is maybe 10 years; Salt Lake Valley’s is about 60-80
years. So, if WFWRD starts putting recycling in the landfills, that window will be shorter. Then,
there would be additional transportation costs, not to mention a bigger carbon footprint.

WFWRD’s next projected fee increase is for 2022. Historically, it has increased fees by about $2
a month every four to five years. It increases the fee when it draws down its fund balance. Right
now, there is fund balance, but the higher costs for recycling may require an increase earlier. She
will be reporting back to the board in September or October on the revenues, at which time the
board will be faced with a touch decision going into 2019.

Planning and Zoning

                Todd Draper, Planner, Salt Lake County Planning and Development Services
Division, reviewed the following rezone application, which was heard at the July 17, 2018, meeting
and continued to today:

             Application #30632 – Bryon Prince requesting approval of a General Plan
amendment and associated zone change from A-1 z/c to R-1-7 at 8003 West 2700 South and
8041 West 3100 South.

The main concern was between the current A-1 zone, the original proposal of R-1-7, and a
compromised proposal that the developer presented to the planning commission, which
forwarded it with a positive recommendation to the Council for approval in conjunction with a
development agreement. He reviewed the following options, pointing out that some of the
calculations are assumptions, so are not 100 percent accurate to the number of lots:

    •     Option 1 is if no rezone were approved, whereby the developer could build about 131 lots,
          with a net density of 4.3 units per acre.
    •     Option 2 is the original proposal to rezone to R-1-7, which allows 187 lots, with a net
          density of about 6.2 units per acre.
    •     Option 3 is an R-1-8 zone, which allows 64 lots, with about 5.4 units per acre, which is the
          option currently being recommended. The developer is comfortable with that if it is

                                                   6
DATE           TUESDAY                                AUGUST                               28, 2018

       rounded up to 66 lots, with a density of 5.5 units per acre. He would like to do all the lots
       immediately abutting the White Farm Village subdivision or Hibler Drive as a minimum
       10,000 square foot lots, with 20 percent of the balance of the land developed at 8,000
       square foot lots or larger, and the balance after that at 7,000 square foot lots or greater.
   •   Option 4 came about at the planning commission meeting, and it is to increase the amount
       of 8,000 square foot lots to a 50/50 balance of 8,000 and 7,000 square foot lots. The
       planning commission did not recommend that, and it would probably be a deal-killer for
       the developer.

It is still not known whether the developer wants to do a standard subdivision or a planned unit
development (PUD), but it looks like it will probably be a standard subdivision. Typically, a
development agreement is just done with a full subdivision plat to give a bonus density in
exchange for some things above what a PUD ordinance would offer. If the Council approved the
developer’s request in Option 3, it could put language in the ordinance to limit the density to that,
and that would accomplish the same goal as a development agreement. If the Council feels a
development agreement is still the best way to go, the planning staff will continue to work with the
developer on a whole package to bring to the Council.

               Council Member Peel asked how the Council would get the developer to agree
to some of the things the Council wanted if it did not do a development agreement.

               Paul Ashton stated the Council could grant the zone change subject to conditions,
and then spell out those conditions.

               Mr. Draper stated ordinance conditions are limited to dwelling unit density, building
square footage, height, and structure. The idea with a development agreement is the developer
would probably be putting a subdivision plat in front of the Council, and it would be the Council
approving that. His concern was if the Council pushed too much, it might end up with a different
developer.

               Council Member Prokopis asked at what point the developer could come back
and ask for a PUD. If the Council approves the zone change, it is basing that on the idea the
developer will do a standard subdivision.

              Mr. Draper stated once the property was rezoned, the developer could do a PUD.
That request would then go to the planning commission. By limiting the density in ordinance, the
Council could get that as a zoning condition, so it would not matter if the developer did a PUD or
a standard subdivision.

              Council Member Prokopis stated the last time the Council met with the
developer, they had already agreed to some of the conditions. He asked if those conditions were
being recommended.

             Mr. Draper stated no, most of those are outside zoning, and are done at the
development phase, whether it be a standard subdivision or a PUD.

               Mr. Ashton stated this would have to go back to the planning commission when
the developer adds the subdivision or PUD, and the planning commission would be approving the
details.

                Byron Prince, applicant, stated last month when he was before the Council, there
was some misunderstanding about what Ivory Homes was proposing; so the developer and owner
of the property and their representatives held a meeting with the community. The two biggest
concerns that came out of that meeting were: 1) what types of homes Ivory Homes was planning
to build, and the quality and design of those homes; and 2) density or lot width. Both the A-1 zone
and the R-1-8 allow for a 65-foot wide lot, but the concern was whether the lot widths would
accommodate a third-car garage, an RV pad, space for toys, etc.

He delivered a presentation on the plans. There are 13 different home plans including eight
ramblers and five two-story buildings. Each home will have two different types of elevations -
traditional or craftsman, or colonial. All homes will be full masonry, stucco, or lap siding. There
will be masonry walls along 2700 South and 3100 South. All homes will have a two to three car
garage. Ivory also provides 2,000 square feet of sod and two park strip trees with the purchase
of the home, and the CC&Rs require that go in within nine months of the certification of occupancy.
The minimum square footage will be just under 4,000 square feet for a four-bedroom home; the
largest home over 4,000 square feet for seven bedrooms, with a basement. The average price
point will be the $360,000-$370,000 range or higher.

The homes adjacent to Hibler Drive will be 10,000 square foot lots, and twenty percent of the
remaining lots in the subdivision will be larger than 8,000 square feet. The minimum lot width will
be 76 feet wide at the setback, except on a corner or knuckle lot where that would be impossible.
So, with that lot size, there will be less than 166 lots, and that is below an R-1-8 density.

There will be open space in the northeast corner, anticipated at about 2.45 acres, but at end of
day, the County’s engineering will decide the capacity of that stormwater facility. The bedrock in
the southeast corner will have to be excavated in or blasted to remove that material. The road
design will be the 55-foot wide right-away, the same road width as Hibler Drive. That will allow
for a 5-foot park strip and 4-foot sidewalk. There is a contractual commitment with the property
owner on roads, and it runs with the land.

Ivory Homes is not proposing an HOA or a PUD. It is proposing a compromise that will have a
superior outcome to an R-1-8 zone or even the current zoning because with the current zoning,
there would not be the wider lots along Hibler Drive.

               Council Member Peay asked if the retention pond would be landscaped.

                Mr. Prince stated he envisioned landscaping a small lot outside the stormwater
facility on the upper grade of the south end. That would be dedicated to the Magna Metro
Township and maintained by the township.

               Council Member Peay asked if Ivory Homes would be putting sidewalk, curb and
gutter along 3100 South.
