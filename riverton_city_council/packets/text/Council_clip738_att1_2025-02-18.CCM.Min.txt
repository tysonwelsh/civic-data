37          Short-Term Rental Discussion
38
39   Ryan Carter, City Attorney, discussed potential changes to Riverton’s short-term rental
40   regulations, prompted by concerns from residents. He referenced Murray City’s recently
41   adopted ordinance and asked the Council to consider whether they wanted to adopt a similar
42   approach to Murray’s or maintain the existing structure.
43
44   Councilmember McDougal asked Mr. Carter to clarify how long property owners must live on-
45   site. Mr. Carter said that property owners must live on-site for at least 185 nights per year if
46   they operate a short-term rental. Additionally, rentals are limited to 150 nights per year. If a
47   property is solely used for short-term rentals without an owner living there, it is in violation of
48   City code, and enforcement action would be taken upon receiving evidence.
49
50   While Councilmember McDougal supported the ability for homeowners to generate extra
51   income through short-term rentals, he also wanted to ensure that neighborhoods did not turn
     Page | 2 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     February 18, 2025




 1   into hotel districts, balancing property rights with maintaining residential quality of life.
 2
 3   Councilmember Buroker emphasized that the most critical aspect of the short-term rental
 4   ordinance is its enforceability. If issues arise and the City can effectively address them, then the
 5   ordinance is functioning as intended. As long as the residency requirement of 185 nights per
 6   year remains in place, the ordinance provides a clear standard for enforcement.
 7
 8   After the discussion, Councilmembers were not interested in changing the current ordinance.

11            City Noise Ordinance and Common Code Enforcement Issues
12
13   Chief Shane Taylor explained that law enforcement and code enforcement frequently address
14   nuisance and noise ordinance violations, with common issues including golf cart violations and
15   off-road vehicle infractions, such as dirt bikes and ATVs. Other prevalent code violations involve
16   operating without a business license, animals running at large, weed control and property
17   maintenance, improper storage of personal property, and unlawful parking, which remains a
18   daily enforcement issue.
19
20   Mr. Carter explained that a resident challenged the City’s noise ordinance, which prohibits motor
21   vehicles within 800 feet of a dwelling, after code enforcement responded to complaints about his
22   children riding mini bikes on an oval track in his backyard. The City maintains that mini bikes
23   generate significant noise and could be considered a nuisance if used continuously. Even
24   without the 800-foot rule, the general noise prohibition would still apply, requiring proof of
25   sustained disturbance. The resident received warnings, but enforcement is limited without clear
26   evidence. While minor exceptions to the ordinance could be considered, they would not change
27   this case.

30            4200 West Pond
31
32   Cary Necaise, Public Works Director, explained that the 4200 West pond project explores three
33   options for expanding water storage and potential public amenities. The existing pond holds 31
34   acre-feet, and the proposed options include: (1) expanding storage by 21 acre-feet with minimal
35   changes; (2) adding a second pond with a walking trail and landscaping; and (3) including a
36   fishing feature, which would require deeper excavation and a lift station. Costs range from
37   $1.1M for basic expansion to $1.4M for a landscaped fishing pond. Impact fees are expected to
38   cover costs within 1.5 years. While amenities enhance public use, they reduce storage capacity
39   and add maintenance costs.
40
41   Councilmember Buroker said that the primary objective of the 4200 West pond project was to
42   increase water retention capacity. She raised concerns about potential privacy issues for
43   neighboring homes if a trail were built at an elevated level. Additionally, access from the nearby
44   park would require crossing the canal, adding logistical challenges. While making the space
45   more visually appealing is desirable, she expressed reluctance to sacrifice significant water
46   storage capacity for amenities.
47
48   Councilmember McDougal reiterated that the primary goal of the 4200 West pond project was
49   water retention, and also expressed reluctance to reduce capacity for additional amenities. He
50   questioned the need for another fishing pond, given that one already exists in the City. Another
51   key concern was the long-term maintenance of new amenities, as multiple parks are already
     Page | 3 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     February 18, 2025




 1   planned or under development.
 2
 3   Councilmember McCay suggested modifying the first option to retain the additional water
 4   storage while incorporating some landscaping, benches, and trees. To address privacy
 5   concerns, she proposed shifting the northern pond closer to the property line and placing
 6   amenities along the Welby Jacob Canal rather than near residents' homes. She supported
 7   making the area a fishing pond, citing similar successful projects and emphasizing the demand
 8   for trails and paths based on resident surveys. She also noted the potential for securing
 9   additional funding through TRCC grants and emphasized the importance of creating
10   recreational opportunities for nearby high-density neighborhoods.
11
12   Mayor Staggs inquired about the potential impact of using TRCC funds on the project’s funding
13   structure, specifically whether adding amenities would limit the use of secondary water impact
14   fees. Mr. Hicks clarified that secondary impact fees could only be used for increasing pond
15   storage capacity, not for amenities. However, TRCC funds and park impact fees could
16   potentially cover beautification efforts, such as landscaping and recreational features. He
17   estimated that the excavation and expansion costs—approximately $1 million—could be funded
18   through impact fees, while the additional enhancements could be covered by other sources.
19
20   Councilmember Haymond asked about the estimated maintenance costs for the proposed pond
21   and potential liability concerns if it were to fail. Mr. Necaise explained that the state conducts
22   annual dam inspections to ensure compliance with safety regulations. He noted that any
23   modifications, including moving the pond closer to residences, would require state approval.
24   The inspection process ensures that structural integrity is maintained, reducing the risk of
25   failure.
26
27   Councilmember Pierucci inquired whether the estimated cost included a bridge over the canal
28   for access. Mr. Necaise explained that a bridge was factored into the estimate but was unsure
29   whether it covered both the canal crossing and the overpass. He noted the cost for a bridge was
30   $75,000 but needed clarification on whether this was for one or both crossings.
31
32   Mayor Staggs emphasized that functionality should be the top priority, noting that the goal of the
33   secondary water ponds is to maintain at least a couple of days' worth of flow for users. He also
34   pointed out that secondary water quality has been a long-standing concern among residents
35   and acknowledged the City's efforts to improve it through filtration, reducing micron levels at
36   pump stations, and increasing pressurization.

39            Business License Code Updates
40
41   Mr. Carter presented an overview of proposed updates to the business license code,
42   emphasizing that the goal was to modernize and streamline the existing regulations. Key
43   changes included requiring all forms to be submitted online rather than using City-provided
44   paper forms. Additionally, businesses would now need to provide EIN numbers and tax ID
45   numbers to verify proper state registration, addressing past issues where businesses operated
46   without state approval. Another significant change involved removing unnecessary or outdated
47   provisions. The issuance and renewal of business licenses were also clarified, ensuring that
48   renewal dates align with the original issuance date, maintaining the City's current practice. A
49   lengthy table detailing different fees for various business types was also eliminated, as it was no
50   longer in use; instead, business license fees will follow the City’s fee schedule, which currently
51   sets them at zero.

17            Water Rate Tiers
18
19   Mr. Hicks set the stage for discussing water rate adjustments by referring to previous
20   conversations in strategic planning meetings. He mentioned that the Council had given
21   feedback on the possibility of adding new rate tiers and possibly applying more dramatic
22   increases for larger water users. He expressed the intention to move forward with some minor
23   fee adjustments in the upcoming meeting and then pursue a more comprehensive study with a
24   consultant to refine the rates.
25
26   Mr. Geer walked the Council through four potential water rate options. His presentation is
27   include in the packet.
28
29   Councilmember Haymond raised a question about residents who never use secondary water
30   but still pay for it, particularly those who only use culinary water for lawn irrigation. Mr. Hicks
31   explained that a disconnection process is in place for those individuals, allowing them to be
32   charged a non-user rate of $4.50 if they prove they have fully disconnected from the secondary
33   water system.
34
35   Councilmember Haymond favored option three, which includes a $1.50 increase in the base
36   rate while maintaining tier adjustments. This approach would ensure the water enterprise fund
37   remains financially stable, with the option to readjust after the water study is completed.
38
39   Councilmember Pierucci expressed support for the fee structure because it charges based on
40   actual water usage, incentivizing more mindful consumption, unlike property taxes, which don't
41   reflect individual usage and also don't apply to schools and nonprofits. While he is generally
42   supportive of Option 3, he prefers a rate study to fully justify any significant changes, especially
43   given the likelihood of a property tax increase for water.
44
45   Councilmember Buroker asked if the current water usage fees were not enough to cover the
46   actual water costs. In response, Mayor Staggs explained that several factors have contributed
47   to this issue, such as the delay in the Greenwell coming online, a significant increase in water
48   usage (from 4,800 acre-feet to 6,200 acre-feet), and the resulting higher costs due to the Jordan
49   Valley Conservancy District's block two rates, which double the price per acre-foot when
50   consumption exceeds certain thresholds.
51
