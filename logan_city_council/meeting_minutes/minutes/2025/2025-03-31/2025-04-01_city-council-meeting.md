Sexual Assault Awareness Month – Jill Anderson, CAPSA (26:50)

Jill Anderson, CEO of CAPSA addressed the Council as April is Sexual Assault
Awareness Month.

Thank you for inviting us to highlight April as Sexual Assault Awareness Month. Last
year CAPSA served 2,429 individuals, with more than 30% of those individuals
experiencing sexual violence. We know that supporting survivors of sexual assault is
about advocacy, healing, and breaking down the immense barriers survivors face. Think
for a minute about the barriers you have seen or assume survivors face? Too often,
barriers such as shame, fear, lack of resources, and societal stigma keep survivors from
reaching out for help.
                                                                                  3 | Page
    Logan Municipal Council Minutes ~ Logan, Utah ~ April 1, 2025
At CAPSA we ensure survivors have access to confidential support, legal advocacy, and
trauma-informed care. And most importantly, we don't do this work alone. Through
education, outreach, and partnerships with healthcare providers, law enforcement, local
businesses, government leaders, all of you, and many others, we're strengthening the
networks that both prevent violence and support survivors toward healing.

But we need the community, and we need you. Businesses, educators, civic leaders, each
of us has a role to play in shifting cultural perceptions, fostering belief and empathy, and
ensuring survivors know they are not alone.

One of the biggest barriers survivors face is shame—a painful, isolating feeling that tells
someone they are flawed or unworthy. Shame doesn’t just impact survivors of sexual
violence; it can affect anyone struggling with mental health, digital harm, or self-doubt.
And too often, shame keeps people silent.

Recognizing this, CAPSA developed a prevention-focused curriculum called Shame-Free
Conversations, that we want to highlight and offer to the community. The curriculum is
designed to help adults support youth by navigating difficult topics in a way that fosters
openness, trust, and healing. These conversations prevent violence and harm while also
reducing feelings of helplessness and fear when harm does occur.

A shame-free conversation is rooted in empathy, understanding, and acceptance. It’s
about choosing words that make people feel safe and heard rather than ashamed or
dismissed. To help guide these conversations, as part of our curriculum we use the SAFE
framework:
   • Specific – Be clear and direct in your communication.

   •   Approachable – Create a safe and welcoming space.
   •   Acknowledge Feelings – Validate emotions and experiences.

   •   Empowering – Focus on strengths and solutions.

And above all, it starts with belief—because when someone shares their experience,
believing them is the most powerful step we can take.

As a community, we can create safer spaces and break cycles of shame. But programs
like Shame-Free Conversations—and all CAPSA’s prevention and response services—
depend on the support of our community. Funding from businesses, donors, and
community partners allows us to reach more youth, train more leaders, and ensure
survivors always have a place to turn.

Who in your circle; schools, businesses, youth programs—could benefit from a deeper
understanding of how to have these meaningful conversations?
Alyna and I here will leave a flyer, along with magnets that can be placed in community
or employee-facing spaces. These serve as a simple but powerful reminder: You matter at
CAPSA. Survivors matter at CAPSA. And abuse is never the victim’s fault.

Chair Simmonds requested for the benefit of the public an explanation of many

Councilmember A. Anderson requested for the benefit of the public an explanation of
EDAM.

Mr. Montgomery explained that EDAM is the Extended Day-Ahead Market that covers
part of the Western states. Currently, the City is not part of that market, the City buys
what is needed on the open market. EDAM mandates that low-serving entities like the
City have dispatchable power resources plus 15% more energy that can be called upon
when needed. Failure to meet the requirements results in penalties and forced purchases
of expensive power.

Councilmember López requested confirmation that the penalties would be incurred
because of a shortage of power.

Mr. Montgomery confirmed that the fees/penalties will be incurred as a result of failing
to have sufficient power.

Councilmember López requested a clarification on the type of penalties incurred.

Mr. Montgomery wasn’t able to provide a concrete answer as the penalties have yet to
become official as they are still being negotiated with FERC (Federal Energy Regulatory
Commission) and Rocky Mountain Power.

Chair Simmonds requested confirmation that the reason the City must comply and
participate is because of the transmission owned by Rocky Mountain Power.

Mr. Montgomery confirmed that the transmission used by the City is owned by Rocky
Mountain Power, and they are primary transmission providers for the State and for the
surrounding states. Rocky Mountain Power has decided to participate in EDAM resulting
in a low-serving Logan City automatically becoming a part of EDAM.

Councilmember A. Anderson said that the configurations of EDAM count different types
of energy differently such as solar, gas, wind, and so forth.

Mr. Montgomery confirmed that energy is calculated differently by EDAM according to
its capacity factor. Solar Energy is available only when the sun is up and can only count
for 30% of energy and as part of the energy capacity factor/calculation.

Councilmember López asked whether the penalty incurred is a result or rather a lack of
baseload power.

REQUEST
This is a proposal to add a fourth commercial node location along Highway 89/91 south of
Logan City at the 1700 South/Park Avenue intersection in the 17.21 “Gateway Overlay”
chapter and a new Special Event Temporary Signs section in the 17.33 “Signs” chapter of
the Land Development Code (LDC).

SUMMARY OF CHANGES
The proposed addition of a commercial node and associated setbacks at 1700 South/Park
Avenue and Highway 89/91 follows the recommendations of the South Corridor Plan
adopted in August of 2011. The South Corridor Plan encourages more concentrated
development around traffic signals and more open pastoral stretches in between. The 1700
South intersection has potential to develop on all four quadrants. The Blacksmith Fork
River runs along the north side of this intersection and development along this river will
be subject to all other applicable code sections.

The proposed addition to the LDC 17.33.110 Temporary Signs applies specifically to
special events and brings more clarity to these types of events. The new regulations have
specific timelines, total sign numbers, sizes, and placement standards for temporary signs
associated with special events. The proposed addition allows up to eight temporary signs
and two banners placed per event. Event signs cannot be placed more than seven days prior
and must be removed 24 hours after the event.

STAFF RECOMMENDATION
The changes to the 1700 South/Park Avenue commercial node will set the same regulations
as the three other southern commercial nodes already adopted. It will create a more uniform
develop pattern at every traffic signal along this corridor and will improve access and safety
for entering and exiting this stretch of Highway.

Prior to this temporary sign proposal, short term special events had to adhere to the general
temporary sign section, that is broader in nature and not always applicable to short-term
events. These two amendments with bring more clarity and equality to these two code
sections. Staff recommends approval of both amendments.

GENERAL PLAN
The Land Development Code was prepared and adopted to implement the vision expressed
in the General Plan. The purpose of these code changes is to update and clarify provisions
in the Land Development Code relative to corridor gateways and special events.
