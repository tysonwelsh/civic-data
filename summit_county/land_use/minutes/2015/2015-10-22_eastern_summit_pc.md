Chair Ure said before he gives the time to Director Putt, there is an issue the 
Commission needs to clarify.  That is the need to codify the minimum lot sizes of the 
different zones.  He opened the time for the Commission to have this discussion.     
 
Commissioner Wharton said the minimum lot size of the different zones will have a lot 
of different impacts.  There are property owners who own parcels with two or more 
different zones.  He believes they should be able to cluster the units of density and to 
move them.  A minimum 6-acre lot in a 6-acre zone would limit a landowner's ability to 
exercise what is called the one-lot subdivision.   
 
Commissioner Wharton said someone with six acres should be able to give or sell one 
acre to a child or sibling.  It should not have to be the entire six acres.  He is firm they 
need to establish a 1-acre minimum lot size in the 6-acre, 20-acre, 40-acre, and the 80-
acre zones.         
 
Commissioner Wharton said they should maintain the landowner’s ability to cluster to 
the maximum possible extent.  For example, if someone owns 18 acres in the AG-6 they 
can have three lots.  If the landowner wants to cluster three 1-acre lots for his children, 
they would have a remainder parcel of 15 acres.  This parcel would be deemed 
undevelopable and would remain as agriculture or another non-developable use.  He 
feels strongly they need to allow people to put smaller sized lots on their larger parcels.   
 
Commissioner Hanson said she agrees with what Commission Wharton has said.  The 
question is what about a one-acre zone.  Would the minimum lot size be one acre?  
Could it be less than an acre?  Commissioner Wharton said there is already language 
that says the minimum lot size in the AG-1 is ¾ of an acre.  So the 1-acre zone doesn't 
need to be discussed.

The reasons behind the "why they are doing this" begin with the fact that they are 
required to.   The State requires that anytime they enact a new law, they come to the 
user first and receive feedback on how the change may affect them.   
 
In addition to the State Code, the County's own Development Code requires they reach 
out to the public prior to making any changes.  Additionally, this is the right thing to do.  
They are the users and they are the ones that will be impacted by these decisions.  He 
emphasized that the material tonight is not the final end product.  Both Staff and the 
Planning Commission want to receive the public’s honest reaction to the draft.   
 
After they receive the public's input from both the south and north sides, they will look 
at the documents to see what needs to be revised, tweaked, or thrown out.  This 
document is not yet ready to recommend to the County Council.   
 
Director Putt said he will walk them through an explanation of the tools they are talking 
about for those who haven't been to a meeting.  If he gives a little background it will 
help them respond and give feedback that might be helpful.  He said the Eastern Summit 
County General Plan was updated two years ago in August.  The General Plan is a 
document that talks about the reasons why they will impose rules, regulations, and 
Codes that will affect their personal private property rights.   
 
The law is written to protect property rights.  They need to be careful in the 
administration of how these are regulated.  Among the most important is that if they are 
going to regulate private property it has to be done for a legitimate reason.  It cannot be 
just because someone thinks that it is a good idea.  Those who determine what a 
legitimate reason is are ultimately the elected officials.  That determination must have 
the input, dialogue, and feedback of the public.  If they are going to zone and map 
zoning, they have to have a legitimate legal foundation.

The General Plan is important because it creates the legal foundation for the things that 
will be implemented, like zoning and the different uses.  Where do these go?  How

Director Putt said in his estimation, the General Plan has a number of significant

The Planning Commission has gone through and taken a look at the uses in the various 
zones to make sure they have the right descriptions of the uses.  They have gone 
through and evaluated if the uses should be allowed, conditional, or low impact.  It was 
determined that in some instances, the uses that have been conditional could become 
administrative through the low impact permit.     
 
The idea is to base development rights on zoning compliance and not when a property 
was created.  To achieve that, they have gotten rid of the lot of record; however, there 
will still be some dates that are important triggers.  If someone comes to the counter to 
ask questions about developing their property, the question they will be asked is what 
zone is it in, and does it meet the minimum parcel size.  If it does, it will be a legal 
complying parcel.  The question won't be asked when it was created.   
 
If it isn't a legal complying parcel, it may have a grandfathered right associated with it.  
The grandfathered right has a date of May 1996.  If there is a grandfathered right, the 
landowner has the right to develop the property.  If the property doesn't have 
grandfathered status, it means the landowner has a legal non-complying parcel.  In 
order to develop it, the landowner would have to wait until the zoning changes.   
 
Director Putt added that a distinction has been made between a development right and 
a land use right.  A development right means that you can build some kind of structure; 
however, there are a lot of ways you can use your land that doesn't require building 
something on it.   
 
In the current language, you have to demonstrate you have a lot of record to use your 
property.  In the new language, if someone has a legal non-complying property, it 
doesn't mean that use of the property has been extinguished.  A list of uses would
