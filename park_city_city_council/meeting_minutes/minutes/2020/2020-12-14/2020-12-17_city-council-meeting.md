Nightly Rentals Discussion:
Rebecca Ward, Land Use Planner, Jonathan Weidenhamer, Economic Development
Manager, Beth Bynan, Business License Specialist, Dave Thacker, Building Official,
Captain Andrew Leatham, Police, and Minda Stockdale, Leadership, presented this
item. Ward stated this session was to track nightly rental complaints and to improve
business license compliance. She reviewed the requirements for owners of nightly
rentals. Nine complaints had been formally filed since 2017, but there were other
complaints that had been filed with Police or Code Enforcement.

Weidenhamer stated a Host Compliance contract went into effect this year and this
service would help with nightly rental business license renewals and scheduling nightly
rental business license applications. A new RFP would be issued in 2022 and the scope
of service would be expanded to include a bi-lingual hotline and an online complaint

were 5,100 nightly rentals in the City limits and the software had identified 2,000 of
those. Two letters had been sent to owners in an effort to increase the number of
rentals in compliance.

Mayor Beerman asked if the 5,100 units included condo/hotel units or only short-term
rentals with AirBnB and such. Stockdale stated this included all nightly rentals. Council
Member Gerber asked if a property management company had to license each unit in a
complex. Stockdale affirmed each unit had to be licensed.

Council Member Worel asked if the City could work through VRBO and other
companies to send notifications to property owners about the requirement to be
licensed. Ward indicated she would look into that. Currently, the police were working on
updating their software and sending noise and disturbance police reports to the Finance
Department so they could be linked to business licenses. Parking was working on
updating the Seven Day Lodging Parking permit so the license plate numbers would be
linked to the business license. She also noted there was a webpage on the City website
with information on nightly rentals so complaints could be tracked.

Council Member Doilney confirmed 212 nightly rentals came into compliance in the last
12 months. Council Member Gerber asked if property management companies could be
required to show that all their units were in compliance. Weidenhamer stated the people
participating in the community like bigger property management companies were
compliant. The City was looking for the small virtual companies or individuals who were
out of compliance.

Mayor Beerman stated many cities had to initiate lawsuits to get compliance. Council
Member Doilney asked how Park City measured to other cities. Weidenhamer stated
cities in Colorado had 80%-90 % compliance and Park City had less than 50 %
compliance.

Council Member Joyce stated the State did not allow cities to go after nightly rentals to
force them to comply. He asked if the City could work with the Utah League of Cities
and Towns (ULCT) to explain this issue was about the safety of the units and have them
try to change the legislation. Dias reviewed that in 2016, the legislature approved the bill
that eliminated the cities’ ability to regulate Transportation Network Companies (TNC).
In 2017, they tried to do the same for nightly rentals, but Park City had limited success
and got the law passed that was currently in place. Staff would continue to try to modify
the law, but it was hard for ULCT to help since the law only applied to six cities out of
300.

Council Member Henney asked about the cost of soils remediation and the remediation
plan. Everitt stated the City was developing a small soils repository at the Gordo site by
Quinn’s Junction. Those costs were built in with the total construction excavation cost.
There would be a cost associated with the construction and maintenance of the
repository. Council Member Henney stated everyone thought the Council did a great

Park City Arts and Culture District - Follow Up Discussion and Feedback
(continued from Work Session):
Council Member Henney indicated in 2017 the project was estimated to cost $60
million. Today the estimate was $107 million, so this conversation was necessary. He
thought Council was on the right path and would continue to figure it out.

Council Member Joyce agreed with Council Member Henney that the increased price
and impact to other projects were concerns. He felt there were other issues the City
needed to tackle. He stressed that there wouldn't be a district without the partners.

Council Member Worel stated she hadn't heard enough about the connectivity to the
Santy and Eccles. She also wanted to revisit the housing component of the district.

Council Member Gerber stated it would be helpful to see the rental model in order to
see how that would work, and the cost of other housing projects such as Homestake. A
discussion should take place on who would live in this housing. She was excited to see
this district move forward. She knew arts and culture was important and it was evident
during the pandemic.

Council Member Doilney agreed with all the comments. The difficult questions had to be
asked because Council had to question moving forward at all stages to make sure it
was viable. It was not irresponsible to ask the partners to commit. He looked forward to
doing the project, but more work on the drawings was needed.

Mayor Beerman summarized Council looked for a path forward. The hitches were tied to
financial constraints. The City needed to sit down with its partners to get firmer
commitments. Their commitment was a critical part of the funding and it wouldn't be the
same project without them. There were concerns with connectivity, walkability, and
transit. Council needed to talk about how to pay for that. They also needed to discuss
why housing was high and compare it to the cost of being done in other areas. Council
wanted to see what the long-term financial model looked like for the project. In January,
a couple scenarios of courses of action would be presented. His goal was to give
direction to staff by mid-January.

Dias thanked Council for the meaningful discussion. He thought Council would hear
from the community over the next few weeks, which would be beneficial in this process.

Council Member Joyce asked to look at the next 15 years to see what the City’s income

BACKGROUND
The goal of the year-long planning phase was to develop a set of collective priorities and action steps, a roadmap
for Arts and Culture, to serve the entire Summit County community including Park City, Snyderville Basin/Kimball
Junction, and North and South Summit County.
The process was designed to identify needs and develop solutions that would benefit the community at large, grow
investment in the creative sector, connect Arts and Culture to other city and county priorities, and support the
long-term vitality of our Arts and Culture nonprofits.
A broad coalition of individuals from the nonprofit, business, government, creative, and philanthropic sectors came
together to guide the process which was managed by the Arts Council.
SEPTEMBER 2015: Local Arts and Culture nonprofit leaders begin to organize and advocate for support to
secure stability and growth within our creative sector.
FEBRUARY 2016: Both Park City Council and Summit County Council appoint a representative to act as a
liaison to a countywide Arts and Culture Initiative Steering Committee.
MARCH 2016: The Steering Committee is formed with representatives from government, business, Arts
and Culture nonprofits, and philanthropy. The committee begins to look at other plans, develops a
definition of Arts and Culture, and starts to compile a list of Arts and Culture assets.
NOVEMBER 2016: The Steering Committee formally states its support for a collaborative, countywide Arts
and Culture master-planning process, and the PCSC Arts Council is appointed to take the lead management
role to move it forward.
DECEMBER 2016 – APRIL 2017: Outreach and fundraising efforts occur to generate community support and
resources for the Arts and Culture master planning process.
MAY 2017: Project ABC publicly launches.

JUNE – OCTOBER 2017: Data-collection activities include a survey, interviews, workgroup meetings, community outreach, and
engagement activities. More than 1,000 people share their ideas, needs, wants, and challenges.

NOVEMBER 2017: Data analysis results in the identification of draft Collective Priorities and Strategic Recommendations. The
Project ABC Summit is held Nov. 14 with more than 80 people coming together during a five-hour work session to review progress
and findings and begin action planning.

DECEMBER 2017 – MARCH 2018: Strategy review and meetings with key stakeholder groups are held to identify projects planned or
in development that connect to specific recommendations. Three open houses offer an opportunity to gather final feedback from
the community.

APRIL 2018: The Project ABC Plan is released with endorsements from key stakeholder groups. Implementation phase begins.

MAY 2018 - PRESENT: Arts Council manages the Project ABC Cultural Plan and tracks progress and implementation supported by an
Advisory Committee. Project ABC Community Convenings are held surrounding topics relevant to the Plan.

Project ABC was designed as a multi-stakeholder initiative, conducted with community engagement at
the forefront. The core intent of the process was to listen, document, and represent the needs of the

Culture in Summit County remain vibrant, collaborative, sustainable, and resilient into the future.

CONCLUSIONS
●   Arts and Culture sector in Summit County is growing with both occupations and industries currently
    outpacing overall growth countywide.


●   Arts and Culture has become an increasingly important piece of Summit County’s economy and will
    continue to grow in importance over the coming years.


●   The greatest concern when looking at the data in this analysis is the average wages in both Arts and
    Culture occupations and Arts and Culture industries. In both instances, Summit County is well below
    the national average. Given that Summit County’s cost of living is approximately 40% higher than the
    national average, a significant disparity has been created. This makes living and working in Summit
    County difficult for Arts and Culture employees and has likely limited the sector’s growth and will
    continue to do so into the future without intervention.
