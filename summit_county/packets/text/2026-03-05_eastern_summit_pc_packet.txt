Proposal 
On June 16, the Community Development Department received a plat amendment application 
requesting approval to vacate approximately 0.33 acres  from lot 5 in t he Weber Bend Ranch  
Subdivision and add that acreage to parcel NS-459-2-A. Parcel NS-459-2-A is owned by Montana 
Gorden and currently contains approximately 5 .28 acres. Parcel WBR -5 is owned by Jackson 
Land & Livestock LLC  and currently contains approximately 5.84  acres. The purpose of the 
amendment is to adjust the common boundary between the two parcels. The plat amendment 
would not create any additional density . Following approval of this plat amendment, p arcel 
WBR-5 would be reduced to approximately 5.51 acres, and parcel NS- 459-2-A would be 
increased to approximately 5.61 acres.

Background 
The W eber Bend Ranch (WBR) Subdivision was legally established through a Summit County 
land-division process recorded under Entry No. 601516 in 2001. Lot 5 is owned by Jackson Land 
& Livestock LLC and consists of approximately 5.84 acres. Parcel WBR-5 is currently  developed 
with a primary residence. Parcel NS-459-2-A is not within a platted subdivision. Parcel NS -459-
2-A is owned by Montana Gorden and currently developed with a primary residence.  
 
Under Section 1 1-4-5 of th e Eastern Summit County  Development Code, the Eastern Summit 
County Planning Commission serves as the Land Use Authority for any amendment to a 
recorded subdivision plat involving a lot  vacation. Community Development staff, in 
coordination with applicable service providers, has reviewed the application and determined 
that the project complies with all relevant requirements of the Eastern Summit County 
Development Code. 
 
Analysis and Findings 
Chapter 4 – Subdivision Plat Amendment (11-4-5) 
The Planning Commission may approve, approve with conditions, or deny the proposed Plat 
Amendment based upon written findings of fact according to each of the following standards. It 
is the responsibility of the applicant to provide written and graphic evidence demonstrating 
compliance. 
 
Standard 1: The Land Use Authority is satisfied that the public interest will not be materially 
injured by the proposed vacation, alteration or amendment, and there is good cause for the 
vacation, alteration or amendment, the Land Use Authority, may vacate, alter or amend the 
final subdivision plat, or any portion of the final subdivision plat. 
 Analysis: There is good cause for the amendment as the proposed plat amendment 
would bring an existing house into the parcel boundaries of the homeowner’s land. COMPLIES 
 
Standard 2: No subdivision plat amendment shall be approved which results in an increase in 
density unless the requirements set forth in subsection E6 of this section for a re-subdivision 
have been satisfied. 
 Analysis: The proposed amendment does not create new density. COMPLIES 
 
Public Notice, Meetings and Comments  
The project was publicly noticed as a public hearing and potential action item before the 
Eastern Summit County  Planning Commission ( ESCPC). The notice was published in the Park 
Record and all required locations. Courtesy postcards were mailed to property owners within 
1,000 feet of p arcel WBR-5 and NS-459-2-A. As of the date of this Staff Report, no public 
comments have been received by Planning Staff.

Recommendation 
Staff has reviewed the application for compliance with all standards in the Eastern Summit 
County Development Code and found that it meets the requirements for approval. Staff

conditions of approval. 
 
Findings of Fact: 
1. On June 16, 2025, the Summit County Community Development Department received a 
complete application for a Subdivision Plat Amendment to vacate a portion of Lot WBR-
5 within the Weber Bend Ranch Subdivision. 
2. The Weber Bend Ranch (WBR) Subdivision was legally established through a Summit 
County land division process and recorded under Entry No. 601516 in 2001. Lot WBR-5 
contains approximately 5.84 acres. 
3. The applicant is requesting approval to vacate approximately 0.33 acres from Lot WBR-5 
and incorporate that acreage into parcel NS-459-2-A. 
4. Following approval of the proposed plat amendment, Lot WBR-5 would be reduced to 
approximately 5.51 acres, and parcel NS-459-2-A would increase to approximately 5.61 
acres. 
5. Lot WBR-5 is owned by Jackson Land & Livestock LLC. Parcel NS-459-2-A is owned by 
Montana Gorden. 
6. Both Lot WBR-5 and parcel NS-459-2-A are currently developed with a single-family 
dwelling. 
7. The Eastern Summit County Planning Commission is the designated land use authority 
for subdivision plat amendments pursuant to the Eastern Summit County Development 
Code. 
8. The proposed amendment does not create any additional lots and does not increase 
development density. 
9. The Community Development Department properly noticed the plat amendment for a 
public hearing before the Eastern Summit County Planning Commission. Courtesy 
notices were mailed to property owners within 1,000 feet of Lots WBR-5 and NS-459-2-
A. 
10. Relevant service providers have reviewed the proposal and have indicated that the 
amendment complies with applicable service standards and requirements. 
 
Conclusions of Law:  
1. The proposal conforms to the applicable standards in set forth in 1 1-4-5 of the Eastern 
Summit County Development Code. 
2. Approval of the proposed plat amendment will not adversely affect public health, 
safety, and general welfare.

Chapter 1: Introduction 
Purpose of The General Plan 
 
The Eastern Summit County General Plan establishes a long -range policy framework to guide 
growth, development, resource protection, and public investment decisions. The Plan reﬂects the 
community’s evolving values and priorities, recognizing the importanc e of maintaining open lands, 
rural and small-town character, environmental stewardship, economic opportunity, and respect for 
private property rights. 
 
The General Plan is intended to support coordinated decision-making by elected officials, appointed 
boards and commissions, County staff, residents, and property owners. It identiﬁes goals, 
objectives, policies, and implementation strategies that provide direction for land use regulations, 
capital improvement planning, infrastructure investment, and community development efforts. The 
Plan emphasizes thoughtful, well -managed growth that aligns with available infrastructure, water 
resources, and long-term community sustainability. 
 
State law requires Summit County to adopt and maintain a General Plan to guide future growth and 
development. While the Plan does not regulate land directly, it serves as the foundation for land use 
regulations, zoning decisions, and County programs. 
 
The General Plan provides clear policy direction while allowing ﬂexibility in implementation. The 
goals, objectives, and policies work together to guide decision- making rather than function as rigid 
standards. Individual decisions should consider the full  context of the Plan, balancing community 
priorities, site-speciﬁc conditions, infrastructure capacity, and long-term impacts. When evaluating 
proposals, decision makers should strive to advance the overall intent of the Plan — supporting rural 
character, responsible resource management, and appropriately phased growth — while 
recognizing that not every policy will apply equally in every situation. 
 
How to Use the Plan 
 
The Plan is organized into Chapters addressing key elements of community planning such as,  Land 
Use, Housing, Transportation and Connectivity, Economic Development, Natural and Cultural 
Resources, Open Space, Community Health and Recreation, Sustainable Development, Water Use, 
and Public Facilities and Services. Each Chapter describes existing conditions, and establishes 
goals, objectives, policies, and strategies.  
 
Goals deﬁne the community’s long-term vision. Objectives identify measurable steps toward these 
goals. Policies guide decision-making, and strategies outline speciﬁc actions. Together they provide 
a framework for implementing the plan. 
 
The General Plan includes a Future Land Use Map illustrating preferred development patterns. This 
map is conceptual and ﬂexible, is not zoning map, and may evolve over time as conditions and 
community priorities change. The map reﬂects the unique character and needs of each area while 
remaining consistent with the overall goals, objectives, policies, and strategies of the plan.

The ﬁnal chapter, implementation, identiﬁes speciﬁc actions, responsibilities, timelines, and

Summit County Context  
 
Eastern Summit County is deﬁned by its landscape, water resources, and rural heritage. For 
thousands of years, the region supported seasonal travel, hunting, gathering and trade by the 
Shoshone and Ute tribes, who established a lasting relationship between people and the land. 
European settlement in the mid -1800s introduced permanent agricultural communities in areas 
such as Henefer, Coalville, and Kamas, where reliable water supplies supported farming, mining, 
ranching and timber production. These activities established the rural working landscape that 
continues to shape community identity today.  
 
Today, Eastern Summit County is experiencing increasing growth pressures that are reshaping land 
use patterns, housing  demand, and community expectations. Interest in rural living, recreation-
based tourism, and second home development has expanded development potential across the 
region. Managing this growth while preserving agricultural viability, open space, and rural character 
presents one of the County’s most signiﬁcant planning challenges. 
 
The East Side’s large geographic scale and dispersed settlement patterns create unique constraints 
for infrastructure and service delivery. Many areas rely on individual water and wastewater systems 
and are located far from centralized public services. Exp anding infrastructure across rural 
landscapes can be costly and requires careful coordination with growth patterns to ensure that 
development remains ﬁscally sustainable and does not exceed the County’s ability to provide long 
term services.  
 
Transportation connectivity is another signiﬁcant issue affecting Eastern Summit County. Residents 
often rely on regional highways and limited roadway networks to access employment centers, 
education, healthcare, and commercial services both within and outside the County. Seasonal 
tourism, commuter travel, and recreational visitation contribute to congestion, safety concerns, and 
maintenance demands on transportation infrastructure.  
 
Housing availability and affordability have emerged as growing concerns throughout the region. 
Increasing land values, demand for second homes, and limited housing diversity make it difficult for 
local workforce populations, agricultural employees, and younger residents to ﬁnd attainable 
housing within their communities. Maintaining stable, year-round communities while 
accommodating population growth remains a key planning priority.  
 
Natural hazards, and environmental conditions also inﬂuence land use decisions. Wildﬁre risk, 
ﬂooding, drought, and watershed protection are increasingly important  considerations as 
development expands into wildland and agricultural areas. Protecting public safety, maintaining 
water quality and quantity, and reducing hazard exposure require coordinated land use planning and 
resource management strategies.  
 
Agriculture continues to play a vital economic, cultural, and scenic role in Eastern Summit County, 
but faces challenges including land fragmentation, rising property values, generational transition, 
18
and competing development pressures. Supporting long term agricultural viability requires policies 
that balance economic sustainability with preservation of working lands.  
 
Eastern Summit County is also inﬂuenced by regional economic and travel patterns. Proximity to 
recreation destinations, neighboring counties, and major employment centers create opportunities 
for economic activity while increasing development interest and infrastructure demand. 
Coordinating planning efforts across jurisdictional boundaries is essential to effectively address 
transportation, housing, environmental, and service delivery challenges.  
 
As growth continues and community expectations evolve, Eastern Summit County faces important 
decisions regarding land use, infrastructure investment, environmental stewardship, and 
community development. This General Plan establishes a policy framework to guide these 
decisions, ensuring that future growth supports community values, protects natural resources, and 
maintains the rural character and quality of live that deﬁne the East Side.  
 
Demographics 
Eastern Summit County is characterized by small family -oriented communities with relatively high 
homeownership rates and modera te but growing population levels. Kamas Valley serves as the 
primary population center , while C oalville, Oakley, Henefer , and smaller rural areas maintain 
distinct community identities. Recent demographic trends reﬂect gradual population growth, 
increasing housing costs, and expanding in-migration inﬂuenced by regional affordability pressures 
and lifestyle migration. The population remains relatively young compared to many rural regions but 
is gra dually diversifying in both age and cultural composition. These trends inﬂuence housing 
demand, transportation needs, infrastructure planning, and service delivery across the East side.

Chapter 2:      Land Use (LU) 
 
Eastern Summit County places a high value on the long-term stewardship of open lands, agricultural 
resources, water systems, and its small-town rural character. Preserving visible open landscapes, 
maintaining separation between communities, and sustaining the scale and identity of existing 
towns are central to the community’s vision for the future. While agriculture remains an important 
part of the County’s heritage and landscape, community discussions reﬂect a broader commitment 
to protecting open and undeveloped lands as deﬁning features of the East Side. 
 
Land use policies within this chapter are intended to guide growth in a manner that reinforces 
community identity, protects natural and working lands, and supports sustainable public services 
over time. These policies recognize private property rights and seek to provide reasonable 
opportunities for landowners, while also acknowledging the broader community impacts of 
development, including infrastructure capacity, water availability, environmental conditions, 
agricultural viability, and long-term ﬁscal responsibility. 
 
The intent of these policies is not to prevent growth, but to guide growth deliberately and responsibly. 
This includes recognizing the cumulative effects of development and ensuring that larger or more 
complex projects are appropriately phased and aligned with available services, infrastructure 
capacity, and long-term community goals. 
 
The broader regional context, existing conditions, and community engagement ﬁndings that inform 
the Land Use Element are described in Chapter 1. This Chapter builds upon that foundation by 
translating community values and planning priorities into land use goals, objectives, and policies. 
 
Community engagement consistently emphasized the importance of protecting open space, 
preserving small town rural character, maintaining separation between communities, improving 
housing options for local residents and workforce, managing traffic impacts, safeguarding water 
resources, and supporting vibrant, well-designed commercial areas that serve local needs. 
 
Existing Conditions  
 
Eastern Summit County’s land use currently includes a mix of residential, agricultural, recreational, 
and conservation areas. Zoning also allows for limited commercial uses along the highway corridor 
from Henefer to Woodland. Residential densities range from approximately one unit per ﬁve acres to 
one unit per eighty acres, with higher densities concentrated in the established unincorporated 
communities of Woodland, Marion, Oakley, Peoa, Wanship, Hoytsville, Upton and Echo. 
 
The Easten Summit County Development Code states that the intent of this zoning framework is to: 
 
“ …ensure that the rural, agricultural and small-town character of the eastern portion of the 
County shall remain, even in the presence of growth and change. The County intends to 
assure the managed, proper, and sensitive development of land to protect and enhance 
these desired qualities and the lifestyle that exists. ”

community areas while maintaining large areas of agricultural and rural land at low densities. This 
approach reﬂects long -standing efforts to preserve rural character, protect agricultural lands, and 
limit scattered or sprawling development patterns across the landscape.

Chapter 3: Housing 
 
Housing Goals, Policies, & Strategies  
 
Rather than being monolithic, moderate-income housing is a multifaceted challenge for Summit 
County. It is tied closely to a variety of challenges including extremely high median housing costs; 
traﬃc congestion and legacy transportation issues; availability of suﬃcient local laborers; 
business challenges due to lack of access to labor; an increasingly non -diverse community; a 
shrinking number of full -time residents with long -term community and generational ties; 
increasing environmental degradation due to increasing development pressures; concerns about 
sustainability of air , land, and water natural resources; and the critical need to manage and limit 
rapid growth in a County that seeks to preserve open spaces, agricultural and cultural heritage 
(collectively, “Critical Concerns”). Accordingly, creating standards and strategies concerning 
moderate-income housing in this Chapter would be incomplete, ineﬀective, and potentially 
harmful to the vision and goals of this General Plan as well as the County Council’s vision, values, 
and strategic goals without carefully evaluating the impacts of any such standards and strategies 
on the Critical Concerns to determine if they will aggravate or mitigate and provide solutions to 
those Critical Concerns. All such stan dards and strategies and the Critical Concerns should 
continually be evaluated given the dynamic nature of growth in and on the borders of the County. 
The Summit County Manager, relevant staﬀ as determined by the County Manager, and the 
County Council should regularly assess the County’s moderate -income housing needs and seek 
to articulate where such housing is most appropriate and for whom the need is greatest at any 
given moment always taking into careful consideration the Critical Concerns (“Moderate-Income 
Housing Assessment”). A priority of any such assessment should seek to identify if housing  
challenges are creating signiﬁcant labor shortages in critical areas such a law enforcement, 
emergency medical services, education, doctors and nurses and other health care professionals, 
and local government workforce. In addition, the Moderate-Income Housing Assessment should 
examine the needs of long-term resident seniors seeking to age in place in the County as well as 
the County’s tourism industry’s workfor ce. With respect to tourism workforce, any strategies 
developed in this Chapter 7 should seek to require major employers to bear some of the burden 
of providing employees with housing opportunities as well as reasonable wages that consider the 
high housing  costs in resort counties much like the diﬀerence in wages paid in rural Utah 
compared to San Francisco, California. 
 
External inﬂuences also need to be part of the Moderate -Income Housing Assessment, such as 
whether nightly rentals are removing long-term housing from the available housing capacity with 
a result of hollowing out formerly well-established neighborhoods, particularly outside of resort 
cores. Such assessment should consider any moderate -income housing permitted, platted, 
commenced and/or built since the prior Moderate -Income Housing Assessment. Careful 
consideration of where to locate housing is a fundamenta l question the Moderate -Income 
Housing Assessment needs to examine in each instance with a focus on the Critical Concerns. For 
example, building or incentivizing aﬀordable housing in the Snyderville Basin for County 
workforce that work primarily in County  oﬃces in Coalville requires commuting, which the 
30
moderate-income housing plan should seek to reduce. In addition, approving a large ratio of 
market rate housing to obtain a smaller ratio of aﬀordable housing exacerbates the dearth of 
aﬀordable housing given the additional services and workers required to service the market rate 
housing, and should be avoided. The critical questions of each Moderate -Income Housing 
Assessment should include for whom, ideal location(s), transit and transportation access, trails 
access, and access to services to reduce comm uting and car trips, as well an assessment of the 
state of each of the Critical Concerns.  Those preparing the Moderate -Income Housing 
Assessment need to include speciﬁc metrics that are transparent, eﬀective, and easy to 
understand that can be used to provide positive or negative impacts that may be used by the 
County to adjust the goals and strategies contained in this Chapter 7. 
 
With respect to all analyses and changes to land use regulations contained in this Chapter 7, the 
County should carefully consider the positive and negative impacts on each of the Critical 
Concerns to make sure any such changes are likely to reduce deﬁcits identiﬁed in the Moderate-
Income Housing Assessment and do not result in unintended consequence s that negatively 
impact the Critical Concerns.  
 
Moderate income housing is deﬁned as: “Housing occupied or reserved for occupancy by 
households with a gross household income equal to or less than 80% of the median gross income

that the median gross income for the County is substantially higher than the national average, 
consideration should be given to requiring moderate -income housing that is targeted to lower 
median gross income depending upon the intended s olutions sought as identiﬁed in the 
Moderate-Income Housing Assessment.

Chapter 4: Transportation and Connectivity (TR) 
 
The Transportation and Connectivity Element establishes policies and strategies that guide the planning, 
design, and coordination of Eastern Summit County’s transportation network, including roads, transit, 
pedestrian and equestrian pathways, and bicycle r outes. Transportation planning supports the County’s 
commitment to preserving small town rural character, maintaining separation between communities, 
protecting natural systems, and directing growth to appropriate locations. 
 
Transportation investments align with land use decisions to ensure development occurs in areas where 
infrastructure capacity exists or can be responsibly expanded. The County coordinates roadway 
improvements, active transportation facilities, and transit p lanning with anticipated growth patterns, 
water resource considerations, and long-term fiscal sustainability. 
 
Summit County maintains a Long- Range Transportation Plan and an Active Transportation Plan. This 
Transportation Element works in coordination with those adopted plans and translates countywide 
transportation priorities into land use and development policy guidance specific to Eastern Summit 
County. 
 
Context 
 
Eastern Summit County’s settlement pattern and transportation network are shaped by the regional 
highway system. Interstate 80 runs through the northern portion of the County and physically divides the 
communities of Hoytsville, Coalville, and Henefer. State Routes 32 and 25 serve as primary corridors 
through South Summit, connecting Peoa, Oakley, Marion, Kamas, Francis, and Woodland. Much of the 
local road network parallels these state and federal routes, with County roads such as Hoytsville Road, 
Democrat Alley, and frontage roads providing local access. 
 
Summit County classifies roads into two primary Level of Service categories: 
 
• Class B Roads, which are improved and maintained by the County; and 
• Class D Roads, which are historic public roads and rights-of-way that are generally not maintained. 
 
Transportation planning in Eastern Summit County must balance regional mobility with preservation of 
rural landscapes, agricultural lands, and community identity. Roadway expansions and new connections 
are evaluated in the context of growth management, open space preservation, and long -term 
infrastructure costs. 
 
Due to Eastern Summit County’s rural form and dispersed settlement pattern, public transit and 
connected active transportation facilities have historically been limited. Community engagement 
identified a desire for improved transit options, expanded sidewa lk networks within town centers, safer 
roadway crossings, and increased connectivity through multi-use trails that link neighborhoods, schools, 
commercial areas, and recreation destinations. 
 
The County partners with municipalities including Oakley, Kamas, and Francis; the Utah Department of 
Transportation; and the Mountainland Association of Governments on corridor-level planning efforts such 
as the State Route 32 Corridor and City Centers Plan. This effort establishes a coordinated vision for the 
SR-32 corridor from Oakley to Francis, integrating land use, transportation, economic development, and 
35
recreation planning to strengthen community centers while preserving small town rural character and 
avoiding corridor-style strip development.

Community Engagement  
 
Community feedback consistently emphasized the importance of improving transportation connections 
throughout Eastern Summit County. Transportation connections —  including pedestrian paths, bicycle 
facilities, public transit, and senior transportation optio ns — ranked as the second most identified 
opportunity for improvement. 
 
Key Themes 
 
1. Heavy Reliance on Driving: Survey results indicate that 87% of respondents currently drive alone 
when accessing destinations within the community. This reflects the rural nature of Eastern 
Summit County and the limited availability of alternative transportation options. 
 
2. Strong Interest in Transportation Alternatives : While most residents currently drive, many 
expressed a desire for additional options: 
 
a. 47% indicated they would ideally like to walk to destinations within their community. 
b. 43% would prefer to ride a bicycle. 
c. 34% would like to utilize public transit for local trips. 
 
This gap between current travel behavior and preferred options suggests unmet demand for safer 
and more connected multimodal infrastructure. 
 
3. Connectivity and Safety Concerns: Residents expressed significant concern regarding the quality, 
availability, and connectivity of pedestrian and bicycle facilities: 
 
a. 55% indicated sidewalks are lacking in quantity and quality. 
b. 53% indicated bike lanes are lacking in quantity and quality. 
c. 66% stated that sidewalks and bike lanes do not connect to final destinations. 
d. 22% identified a lack of safe roadway crossings as a concern. 
 
These responses highlight the importance of focusing not only on adding infrastructure, but 
ensuring it is continuous, connected, and safe. 
 
36
4. Preserving Community Character: Community members also expressed a preference for 
minimizing the visual dominance of automobiles and roadway infrastructure, particularly within 
town centers. This reflects a desire to enhance small-town character while improving safety and 
accessibility.
