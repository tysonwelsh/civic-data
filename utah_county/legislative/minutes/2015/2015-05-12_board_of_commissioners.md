1.     PRESENTATION OF THE UTAH COUNTY VETERANS SERVICES ORGANIZATION
       CONSOLIDATED REPORT
Randy Edwards, Utah County Veterans Service Coordinator, came forward to read a prepared statement (a copy of which
can be found attached to these minutes). He spoke of the importance of veterans in the community, and the problems
facing organization such as membership slumps, apathy, and sporadic attendance. Mr. Edwards stated veterans need to
be renewed with action, and the responsibility of telling their stories. He thanked the commissioners for their help in
achieving a new vision wherein the focus is service and community.

Randy Edwards reviewed accomplishments of the Utah County Veterans Service Organization during 2014, including
Service Councils, Holiday Ceremonies, involvement in the Freedom Festival, and collaboration with local schools, scout
groups, and medical personnel. The veterans have participated, hosted, and organized all sorts of events from American
Legion meetings to charity pancake breakfasts, blood drives to memorial services, and Yellow Ribbon ceremonies to

                                               PUBLIC HEARING
           PUBLIC HEARING TO RECEIVE COMMENT WITH REGARD TO THE PROPOSED
           ISSUANCE OF UP TO $65,000,000 OF SUBORDINATED TRANSPORTATION SALES
           TAX REVENUE BONDS (Public Hearing set April 21, 2015)
Commissioner Ellertson invited members of the public with comments regarding the proposed issuance of Subordinated
Transportation Sales Tax Revenue Bonds to approach the bench.

Lindon resident Michael Terry expressed concern that people have been misinformed of the differences between general
obligation bonds and special obligation bonds in relation to the proposed Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) project.
He noted the assumption that Utah County will be obligated to pay for a $65,000,000 bond issue is incorrect, explaining
that a general obligation debt is similar to a promissory note or individual mortgage, while a special obligation bond is
for a specific purpose and can only be paid by a specific pledged source. Mr. Terry pointed out Utah County currently
has no outstanding general obligation debt, which should be a source of comfort for citizens as taxes will not need to be
raised for repayment. He stated funding for BRT would come only from the sales tax proceeds as determined by the
special obligation bond, reiterating that BRT will not cause a raise in taxes.

Erik Watkins, Provo resident, stated the county will be spending less money by planning for infrastructure now instead
of waiting another ten or fifteen years as Utah County continues to grow. The proposed Bus Rapid Transit also provides
potential for a light rail system, offering multiple choices for people who choose to or choose not to drive. Mr. Watkins
noted this option is less costly than knocking down buildings in order to expand roads; mass transit is a necessity.

Provo resident David Harding referred to a proverb that “the best time to plant a tree is twenty years ago; the second best
time is now.” Since it is not possible to go back twenty years to build an efficient transit system, the best option for Utah
County is to begin now. Mr. Harding noted the recent completion of the Interstate-15 core reconstruction project which
is predicted to accommodate traffic for only fifteen more years; he hopes that we will not look back in twenty years and
lament that we did not take the opportunity to begin building a transit system designed to make the roads more efficient.

Greg Sanchez is a Brigham Young University student who plans to stay in Utah after graduation. Originally from the
East Coast where public transportation is abundant but there is no space to expand, Mr. Sanchez sees the value in a
community where space and funds are currently available to begin a Bus Rapid Transit system. He personally plans to
ride a bicycle and use the university bus as a student, and will continue to use Utah Transit Authority and public
transportation when finished with school.

Mary Ann Nielsen of the Utah Bicycling Club started a chapter in Utah County to promote health and fitness. There is
an obstacle in getting people of all fitness and ability levels to participate, which would be improved by having more
space and safer space on the roads for bicycles. Ms. Nielsen explained how cyclists are encouraged to use the entire
street and middle of the road; she is worried about informing the general public about this change for cyclists along the
BRT route. She also sees wasted space of grass between islands and roads that could be better used for bike lanes, and
asked the Board to consider parking issues while altering the roads - it would be nice to ride without fretting over car
doors opening into the path and causing accidents. Vehicle exhaust and options to get around Utah Valley for
recreational bike rides are also concerns.

Provo resident Sherrie Hall-Everett is in support of the proposed Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit project. She repeated
the earlier point that BRT funding comes with no new tax increase, and was approved by a vote of the people in 2006
indicating portions of a sales tax revenue should go to mass transit. The citizens of Provo and Orem pay 44% of the sales
tax in Utah County which has allowed the wise expansion of road systems including Pioneer Crossing, SR-92, and North
County Boulevard. Provo residents have spent $270,000,000 on roads throughout the county which they may only drive
occasionally, proving that not every individual must personally use a road in order to help take care of it. Ms. Hall-
Everett clarified there is not a single part of the road system which is not subsidized. The Mountainland Association of
Governments has been tasked with transportation for decades; BRT is the next key step for progress. Ms. Hall-Everett
stated a petition being circulated in opposition to BRT is being misrepresented to the public, making the process unfair.
She closed her comments with the note that providing transportation infrastructure is an important part of government,
and encouraged the commissioners to continue with the vision that will improve quality of life.

Brigham Young University student Alec Barton has lived in Utah most of his life. He thanked Commissioner Ellertson
for mentioning Envision Utah earlier in the meeting, also encouraging residents to take the online survey. Mr. Barton
believes Utah and Bus Rapid Transit are about vision and making the best use of resources for decades to come.
Investment in a transportation system to accommodate the expected population growth in Utah County is a necessity.
Comments at previous meetings have declared the opinions of students should not be given as much importance since
they are not permanent residents, but Mr. Barton pointed out that if he leaves, another student will take his place. BRT
is a community resource; if it benefits students, it benefits the county and region at large.

Jamie Littlefield is a Provo homeowner in favor of Bus Rapid Transit. The difference in quality for public transportation
between Salt Lake County and Utah County is noticeable; if Utah County does not accept these funds from the federal
government to improve the situation, our own money will be sent and spent elsewhere, probably to help a community
not even within this state. Ms. Littlefield repeated that residents voted on use of the funds and it is not a new tax. Provo
and Orem are entitled to benefits as much as any other cities, and mass transit will help correct poor air quality. While
there may be flaws in the plan, the county must act now or lose the opportunity entirely. Ms. Littlefield looks to BRT
as a reason not to move to Salt Lake City, and asks the commissioners to please not reject our own tax dollars coming
back into Utah County.

Susan Krueger-Barber of the Joaquin Improvement Association stated her neighborhood needs Bus Rapid Transit. She
does not ride the bus anymore because the hassle of carrying groceries and children is too great; BRT, on the other hand,
will be stroller-friendly and Ms. Krueger-Barber would prefer to use public transit to avoid polluting the air. The system
will be beneficial for university students, and will also increase home values.

Orem resident James Fawcett acknowledged the comments in favor of public transportation, but counters that it is already
in place. Utah Transit Authority bus route 830 runs every fifteen minutes and is sufficient for current community needs.
He questioned whether ridership has increased in the eleven years since discussion began regarding Bus Rapid Transit.
This project will cost $150,000,000 of federal and county funds, which is pure silliness when considering the other
transportation uses for this money in Utah County. Mr. Fawcett stated it would be unfair to commit such a large amount
to one route. There are already public transit options throughout Provo and Orem while other parts of the county are
neglected; this argument is about putting the funds into this specific corridor. Mr. Fawcett noted the system would mostly
be used for the convenience of young students and missionaries who are already capable of walking to bus stops; truly
efficient rapid transit would instead serve University Parkway and not have a dedicated lane. Mr. Fawcett stated this
project is overkill, and the referendum to collect signatures in opposition is unnecessarily long.

Rachel Whipple is a Provo resident living in the Maeser neighborhood. She loves using public transportation whenever
possible, in places such as Salt Lake City or Portland, Oregon and Washington D.C. In those locations, mass transit is
intuitive and easy to use; it is much easier to hop on Trax or the Metro than to figure out a bus route in an unfamiliar city.
Comparing BRT to busses is comparing apples to oranges. Ms. Whipple pointed out how Bus Rapid Transit would serve
many in the community, including students, children, individuals with disabilities and low-income families. Not everyone
can legally or financially drive a car, and we also cannot afford to continue hurting the air quality. The existing
infrastructure has been planned around the BRT project; many streets are in need of repair, and road maintenance is
directly linked to approval of BRT. Ms. Whipple encouraged the Board to use BRT as an economic driver, making the
community a place where businesses want to come because their employees want to live and work here. She also spoke
of the benefit to youth, including her own son who is not interested in driving because of the options available through
public transportation.

Brigham Young University student Jeffrey Jarvis has experienced transportation systems on a global level, and has seen
great improvements to transit in Utah in the eight years he has lived here. The Provo/Orem BRT will also have a positive
impact on the surrounding communities; many people who commute will be able to utilize it. Mr. Jarvis noted it is
important to prepare now for the future, mentioning the BRT project in Salt Lake (M-35 route) which functions very well.

Commissioner Lee stepped down from the bench in order to make comment as a member of the public. He asked Utah
County Clerk/Auditor Bryan E. Thompson whether any funds have been allocated from the quarter-cent sales tax for the
SR-92 project. Mr. Thompson responded he was unsure and would have to check, but believes the answer is yes.
Commissioner Lee clarified that the voters in 2006 approved the second quarter-cent sales tax through an opinion poll;
that is not the same fund paying for this Provo/Orem Bus Rapid Transit project bond. BRT would be sourced through
the third quarter-cent sales tax. The third quarter-cent sales tax came into place in 2009. Commissioner Lee stated, “We
are talking about a new sales tax; it is going to be the fourth quarter - the county option.” If the third quarter-cent sales
tax was not effectively filled out, the discussion would be different in talking about the fourth quarter new tax that is on
the way. He reiterated that although this project does not promote a new tax, in essence it is promoting the thought for
the future of possibly a new tax.

Commissioner Graves also stepped down from the bench in order to make comment as a member of the public. He
learned last week that the University Parkway intersection is the third busiest in the entire state. The Provo/Orem Bus
Rapid Transit addresses that intersection, which would be a standstill without this project. Commissioner Graves talked
about the time value of money from a business perspective to emphasize the importance of beginning this project now
instead of in ten years, explaining that the current cost of transportation for roads is increasing at eight percent annually.
On a $150,000,000 project, that is roughly $12,000,000 at current rates - the cost of waiting can be much greater.
Commissioner Graves noted Utah County can buy bonds right now at less than book rates, which may make it possible
to purchase a $65,000,000 with a return of $61,000,000 based on the market. Lastly, he clarified that the question on
the referendum petition against Bus Rapid Transit is not to stop BRT: the question only addresses the proposed
parameters of the bond. The BRT project was entered into through an interlocal agency agreement on December 2, 2014
by the previous Board of Commissioners, before both he and Commissioner Lee were members of the Board.
