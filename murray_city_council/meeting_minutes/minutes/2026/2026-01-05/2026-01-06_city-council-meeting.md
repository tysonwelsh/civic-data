Public Hearing:
      Consider an ordinance enacting Section 17.76.165 of the Murray City Municipal Code relating to
      residential ponds. Community & Economic Development Director Chad Wilkinson recapped about why the
      potential ordinance came about. He reminded the Council that the only frequent standard used by many
      cities, was to require a building permit for ponds deeper than 24 inches holding 5,000 gallons of water. He
      explained that staff initially modeled the draft ordinance after Herriman City, but later learned Herriman
      had discontinued its pond ordinance when discovering the 24-inch, 5,000-gallon standard applied only to
      swimming pools, not ponds.

      Mr. Wilkinson noted the frequent standard in the original proposed ordinance as Standard E. He explained
      that when the Murray PC (Planning Commission) received the updated information showing that swimming
      pool standards were being applied to ponds, they voted 4–1 to recommend removing Standard E. He said
      staff fully supported the modification and clarified that the only no vote opposed adopting a pond
      ordinance. The modified ordinance was displayed with Standard E stricken from the Code, basic standards
      remained like requiring a liner, having a minimum setback and requiring a permit for an electrical pump.
      Other guidelines related to fish and mosquitoes were noted and Mr. Wilkinson said the modified ordinance
      would basically codify what the City had already been requiring for years.

      Mr. Bullen asked about a fencing requirement and expressed concern about people constructing ponds in
      a haphazard way. Mr. Wilkinson used photos of public and residential ponds to show that Murray has never
      required pond fencing. He noted that fencing small front-yard ponds would conflict with the existing fence
      code, explained that pond permitting would raise costs due to hiring third-party soil engineers and he
      confirmed that City engineers and building officials agreed there was no significant reason to inspect ponds
      because ponds have no structure to inspect.

       Mr. Hock said he voted as he did because the City had very few pond related issues in its history, he was
       hesitant to move the ordinance forward especially after Standard E was removed and he felt the ordinance
       would not add much to what was already common practice and that requiring building permits for
       residential ponds would be a burden to residents.
