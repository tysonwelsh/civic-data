   4. PUBLIC COMMENTS

Tracy Gibson addressed the council, representing the Salt Lake County Aging Adult Services.
During her comments, she expressed the goal of expanding the Meals on Wheels program in
Magna by recruiting local volunteers. She emphasized the significant impact that community
involvement can have, explaining that when community members deliver meals, it helps
combat isolation among elderly residents. She shared insights into the program's structure,
including the volunteer requirements, which entail a time commitment of approximately an hour
per week if there were five volunteers, or as little as one day a month if 20 volunteers were
involved. Gibson outlined the specific route in Magna, which runs along 3500 South from 7200
to 8400 South, north of 3500 South, serving around 10 individuals who receive meals. She
shared a poignant statistic highlighting the detrimental effect of isolation on seniors, equating it
to the health impact of smoking 14 cigarettes a day. Additionally, she recounted her personal
connection to Magna, having worked at the Magna Senior Center and expressed her love for
the community. She mentioned having posters and flyers available and inquired if any
businesses or places recommended by the city would be willing to display them.

   5. STAKEHOLDER REPORTS
      A. Unified Police Department

Lieutenant Shane Manwaring reviewed a prepared presentation and handout (Attachments A
and B) on behalf of acting UPD Chief Del Craig, who was on leave, and went on to discuss the
recent developments in crime statistics for Magna. He explained the role of NCIC codes, which
are part of the National Crime Information Center established by the FBI in 1967 as a database
initially containing five files: wanted persons, stolen articles, vehicles, license plates, and guns.
The NCIC now includes hundreds, possibly thousands, of files. He explained that law
enforcement officers categorize incidents as either short forms, like stopping to help a motorist
or answering questions, or long forms, which require detailed reports. In efforts to improve
accuracy in crime statistics, officers have been trained to provide specific NCIC codes even for
short-form incidents. This training initiative began around November of the previous year, with
the expectation that better classification would lead to clearer statistics, avoiding generic
categories like public order, which can distort data interpretation. In presenting the crime
statistics, Lt. Manwaring noted that burglaries had increased by six from the previous year's
number, bringing that to ten incidents relative to four. He analyzed this increase, remarking
that although six might seem substantial, the overall numbers remain low compared to
surrounding cities. He recommended that residents leave their front lights on during the night
and consider installing cameras as deterrents to help lower the burglary rates. He emphasized
that one of the best preventive measures involves residents watching out for their neighbors
and reporting any suspicious activity. Additionally, incidents of damaged property were slightly
up, primarily attributed to graffiti. The police department has made significant progress in
identifying repeat offenders, resulting in several arrests. To prevent vandalism by minors, the
department adopted a zero-tolerance policy last summer for curfew violations, issuing citations
to those found in violation to curb nighttime criminal activities. He provided detailed reports on

      A. My Hometown Initiative – Mark and Jana Rupp

Alisha Cartier introduced Mark and Jana Rupp to present on the My Hometown Initiative. Mark
Rupp, along with his wife Jana, provided details about the initiative, which is currently active in
West Valley City, with the aim to improve community engagement and address various needs
through interfaith cooperation.

Mark Rupp reviewed their prepared presentation (Attachment C) and shared a key element of
the initiative is building leadership in homes, churches, workplaces, and the community. Faith
group leaders can recommend individuals to become block captains, enabling citizens to
emerge as leaders.

Craig Thomas, an employee of West Vally City, shared the example of the organization of
National Night Out block parties, which have significantly increased since the initiative's
inception. He noted that such events have more than doubled, from prior numbers to
approximately twenty additional block parties, demonstrating the initiative's power in fostering
community engagement. Local churches play a crucial role as community resource centers,
providing classes on diverse subjects based on community needs identified by faith group
leaders.

Mr. Rupp pointed out that these faith leaders not only highlight the community's problems but
also tap individuals within their congregations who can teach necessary classes. Regular
monthly meetings at West Valley City Hall among interfaith council members facilitate the
realization of such community improvements, showcasing a collaborative spirit that is both
resourceful and supportive. The initiative also unifies and beautifies neighborhoods through
days of service. Projects include helping elderly individuals and others who may struggle to

      B. Consideration of an Ordinance (OAM2024-001295) Amending Sections 14.12.060,
         Sidewalks; 18.12.050, Lots; 19.50.060, Landscaping of Park Strips; 18.14.080,
         Street Lighting; Clarifying Language on Pavement, Turf, Plants and Ground Cover,
         Street Lighting Requirements, and Trees in Park Strips – Brian Tucker, Planning
         Manager

Brian Tucker presented the proposed ordinance amending sections related to sidewalks, lots,
landscaping of park strips, and street lighting requirements. The ordinance aimed to address
several ongoing issues regarding street design and maintenance in Magna City. Key points of
the proposed changes included:
   • Increasing Sidewalk Width: The ordinance proposed increasing the sidewalk width to
       5 feet while maintaining a 5-foot park strip. This adjustment was intended to enhance
       pedestrian accessibility and uniformity in Magna’s neighborhoods.
   • Masonry Fences on Corner Lots: Developers would be required to install decorative
       masonry fences on corner lots. This requirement aimed to improve aesthetic appeal and
       uniformity while maintaining visibility and safety at intersections.
   • Clarification on Park Strip Paving Rules: The ordinance sought to clarify the rules for
       paving park strips. Currently, park strips can only be paved under certain conditions,
       such as when adjacent to major streets that separate the homes from the street or when
       less than a specified width. The proposal would require specific patterns and colors for
       the stamped concrete used in these cases. An existing requirement for at least 30% live
       plant material in park strips would be maintained, with restrictions on the use of turf
       unless the park strip is sufficiently wide.

   •   Street Tree Installation and Maintenance: The ordinance specified new requirements
       for the installation and maintenance of street trees. These would include conditions for
       using trees with low water use, vase-like growth habits, and minimal root disturbance to
       enhance viability in park strips, especially under power lines.
   •   Street Light Placement and Spacing: The proposal detailed specific placement and
       spacing for street lights, varying based on the street type. This included residential,
       industrial, and commercial zones, ensuring optimal lighting for safety and visibility.

Mr. Tucker said the Planning Commission recommended allowing more flexibility for
homeowners in R1 and R2 zones with respect to paving options for their park strips. They
suggested that residents in these zones could have more leeway in choosing paving materials,
provided that overall aesthetic and safety guidelines are adhered to.

During the discussion, council members raised concerns about elements like rock sizes in park
strips and the use of weed barriers. Some council members emphasized the potential issues
with medium-sized rocks, which could pose safety risks, and stressed the importance of weed
barriers to prevent unsightly growth. The discussion also highlighted the need for separate
requirements for residential versus collector and arterial streets to accommodate their different
functionalities and maintenance needs.

Mayor Barney proposed bifurcating the code, suggesting separate guidelines for park strips on
residential streets as opposed to collectors and arterials, which often fall under city
maintenance. This would ensure clarity and suitability of regulations based on differing street
types and uses.

Mr. Tucker agreed to provide an updated version of the ordinance, incorporating these
suggestions and concerns, for the council to review before the next meeting. He aimed to
ensure that the new regulations would effectively address the current issues while
accommodating the needs and preferences of Magna’s residents.

       C. Discuss Current Code for Magna City’s Abatement Process – David Brickey, City
          Manager

David Brickey presented issues with the current abatement process, highlighting a specific
case where a property has required three abatements in less than 12 months, costing the city
approximately $12,000. This example of a troubling property has underscored numerous
challenges for Magna in managing repeated violations effectively. He detailed how the
property, inhabited by a tenant and owned by the tenant's girlfriend, necessitated substantial
intervention by the city. Despite recurring abatements costing the city upwards of $12,000, the
problematic behavior persisted due to the tenants stripping items for recyclable metals and
piling trash in the yard. He proposed that the existing due process timeline should be adjusted
for instances of repeated violations. Given that current procedures allow for a potentially
lengthy abatement process, extending well over two months from the initial complaint to court
proceedings, he emphasized the need for a more expedient timeline. To this end, he posed a
question to the council about what they deem an appropriate threshold for enacting shortened



    Magna City Council • Webster Community Center • 8952 W Magna Main Street • Magna, UT 84044
timelines, suggesting thresholds like two abatements within six months. He also introduced the
idea of adding abatement costs directly to the property tax bill or potentially imposing a lien on
the property to ensure reimbursement for the expenditures. This approach aims to address the
financial burden on the city while compelling property owners to take corrective actions more
seriously.

During council discussions, members explored the possibility of notifying mortgage companies
of ongoing violations, the considerations surrounding a property's history when evaluating
future violations, and the challenge of balancing due process with addressing frequent
offenders. It was noted that without addressing the core issues, recurrent violations might
persist, further straining city resources and patience with the offending properties.

Council Member Sudbury inquired about best practices and whether three instances of
abatement are generally considered the norm. In response, Mr. Brickey suggested a more
stringent approach, advocating for a timeline that would allow quicker enforcement after at
least two of the same violations over six months. He stressed the importance of being able to
address such issues promptly to prevent them from becoming long-term nuisances affecting
neighborhood quality and property values. By modifying the abatement process to be more
proactive and reducing the waiting periods, he hopes to enhance the community's environment
and ensure a fair, efficient process that effectively curbs recurring violations. Through these
proposed changes, he aims to bring about a more livable Magna by systematically tackling
problem properties, and he agreed to bring back detailed recommendations formulated from
the council's feedback for further consideration.

       D. Discuss Communities That Care (CTC) and Magna City Memorandum of
          Understanding – Trish Hull, Council Member

Council Member Hull introduced a proposed memorandum of understanding (MOU) between
Communities That Care (CTC) and Magna City, aiming to establish a clear understanding
between the two entities regarding the fiscal management and oversight of the grant funding
that CTC receives. A central focus of the MOU is to delineate the roles and responsibilities of
the city and the coalition in managing these grants. The key points of the MOU include:
   • Clarifying Roles: The document sets out to clearly define the city's responsibilities as
        the financial agent for the coalition, in accordance with the requirements of the grant.
        Mayor Barney emphasized that Magna City is identified as the fiscal agent for the CTC
        coalition, responsible for receiving, holding, and disbursing the funding earmarked for
        CTC activities. This role obligates the city to adhere to the stipulations outlined in the
        grant funding agreements, ensuring that funds are used strictly for their designated
        purposes.
   • Authorizing Spending Approval: It proposes that the coalition chair be empowered to
        approve spending within the coalition, a change designed to reduce the volume of
        routine financial approvals required from the mayor, thereby streamlining the approval
        process. Council Member Hull explained that up until recently, spending approvals had
        been channeled directly through Mayor Barney, which created a bottleneck in financial




    Magna City Council • Webster Community Center • 8952 W Magna Main Street • Magna, UT 84044
       management. The shifts in responsibility aim to distribute the workload more evenly
       while ensuring accountability remains within the coalition structure.
   •   Financial Oversight and Audit Process: The MOU outlines a robust financial
       oversight process, including the establishment of a fiscal committee and provisions for
       annual audits. The fiscal committee would comprise the coalition chair, two
       coordinators, financial representatives such as Stewart Okobia and Daniel Hoffman
       from the MSD, and potentially county representatives, tasked with reviewing the
       coalition's books and approving financial actions. Annually, an audit would be conducted
       to ensure transparency, address compliance with the grant's conditions, and allow the
       city to continually monitor and manage any emerging issues.

The council discussed the proposed changes, specifically focusing on the approval process,
the established checks and balances, and the implications for the city's liability.

Council Member Sudbury raised concerns about the checks and balances in place to prevent
potential mismanagement of funds, highlighting the importance of maintaining oversight to
safeguard against misuse.

Mayor Barney addressed these concerns by clarifying that while the coalition chair would have
approval authority, a fiscal committee and regular oversight mechanisms would remain firmly
in place. The city would continue to play a critical oversight role, reinforced by detailed
spreadsheets tracking all expenditure possibilities, further ensuring that it remains aligned with
the grant's stipulations.

Council members also expressed their commitment to ensuring that, should any issues arise,
the MOU and the processes it describes would be revisited and adjusted as necessary to
maintain integrity and proper use of funds. The finalization of the MOU remained contingent
upon legal review by City Attorney Paul Ashton, who will continue working to refine the
document and address any outstanding concerns.

      A. Discussion and Potential Action Regarding 2025 Municipal Election – Diana Baun,
         City Recorder

Diana Baun presented detailed information regarding the 2025 municipal election, highlighting
the available options between ranked-choice voting and the traditional primary and general
election format. During the discussion she provided key points, such as the costs associated
with each election method and the financial implications. She noted that for ranked-choice
voting, a fixed cost would be shared among participating cities. As of the meeting, Salt Lake
City, South Salt Lake, Millcreek, and Cottonwood Heights were confirmed to be using ranked-
choice voting, potentially influencing Magna's decision through shared expense distribution.
The deadline for deciding on ranked-choice voting was emphasized as April 1, 2025,
necessitating a prompt decision from the council.

Council Member Prokopis noted a critical component was the potential need for voter
education to ensure the public understands the ranked-choice voting process.

Ms. Baun acknowledged that despite the seemingly straightforward nature of ranked-choice
voting, many voters face challenges in comprehending how to appropriately rank candidates,
which can potentially lead to erroneous ballots. Council members considered the implications
of such misunderstandings and the importance of allocating funds for educational campaigns
to raise voter awareness and correct ballot completion.

Additionally, the conversation touched on changes to the city's form of government that would
affect the upcoming election. It was noted that the new election would include the election of
district seats 2 and 4 and a mayor at large, conforming to recently enacted legislative changes.
Council members expressed an interest in gathering more information about other cities'
decisions regarding ranked-choice voting to better understand their collective experiences and
outcomes.

Council Member Sudbury expressed a desire to see if other cities would join in ranked-choice
voting, thereby potentially altering cost projections. The discussion culminated in a decision to
delay the voting method choice until the next council meeting to gather further information and
assess developments among peer cities.

       B. Discussion and Potential Action Regarding Appropriate Conditions to Consider
          Vacating Dora Street – Paul Ashton, City Attorney

Paul Ashton presented information on the potential vacation of Dora Street, detailing several
critical components for the council to consider. He explained the legal requirements for
vacating a street or road, emphasizing that adequate consideration must be paid for the
property in question. Paul highlighted the appraisal, which valued the property at an amount
between $225,000 and $270,000, a necessary figure for the city to contemplate should they
decide to vacate the road. In addition, he noted that potential conditions for the vacation of the
street could include the mandatory purchase of the property. One practical consideration
discussed was putting the property on the open market, although it seemed evident that
interest would primarily be from the surrounding property owners, including the school district
and a developer who initially petitioned for the street's vacation. This was confirmed as
developers were informed of the appraisal findings, which positioned them for potential
negotiations.

Council members engaged in discussions regarding the implications of vacating the street and
touched upon potential development issues tied to the area. Mr. Ashton reiterated that the
council must determine whether vacating Dora Street would materially injure the public interest
or any individual, per statutory requirements. He also raised awareness of the importance of
establishing time limits on any conditions placed upon the vacation to avoid future
complications, as seen in other municipalities. He pointed out that while proof of development
feasibility, like access to water and sewer services, is crucial, it should be left between the
property owners and Magna Water Improvement District; a suggestion aimed to ensure the city
remained outside of utility negotiations.

Council Member Sudbury moved to deny the vacation of Dora Street. Council Member
Pierce seconded the motion; vote was 4-1, motion passes with a nay vote from Council
Member Prokopis.
