20. APPROVE AND AUTHORIZE A RESOLUTION AMENDING SECTIONS VI.A.3 AND IX.A1.A OF
       THE UTAH COUNTY HUMAN RESOURCE RULES AND REGULATIONS
(Track A, 18:30)
Utah County Deputy Attorney Robert J. Moore noted he was asked to draft this resolution to clarify how county
elected officials, as well as the County Commission Office staff, are not required to submit documentation of hours
worked each pay period. They also are not subject to accrue and spend sick leave or vacation time. Commissioner
Lee explained elected officials, and the at-will employed Commission Office staff, are performance-based
employees; being salaried, they cannot be paid less than the set amount for a forty-hour work-week. Commissioner
Graves stated he takes issue with filling in a weekly paper showing he has worked 8:00 A.M. to 5:00 P.M. daily
when those are not necessarily the hours worked, as demonstrated during the Memorial Day holiday wherein he and
other elected officials were engaged in official county business. They also discussed statutory requirements to show
time, value, and money allocated.

Commissioner Ivie stated he has reservations with exempting specific individuals and positions from the policy
when it can be argued that there are additional similar positions within other offices in the county. The
commissioners discussed the capabilities of the new Human Resources Information System, which can adjust
payroll as needed. Commissioner Graves pointed out current policy already defines the ten county elected officials
as being exempt from accruing vacation and sick leave as well as other benefits granted to appointed employees, but
does not clearly define the parameters. Commissioner Graves suggested eliminating ‘Commission staff’ and any
references of those individuals and positions from the proposed resolution so that it only applies to elected officials.

22. APPROVE AND AUTHORIZE AN AGREEMENT WITH MCGRATH HUMAN RESOURCES
       GROUP FOR A COMPENSATION STUDY
(Track A, 29:25)
Utah County Deputy Attorney Robert J. Moore summarized this agreement with McGrath Human Resources is
based exclusively on the county request for proposals (RFP) and responses. The price was also negotiated to match
a competing bid, with the potential reimbursement of travel not to exceed the difference of $3,000 from McGrath’s
original proposal.

The commissioners addressed a sentence in the contract which read ‘Utah County competes with comparable public
employers along the Wasatch Front and other public employers as designated with the Utah County Office of
Human Resource Management and in consultation with the Utah County Board of Commissioners, and the survey
should sample comparable positions as so designated.’ Commissioner Lee stated he would like to remove the
reference to ‘along the Wasatch Front,’ and the words “in consultation.” When Commissioner Lee pointed out the
Board of Commissioners ultimately makes the decision rather than serves as consultants, Robert J. Moore explained
statute technically designates Human Resources as the administrator of the study. The Board can choose to approve,
reject, or amend and act based on the study results, but it is a Human Resources project. Commissioner Graves
countered it is pointless to engage in a study without involving the Board; it would be a waste of money to move
forward because if the Board is not included in the process, as he will vote “no” on the results.

Commissioner Ivie agreed the reference to employers along the Wasatch Front should be stricken, and summarized
being “in consultation” with the Board would be in compliance with statute while allowing the commissioners to
have flexibility with the Utah County Human Resources (HR) Management Director and be involved in gathering
information for the study. Commissioner Graves noted several points of personal disagreement with the HR
Director, stating several times that he will not vote for anything that does not heavily involve the Board. He
referenced messages between HR and the Commission Office, and Robert J. Moore clarified the HR Director fully
intends to include the Board throughout the entire process. Commissioners Lee and Graves reiterated their desire to
be involved, with Commissioner Lee pointing out that results can be manipulated to provide an outcome if not
guided correctly; the Board needs to be there for critical decisions. They discussed contract language that would
appease statute with Mr. Moore.

2.     PRESENTATION ON PROSECUTORIAL REVIEW
(Track B, 08:00)
Connor Boyack, President of Libertas Institute and Utah County resident, came forward to discuss a resolution
which had been presented to the Board at a previous session (Public Comments recorded during the May 9, 2017
Commission meeting). Mr. Boyack noted critical responses to the proposed Restorative Justice Commission, with
individuals claiming it is a personal or political program. Mr. Boyack is speaking today instead of any who
presented during that earlier meeting because his only interest is answering this question: “What happens when the
justice system becomes a tool of injustice? What is your duty as elected officials?”

Connor Boyack stated prosecutor misconduct being a major concern for the public. He talked about Michael
Morton, a man in Texas who was sentenced to life in prison during the 1980’s for the brutal murder of his wife. Mr.
Morton maintained his innocence and it was eventually discovered that the prosecutor in that case withheld
evidence; Mr. Morton was released after 25 years of unjust incarceration. The case made international headlines
because the prosecutor was sentenced to ten days in jail, a $500 fine, and 500 hours of community service – a “slap
on the wrist” compared to what Mr. Morton endured.

Connor Boyack talked about the significant authority given to prosecutors, which should come with accountability.
He reviewed the proposed resolution to create the Restorative Justice Commission, emphasizing this would only
apply to closed cases so as not to impede with any ongoing investigations. Mr. Boyack also talked about the
differences between mistakes and misconduct. The existence of the Restorative Justice Commission itself would
serve as a deterrent, and is solely an advisory board to the County Commissioners – they are there to help the Board
do a good job as administrators of the county. Mr. Boyack and the commissioners discussed the purpose of the
Restorative Justice Commission, including financial compensation. They also talked about using elections and
legislation as a recourse, and the need for checks and balances within the government.

(Track B, 43:55)
Utah County Deputy Attorney Tim Taylor questioned if this proposal is possibly a “solution in search of a problem.”
The County Attorney’s Office receives approximately 3,500 felony cases per year, meaning there have been roughly
35,000 cases to be screened through the office in the last decade. The public is not aware of the hundreds of cases
that are declined from the start – the attorneys have standards for reasonable chance of success in going to trial. Mr.
Taylor also pointed out there are already oversights for attorneys in Utah; this year alone the bar is budgeting
$1,500,000 to investigate any allegations of misconduct.

Tim Taylor addressed immunity versus accountability with the commissioners, and the repercussions of causing
prosecuting attorneys to second-guess themselves. They discussed the court process for the State Attorney
General’s Office, the Utah County Attorney’s Office measures taken to prevent errors, and the need to build public
confidence through open communication. Commissioner Graves also commented on employees feeling entitled to
their jobs, suggesting there are two specific individuals in the County Attorney’s Office he would choose to fire
immediately. Mr. Taylor noted the idea of this Restorative Justice Commission is being driven by one individual
with a personal vendetta. Mr. Taylor is not opposed to receiving feedback, but wants to be involved in the
conversation.
