City Planner Maloy reviewed accessory structure amendments. He explained they were discussed multiple
places in City Code. The current amendment Ordinance was initiated to consolidate all accessory structure
information into one chapter. Ideally, the new provisions would provide clear, easily understood standards.
Residents with unique lots have expressed frustration with seemingly simple requests not being allowed by the
City. Prescribed standards were difficult to apply to unique lots. Standard requests may be allowed with staff
approval. The draft included an exception allowing staff the ability to refer non-standard requests to Planning
Commission.

Commissioner Jacobson expressed the opinion City standards should be reduced for accessory structures. He
thought distances between structures, and setback requirements should be reduced. Required separation of
homes from barns and animal stalls should be removed from agricultural lots. The only standards he thought
the city should enforce were height restrictions, and larger structures should match the primary dwelling.
Commissioner Ferguson mentioned fire codes may require three feet between structures. City Planner Maloy
offered to research best practices including fire code requirements. Commissioner Garcia was in favor of a
three-foot minimum to allow maintenance access and prevent roof lines from crossing property lines.
Commissioner Bradford agreed with Commissioner Garcia’s comment. Commissioner Palmer thought most
requests could be reviewed and approved by staff, and exceptions only should need Commission review.
Commissioners agreed a separate chapter for accessory structures was needed. City Planner Maloy mentioned
sending letters to neighbors when a non-standard request was submitted. If feedback was received and issues
were not resolved, Planning Commission review could be requested. Commissioners responded affirmatively
to such a process. City Planner Maloy would conduct research and bring the information back for further
discussion at a work meeting.



City Planner Maloy explained Council requested examples of flag lot regulation issues and requested
recommendations for specific streets and circumstances that would allow them, with conditions Current
ordinance did not allow more than one, and they were not allowed on collector streets. A specific subdivision
lost two lots due to the requirement, even though it met density standards. Another subdivision was denied by
Council due to zoning. Splitting into two separate subdivisions would allow four buildable lots but would add
four access points. Allowing shared access for flag lots would reduce access points on the collector road. The
city classified both major and minor connector streets. He asked the Commission if they would consider
changing the requirement to allow flag lots on minor collector roads. All flag lots required full white, vinyl
fencing, even if a precast wall was already in place. As such, modification of the fencing requirement may be
beneficial.
City Planner Maloy reviewed the following potential reasons for and against flag lots.

        Reasons in favor of flag lots:
    •   Improved City Revenues for services through property taxes
    •   Increased population, which increases indirect revenue sources
    •   Increased Private property value through development potential
    •   Fostered preservation and sustainability

        Reasons against flag lots:
    •   Discouraged property accumulation and subdivision development
    •   Changed historic or agricultural use of private property
    •   Potential privacy impacts
    •   Increased vehicular traffic, congestion, and emissions

City Planner Maloy informed the Commission some communities were in favor of flag lots, and he displayed
their regulations. Of those reviewed Herriman was the most restrictive. He requested feedback.

Commissioner Jacobson explained he thought the original intent of flag lot fencing requirements was for vinyl
fences as a minimum, and precast walls should be allowed. He was opposed to allowing two flag lots in a new
development. He expressed the opinion dual access onto major collector roads was problematic. Dual access
could possibly be considered on some minor collector roads. He did not see benefit to removing the half acre
requirement. Commissioner Rypien agreed allowance, with conditions, on some minor collectors may be
worthy of consideration.

Planning Manager Clint Spencer reviewed the request to increase architectural design standards for multi-family
homes. Larger buildings would be required to incorporate more positive design elements than smaller buildings.
The following provisions were proposed with the amendment:

    •   Increase brick, rock, and stone to 40% for the front, façade, and 20% on all other facades.
    •   Require standards that previously only applicable to the R-M zone to R-2-10, R-2-15, and MU zones.
        MU-2 zones design standards are typically addressed in an MDA.
    •   Require variations in wall planes on a building. Minim 2’ per 25’ linear feet of building.
    •   Require additional building types as developments get larger:
            o For developments less than 2 acres: 1 building type
            o Between 2-4 acres: 2 building types

The four types of recommended building types were garden units, owner occupied stacked units, townhomes,
or apartments. The proposal was compliant with 10-5-8(E).

Chair Berbert opened the public hearing.

No comments were offered.

Chair Berbert closed the public hearing.

Commissioner Garcia expressed the opinion brick, rock or stone requirements should be closer to 80 percent,
due to the way they were calculated, and they should be higher on the sides of the buildings as well.
Commissioner Jacobson agreed the requirement should be a higher percentage and expressed it should apply to
all sides of the building, in addition, single family and multi-family should have the same requirement. City

Planning Manager Clint Spencer reviewed the request recommendation of approval to amend manufacturing
zones pertaining to uses and establishment of the M-2 Zone. The City currently includes two manufacturing
zones, the M-1 and T-M zone which encompass two percent of the overall acreage of the City. There had been
interest from the City to designate areas of higher manufacturing intensities as some have recently located
adjacent to other incompatible uses. The proposed ordinance would create the M-2 zone, and remove more
impactful uses from the M-1 zone, while adding those uses to the proposed M-2 zone. As the City continues to
grow, additional uses may be requested, and adding the M-2 zone would help accommodate those uses in an
appropriate area.

The proposed amendment does not apply the M-2 zone to any property in the City, it creates the opportunity
for such rezoning to take place. All existing uses in the M-1 zone would be allowed to continue to operate as
legally established, however, if a use category (Permitted, Conditional, Not Permitted) has changed with this
proposal, those uses would be considered as legal non-conforming uses. Staff researched and reviewed other
city ordinances which have both an M-1 and M-2 zone to help determine which uses would be appropriate in
each zone.

Chair Berbert noticed hotels were allowed in M-1 and T-M but not in M-2. M-2 was the only zone allowing

City Planner Maloy informed the Commission Herriman City did not prohibit the use or reuse of metal shipping
containers, and staff often received inquiries about them. The city treated them as accessory structures. As such,
they are required to have color and materials compatible with the single-family home. Modification to meet the
standard reduced the financial benefit. The draft ordinance did not allow conversion of a shipping container as
an accessory dwelling unit, but would allow reuse for accessory storage or habitable space such as a detached
home office, craft room, or “she shed”—among other permitted land uses

City staff often receives inquiries from residents about the reuse of metal shipping containers as an accessory
use. Shipping containers are structurally sound, waterproof, and often economical for reuse. Generally, shipping
containers have been reused for storage, but more recently they have been reused for residential and commercial
purposes.

Current regulations do not prohibit the reuse of metal shipping containers, but requires accessory structure to
include building materials and colors that compatible with the principal building when visible from a street. The
draft ordinance proposed

Draft ordinance requirements. Pros and cons. Structurally strong. Safe in seismic events. Health related issues.
Coastal communities. Should they be permitted use. Unintended consequences.

Chair Berbert opened the public hearing.
No comments were offered.

Commissioner Jacobson recommended using shipping containers as living space but only with conditional use
approval by the Planning Commission. Once the ordinance has been refined, effective standards have been
developed, and the review process has become routine, it would be possible to extend administrative review
authority to staff. The Commission debated whether to require architectural compatibility of shipping containers
with the main building, such as a residence. Staff recommends that reuse of 200 square foot containers, or
smaller, for storage only be exempt from material and roofline standards for accessory structures but require
compatible colors with the main building. Use of a container as living space shall comply with material and color
standards for accessory structures but exempt from any roofline requirements. The front of any accessory
structure and any additional portion of the structure viewable from a public street should have materials and
color similar to the primary dwelling. Buildings used for agricultural purposes, such as barns or other such
buildings to house animals and located in a zone which allows family food production, should not be subject to
that requirement.

Commissioner Jacobson expressed significant concern that shipping containers would be abused to store
commercial materials on residential property, regardless of zoning. The staff shared this concern and offered to
draft an additional standard to address the issue.

RECOMMENDATION:
Staff recommends the Planning Commission conduct a public hearing on the proposed amendment, and:
    • Direct staff to conduct further research and refinement of the proposed amendment, or
    • Forward a recommendation to the City Council for further consideration and final decision.
