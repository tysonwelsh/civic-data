Action: RTP Amendment Process Bob Allen addressed the Committee, stating that this item is relative to the Regional Transportation Plan, also called TransPlan50, which lists regionally significant transportation projects through 2050. Federal law dictates what the RTP must include and will conduct process audits. In the recent Federal Certification Review, FHWA recommended that the RTP amendment process be updated for changes outside the normal adoption cycle. With this change, each amendment proposal will be classified into three levels. This proposed change is mirrored by the process that the Wasatch Front Regional Council follows. When MAG staff receives an amendment request, they review it with the Interagency Consultation Team (ICT). The ICT evaluates the amendment and determines whether the request is level one, two, or three. The ICT comprises the MPOs within the state that have Air Quality, UDOT, UTA, Federal Highway Administration, Federal Transit Administration, and the Department of Air Quality. The first level is Administrative Amendments, which are for exempt projects. Exempt projects are typically safety projects, active transportation projects, or quality projects, as well as non-regionally significant projects that have no air quality conformity needed for those projects. These include changing or adding to active transportation projects, clarifying a project's description, or changing ownership from a city project to a UDOT project. Bob Allen stated that the amendment request is reviewed by MAG staff and then discussed with the ICT. If a Level 1 Amendment is determined, MAG staff will consult with the MPO Board Chair and Vice Page | 2 Chair. The MAG Executive Director will approve the amendment. The amendment approval will be relayed to the MPO TAC and MPO Board. The second level is a Board Amendment. These are for non-exempt projects and non-regionally significant projects. A few examples include changes to existing project right-of-way, additions of roadway, changes to the existing functional classification, or transit corridor preservation projects. MAG staff will review the amendment with the ICT, and if determined to be Level 2, then a technical review will be completed. The amendment will be brought to the MPO TAC review and recommendation of approval to the MPO Board. After action from the MPO Board, there is an option to have a 30-day public comment period, but it is not required. Bob Allen stated that he doesn’t foresee making many amendments. Amendments are typically either Level 1 or Level 3. The third level is a Board Full Amendment. These amendments are non-exempt and regionally significant projects, and a new or renewed emissions analysis is needed. These amendments may include:● Significant change in the design or scope of a regionally significant transportation project, such as the number of lanes or length,● Adding changes more than a half mile,● Addition or subtraction of a primary transportation feature,● Significant change in the location type, size of a fixed guideway transit facility or station,● Recommended financially constrained phasing of a regionally significant project,● Addition or deletion of any regionally significant transportation project or● Change to the existing RPT functional classification, which is higher than minor arterial. The amendment has been reviewed by MAG staff and ICT. It will undergo a technical review and consideration, air quality conformity determination, MPO TAC review, and recommendation to the MPO Board. Then, the MPO Board will review and release the amendment for a 30-day public comment period. Upon the conclusion of the public comment period, MAG staff will review any comments and recommendations. The MPO Board will then determine if regionally significant changes are needed. Bob Allen said one thing that might be on the Committee's mind is what is changing. The reality is very, very little. Ultimately, this creates a transparent amendment process and ensures that MAG conforms to all Federal guidelines. Jason Bond asked what is the lead time for an amendment request. Bob Allen stated that a Level 3 amendment would take at least three months, while a Level 1 amendment may take about one month. The study's partners include MAG, UDOT, WFRC, UTA, CMPO, Dixie MPO, and RSG. This is an informative process of how people move day-to-day and travel as residents in a region. The survey incorporated a GPS within a person's phone for 1-7 days. The core survey happened in the Spring of 2023, with an additional follow-up survey completed in the Fall of 2023 that captured long-distance trips. Tim Hereth stated the sampling was random throughout the state, mostly done via USPS mailings. The data was collected through an app, call service, or website. The data collection was cleaned and evaluated for reasonableness, accuracy, and completeness. The Household Travel Survey (HTS) is primarily used for transportation master plans. It is important to capture travel patterns, which can be used in typical planning scenarios. Page | 6 The survey is expensive and was about $1.7 million statewide. Tim Hereth stated that in 2012, the cost was about the same. The frequency of this survey is about every ten years. About 10,000 households throughout the state were surveyed, meaning that about 1 in every 100 homes could participate. Tim Hereth highlighted the large amount of data that could be collected. Tim Hereth continued that from the survey and data. The desired deliverables included Results Data Tables, the Data Explorer Website, the Final Report PDF, and the Dataset Guide website. Tim Hereth covered some data that compared home-based work, home-based other, and non-home-based travel. The number of trips has increased by about 11% over the last ten years. Travel trips were broken down into the modes of transportation, the commuters' income, and the commuters' age. Data related to telecommuting to work frequency was broken down. Some data illustrated those who had telecommuting frequency based on their education level. Some data showed the frequency of package deliveries for online retailers, which was just over two trips per week per household. Those deliveries replace just under 50% of physical trips to a store. The survey included questions about what factors would encourage community transit to travel to work and options to cycle to work. There were questions about whether moving to a new home would be more desirable as a single-family detached house that would have a longer work commute and have to drive to shops/restaurants or an apartment/townhome that would have a shorter work commute and walk to shops/restaurants. Tim Hereth stated that additional information is still being collected from stakeholders. The data can be accessed by visiting https://unifiedplan.org/household-travel-surveys/ There is an option to break the data down by origin and destination. If a city is looking for something in particular, it can contact MAG’s team to see if the more extensive data set has the information.
