Proposal 
The applicant is proposing a Major Accessory Building to be located at 603 Aspen Lane. The 
proposed structure has a footprint of 4,298 square feet and gross square footage of 5,284.  
According to the applicant, the proposed accessory structure is primarily a garage to house 
classic vehicles, containing 10 parking spaces, a work area, and a wash area on the ground floor, 
and an open bar space on the second level.

Vicinity Map 
 
 
Background 
603 Aspen Lane is located within the Silver Creek Estates Unit B Subdivision, which was platted 
in 1963. 
 
Currently, there are two minor accessory structures and a primary residence located on the 
property, which is 8.25 acres. The primary residence has approximately 5,100 square feet of 
living space, 525 square feet of mechanical space, and 1,325 square feet of garage space. The 
first minor accessory structure contains approximately 1,000 square feet of garage space and 
an Accessory Dwelling Unit of 625 square feet. The other minor accessory structure, 
categorized as a secondary structure, is a storage shed of approximately 225 square feet. 
 
The applicant, RM Koemans Construction, has submitted an application on behalf of the 
homeowners.  The same architectural firm has designed the primary residence, the minor 
accessory structure, and the proposed Major Accessory Building. The buildings designs are 
meant to enhance the site and each other. The proposed Major Accessory Building will have 
natural materials, specifically, a reclaimed barnwood exterior. The landscape plan is designed to 
return the surrounding property to a mostly natural state. 
6
3 
 
 
According to Section 10-8-17 of the Snyderville Basin Development Code, a Major Accessory 
Building requires a Conditional Use Permit in the Hillside Stewardship Zone.  A Major Accessory 
Building is defined below. 
 
ACCESSORY BUILDING, MAJOR: A building or structure greater than two thousand 
(2,000) square feet but not exceeding twelve thousand five hundred (12,500) square 
feet in size which is subordinate to, and the use of which is incidental to that of, the 
principal building, structure or use on the same lot. 
 
Based on the acreage of the lot, the maximum allowed size of a Major Accessory Building is 
12,500 square feet.

Mr.
 Shane Mang contracted BIO-WEST, Inc., (BIO-WEST) to perform an aquatic resources 
inventory of UT Parcels SS-60-E and SS-60-E-1 in Summit County, Utah. The aquatic resources 
inventory is used to locate and delineate wetlands and surface waters within a survey area and to 
document existing conditions (vegetation, soils, hydrology). The survey area consists of 
approximately 20.29 acres of land located in Snyderville. The entire survey area lies within 
section 33 of township 1 South, range 4 East. Downtown Snyderville is located approximately 
1.9 miles west of the survey area. Swaner Nature Preserve is located approximately 1.15 miles to 
the north. North Old Ranch Road borders the eastern survey area (Figure 1). The approximate 
GPS coordinates of the center of the survey area are 40.692185° N latitude, and -111.508555° W 
longitude, WGS 1984 (Figure 2).  
2.0 METHODS 
BIO-W
EST personnel performed an aquatic resources investigation of the survey area on 
September 7, 2021, to delineate potential wetlands and surface-water boundaries. Wetlands and 
their boundaries were identified in accordance with the US Army Corps of Engineers Wetlands 
Delineation Manual (Environmental Laboratory 1987). In addition, the Regional Supplement to 
the Corps of Engineers Wetland Delineation Manual: Western Mountains, Valleys, and Coast 
Region (Research and Development 2010) was used for regional specificity. Determining the 
presence or absence of wetlands and their boundaries requires investigation of vegetation, soils, 
and hydrology within areas thought to be wetlands, and all three of these indicators must meet 
predefined qualifications to be classified as a wetland by the US Army Corps of Engineers 
(USACE) (Environmental Laboratory 1987). BIO-WEST personnel used predefined 
characteristics of wetlands and surface waters, if present, to determine the potential for 
jurisdiction as waters of the United States under the Clean Water Act (33 U.S.C. §1251 et seq. 
[1972]). However, the USACE has final jurisdiction when determining whether a waterbody or 
wetland is a water of the United States. 
Sampling
 points were established to examine and record existing conditions and to determine the 
presence or absence of wetlands within the survey area. Generally, when potential wetland areas 
were identified, one sample point was conducted in the potential wetland and the other in a 
nearby non-wetland area. In some cases, sample points may have been conducted at an upland 
site only, to demonstrate the absence of a wetland. BIO-WEST personnel analyzed and recorded 
existing soil, vegetation, and hydrologic conditions at each sample point. Typically, sample point 
locations that met all three wetland indicator requirements (hydrophytic vegetation, hydric soil, 
and wetland hydrology) were classified as wetland. Sample point locations not meeting all three 
indicators were classified as upland. In problematic situations, best professional judgement was 
required and the problematic situation was recorded. For example, in wetland areas where one or 
more wetland indicator(s) were absent or misleading, sample points and delineation mapping 
were completed using mainly soil characteristics, depressional landscape position, remnant 
hydrophytic vegetation, or persistent hydrological indicators, as specified by the manual 
(Environmental Laboratory 1987). If wetlands were determined present, the paired sample points 
were then used to determine and record the wetland boundary using a handheld GPS unit with 
sub-meter accuracy, and the boundaries were subsequently mapped using a GIS mapping 
program (Appendix A).

Vegetation community composition and structure were used to determine whether the plants at 
each sample point met the requirements of a hydrophytic vegetation indicator (typical of a 
wetland plant community). BIO-WEST personnel recorded the relative abundance of each 
vegetation species at each sample point using Western Mountains, Valleys, and Coast data forms 
(Appendix B) following procedures outlined in the US Army Corps of Engineers Wetlands 
Delineation Manual (Environmental Laboratory 1987). Four vegetative strata (tree stratum, 
sapling/shrub stratum, herb stratum, and woody vine stratum) were independently analyzed 
within predetermined radii at each sample point using the sample point soil pit (discussed in the 
soils section, below) as the center of the circle. BIO-WEST personnel recorded percent cover, 
when present, of each tree and woody vine species within a 30-foot radius, percent cover of 
sapling and shrub species within a 15-foot radius, and percent cover of herb species within a 5-
foot radius. Subsequently, the wetland-indicator status was determined for all plant species 
according to the USACE North American Digital Flora: National Wetland Plant List (USACE 
2018). The wetland-indicator status estimates the probability of a particular plant species 
occurring in wetland environments. For example, wetland areas are typically dominated by plant 
species with a vegetation-indicator status of facultative (34–66 percent probability of occurring 
in wetlands), facultative wetland (67–99 percent probability of occurring in wetlands), and 
obligate wetland (more than 99 percent probability of occurring in wetlands). Subsequently, and 
following procedures outlined in the regional supplement (Research and Development 2010), the 
vegetation community information recorded from each sample point was used to determine the 
presence or absence of the following hydrophytic vegetation indicators: (1) dominance test, (2) 
prevalence index, (3) morphological adaptations, (4) rapid test for hydrophytic vegetation, (5) 
wetland non-vascular plants, and (6) problematic hydrophytic vegetation.   
 
Soil profiles were used to determine if the soil at each sample point met the requirements as a 
hydric soil indicator (typical of wetland soils). BIO-WEST personnel produced soil profiles by 
digging soil pits at each sample point to a depth of at least 18 inches (if possible). At least one 
positive hydric soil indicator must be present at a sample point to classify a soil as a wetland soil 
(Environmental Laboratory 1987). For example, soils in prolonged anaerobic conditions undergo 
chemical reduction, thereby producing lighter soil colors and reduced iron concentrations called 
redox features. During the field survey, the colors of the soil profile matrix and redox features 
were identified using Munsell soil color charts (Kollmorgen Instruments 1990). Soil horizon 
thickness, texture, moisture content, and depth-to-soil saturation and the water table were noted 
when applicable. The presence or absence of particulate organic matter, organic matter staining, 
concretions, depletions, mottling, and gleying were also noted. BIO-WEST personnel used these 
characteristics to determine whether the soils observed were hydric or non-hydric following 
procedures outlined in the US Army Corps of Engineers Wetlands Delineation Manual 
(Environmental Laboratory 1987) and descriptions found in the Regional Supplement to the 
Corps of Engineers Wetland Delineation Manual: Western Mountains, Valleys, and Coast 
Region (Research and Development 2010). 
 
Standard wetland delineation procedures require the comparison of soil profiles observed in the 
field with the soil descriptions referenced in the Natural Resource Conservation Service (NRCS) 
Soil Survey (USDA NRCS 2021a). The Summit Area, Utah, Parts of Summit, Salt Lake and 
Wasatch Counties soil survey was accessed using the NRCS Web Soil Survey website (NRCS 
2021b) to produce survey-area soil maps, which were referenced during the survey area visit 
(Appendix C).

Hydrology was also investigated and recorded at each sample point, and the data were used to 
determine if the sample point met the requirements as a Wetland Hydrology Indicator (typical of 
hydrology found in wetland environments). BIO-WEST personnel determined wetland 
hydrology based on the presence of at least one positive primary indicator of prolonged 
saturation, or two positive secondary indicators of prolonged saturation (Environmental 
Laboratory 1987). Primary indicators for the western mountains, valleys, and coastal region 
include surface water, high water table, saturation, watermarks, sediment deposits, drift deposits, 
algal mat or crust, iron deposits, surface-soil cracks, inundation visible on aerial imagery, 
sparsely vegetated concave surface, water-stained leaves, salt crust, aquatic invertebrates, 
hydrogen sulfide odor, oxidized rhizospheres along living roots, presence of reduced iron, recent 
iron reduction in tilled soils, and stunted or stressed plants. Secondary indicators include water-
stained leaves, drainage patterns, dry-season water table, saturation visible on aerial imagery, 
geomorphic position, shallow aquitard, raised ant mounds, frost-heave hummocks, and 
hydrophytic results from the facultative-neutral test. Additionally, BIO-WEST personnel 
recorded surface-water depth, and depth-to-saturation and water table, when present.  
 
Non-wetland surface waters were also delineated and mapped during the aquatic resources 
survey. The ordinary high-water mark (OHWM) of non-wetland waters was delineated using 
guidance provided in the USACE A Guide to Ordinary High Water Mark (OHWM) Delineation 
for Non-Perennial Streams in the Western Mountains, Valleys, and Coast Region of the United 
States (Research and Development 2014). Two cross-section sample points (XS1 and XS2) were 
conducted to document the OHWM of non-wetland surface waters within the survey area. Two 
creeks crossed through the survey area from the south to the north. Kimball Creek crossed 
through the eastern portion of the survey area, and McLeod Creek crossed through the 
northwestern portion. Cross section 1 (XS1) was conducted on McLeod Creek in the 
northwestern portion of the survey area and cross section 2 (XS2) was conducted on Kimball 
Creek in the southeastern portion. The OHWMs and their corresponding cross sections are 
illustrated in Appendix A, and photos of each are found in Appendix D. 
 
Characterization of vegetative, hydrologic, and soil conditions guided determination of the 
presence or absence of wetlands, their boundaries, and other aquatic resources within the survey 
area. BIO-WEST personnel established eight sample points to characterize potential wetlands 
and existing conditions. If present, wetland boundaries were defined between the wetland and 
upland sample points and mapped according to wetland-defining features such as changes in 
vegetation or topography. Each sample point was photo-documented at the time of the 
investigation (Appendix D). If present, the approximate locations of delineated wetland 
boundaries, ditches, open-water features, and sample points were surveyed using a GPS with 
sub-meter accuracy. The survey data were downloaded into a GIS program to produce mapping 
that shows delineated wetland boundaries, non-wetland waters and their boundaries, estimated 
wetland and non-wetland acreages, and cross-section and sample-point locations (Appendix A).

4.3   Soils 
 
Soils were examined in the field during the BIO-WEST aquatic resources survey. The soil 
textures encountered were primarily loam, with sand, clay, silt, and highly organic peat-like soil 
present at some sample point locations. The NRCS Web Soil Survey (USDA NRCS 2021b) of 
the survey area was mapped and reviewed prior to use as a reference while on site (Appendix C). 
Wanship loam, 0 to 3 percent slopes (178), was the most abundant soil type mapped within the 
survey area (71.3%). Wanship-Kovich loams, 0 to 3 percent slopes (179), was the next most 
abundant (26.9%). Fewkes-Yeates Hollow complex, 15 to 30 percent slopes (135), accounted for

the remaining 1.8 percent of soil mapped within the survey area. Wanship loam, 0 to 3 percent 
slopes (178), is listed by the NRCS as predominantly hydric; Wanship-Kovich loams, 0 to 3 
percent slopes (179), is listed as predominantly non-hydric; and Fewkes-Yeates Hollow 
complex, 15 to 30 percent slopes (135), is listed as a non-hydric soil in Utah (USDA NRCS

The NRCS (USDA NRCS 2021a) provides the following soil series descriptions: 
 
The Wanship series (178 & 179) consists of very deep, somewhat poorly drained 
soils that formed in alluvium derived from sandstone and conglomerate. Wanship 
soils are on flood plains and stream terraces with slopes ranging from 0 to 3 percent. 
Mean annual precipitation is about 18 inches and the mean annual temperature is 
about 43 degrees F. Wanship soils are classified as fine-loamy over sandy or sandy-
skeletal, mixed, superactive, frigid Aquic Cumulis Haploxerolls . In regular years, 
endosaturation occurs from 24 to 60 inches. The NRCS describes ground water 
from a pedon sample at 40 inches below the soil surface. Drainage of Wanship soils 
is somewhat poor with low runoff, with moderate over rapid permeability. Wanship 
soils are rarely flooded for brief periods during the months of March through May. 
Wanship series soils are used as pastureland, and in Utah are considered semiwet 
fresh meadow ecological sites.  
 
The Kovich series (179) consists of very deep, poorly drained soils that formed in 
alluvium from mixed rocks, mainly sandstone, andesite, and quartzite. Kovich soils 
are on flood plains, alluvial fans, and broad valley floors with slopes ranging from 
0-4 percent. Kovich soils are classified as fine- loamy, mixed, superactive, frigid 
Cumulic Endoquolls. No water table is described for the Kovich series but the soils 
are saturated with water at some period during the year. Permeability is moderate 
or moderately slow and Kovich soils are poorly drained with slow runoff. Kovich 
soils are used for irrigated crops, wet meadow hay, and pasture. Native plants are 
cottonwoods, willows, river birch, white alder, native hawthorne, with an 
understory of grass, forbs, and shrubs. Understory species include wiregrass, 
timothy, redtop, native bluegrass, tufted hairgrass, sedges, rushes, yarrow, and 
cinquefoil. 
 
The Fewkes series (135) consists of very deep, well drained soils that formed in 
slope alluvium, residuum, and colluvium derived from quartzite, sandstone , and 
shale. Fewkes soils are on hills, fan remnants and mountain slopes  with slope s 
ranging from 2 to 60 percent. Mean annual precipitation is about 18 inches and the 
mean annual temperature is about 42 degrees F. Fewkes soils are classified as fine-

with high to very high runoff. Fewkes soils are used as rangeland and are typically

Hydric soil indicators at wetland sample point locations were indicated by a depleted layer 
beneath dark soil, hydrogen sulfide odor, or redox concentrations in the soil profile, all of which 
typically result from prolonged periods of flooding and soil saturation. A thick dark surface 
(hydric soil indicator A12) characterized most of the wetland soils (sample points 01, 02, 03, 05, 
06, and 08). Hydrogen sulfide odor (hydric soil indicator A4) was also present at sample point 
06, near Kimball Creek. A redox dark surface (hydric soil indicator F6) characterized sample 
point 04, near McLeod Creek. Wetland soils were generally very dark (10YR 2/1) and were 
mostly loam with some silt, clay, and organic matter present.  
 
The soils at sample points 04 (Wetland B) and 06 (Wetland C) were saturated, but the loamy 
soils throughout the remainder of the wet meadow were dry. Sample points 04 and 06 were 
conducted near McLeod Creek and Kimball Creek, respectively. Soils adjacent to creeks are 
typically saturated due to lateral seepage from the flowing water. Conversely, the wet meadow 
soils absorb water from the water table and may be endosaturated during snowmelt and rain 
events. Drought, and the long, hot summers typical in arid Utah can cause wetland water tables 
to decline. As the water drains, wetland soils can dry out completely, but indicators of ponding 
and saturation such as redox concentrations, a depleted layer, highly organic soils, and 
hydrophytic vegetation are left behind. A depleted layer with redox concentrations was detected 
below the thick, dark soil at sample point 02. Thick, dark soils were present throughout the entire 
wet meadow and very little topographic variability suggests a depleted layer is likely present 
deep below the dark soils. Depleted layers are typical in wetlands with a seasonal high water 
table. Additionally, a highly organic, peat-like soil layer was present above the dark soils at 
sample points 05 and 08. Saturation causes the soil environment to become anaerobic, which 
reduces the efficiency of soil microbes to decompose organic matter. As a result, organic 
material tends to build up in soils that are saturated for extended periods of time. The presence of 
a depleted layer, dense wetland vegetation, and highly organic, peat-like soils throughout the wet 
meadow suggest wetland conditions during normal water years.  
 
Upland soils (soils outside of the wetlands) were very dry and hard-packed. In some cases, 
upland soils contained redox concentrations typical of wetlands. Upland soils also contained 
cobble and gravel is some cases and appeared mixed. Mixed soils with redox concentrations in 
only part of the matrix can indicate imported fill material. Soil may have been excavated from 
nearby wetlands and mixed with upland soils to create fill material used for the berm around 
portions of the pond.

4.4  Hydrology 
 
Survey area hydrology appeared to be primarily influenced by flowing creek water, spring-seep 
water, and a seasonal high water table. The survey area lies in a low area on an extensive flat at 
the toe of a mountain slope. Mountains border the eastern survey area, and hillslopes rise 
approximately 200 yards to the south. This low basin appears to collect creek and ground water 
as it flows downslope from the surrounding mountains. Collected groundwater discharges into a 
number of ponds along the basin floor, at least three of which appear to be the headwaters for 
Kimball Creek. Kimball Creek drains the water from these upslope ponded areas (south of the 
survey area) before flowing downslope, where it then crosses south-to-north through the eastern 
portion of the survey area. A ponded area within the survey area also appeared to intercept the 
water table. Excess pond water flows into Kimball Creek before continuing downslope. 
Furthermore, three spring-seep sources daylight from the toe of the eastern hillslope within the 
survey area. The spring water flows through Wetland F before converging with Kimball Creek. 
After collecting water from the ponded area and the springs, Kimball Creek exits the survey area 
through the northern survey area boundary. McLeod Creek drains water from the mountains 
southwest of the survey area. The water flows along the base of Quarry Mountain before flowing 
into the large wet meadow west of the survey area. McLeod Creek crosses through the 
northwestern corner of the survey area and then continues north, where it converges with 
Kimball creek downstream (north) of the survey area.  
 
Multi-year drought cycles can cause wetlands to periodically lack indicators of wetland 
hydrology; and the long, hot summers in Utah can cause water to recede from wetland margins, 
water tables to drop, and many wetlands to dry out completely (Research and Development 
2010). Most of the wet meadow sample pits did not exhibit a water table during the aquatic 
resources survey. However, the survey was conducted during an extreme drought year (The US 
Drought Monitor 2021), with 2020 being the driest year on record and one of the hottest (Utah 
Department of Natural Resources 2021). Most of Utah has experienced exceptional drought 
conditions during 2021. Summit County’s drought conditions have been classified as extreme 
(The US Drought Monitor 2021). Additionally, the aquatic resources survey was conducted in 
September, long after peak runoff. Water tables in wetlands often drop well below 24 inches of 
the soil surface during dry periods (Research and Development 2010). Therefore, the absence of 
a water table within 24 inches of the soil surface does not necessarily indicate a lack of wetland 
hydrology. The dominance of the meadow by obligate wetland plants, the survey area’s 
geomorphic position, and the presence of a depleted layer beneath very dark soil all provide 
evidence for a high water table during normal water years. Indeed, historic aerial imagery from 
various years (e.g., 2011, 2017) show very wet conditions throughout the wet meadow, which 
appears to be supported primarily by a high water table (Google Earth Pro 2021).  
 
Primary and secondary indicators of wetland hydrology were present during the aquatic 
resources survey, providing evidence for wetlands within the survey area. Primary indicators at 
wetland sample points included saturation, surface water, and hydrogen sulfide odor. Secondary 
wetland hydrology indicators included geomorphic position and the FAC-neutral test.

5.0 DELINEATION SUMMARY  
AND CONCLUSIONS 
 
Three palustrine emergent wetlands (11.12 acres), three scrub-shrub wetlands (3.84 acres), two 
creeks (1,092 linear feet; 0.25 acres), an open water pond (0.35 acres), and three spring-seep 
sources were identified within the survey area during the BIO-WEST aquatic resources survey in 
September 2021 (Appendix A). All six wetlands exhibited the characteristics required to meet 
hydrophytic vegetation, hydric soil, and hydrology indicators of wetlands (Appendix B). Survey 
area hydrology was supported primarily by creek water flowing through the survey area, spring 
water discharge from the toe of the eastern hillslope, and a seasonal high water table. Upland 
habitat accounted for the remainder of the survey area. The upland sample point (sample point 
07) lacked all three indicators required of wetlands (hydrophytic vegetation, wetland hydrology, 
and hydric soil).  
 
Hydrology determination at this site was complicated by the timing of the aquatic resources 
survey, which was conducted during a multi-year drought cycle and during late summer, long 
after peak runoff in Utah. Dry, warm conditions can significantly limit the influence of a high 
water table and lotic flows. Multi-year drought cycles can cause wetlands to periodically lack 
indicators of wetland hydrology, and the long, hot summers in Utah can cause water to recede 
from wetland margins, water tables to drop, and many wetlands to dry out completely (Research 
and Development 2010). If it could be definitively proven that the water table does not rise to 
within 12 inches of the soil surface for an extended period of time (approximately 14 days 
consecutively) during the growing season, during normal water years, then the USACE would 
likely not have regulatory authority over those areas. It may be possible that some of the higher-
elevation areas within the wet meadow do not experience a high water table within 12 inches of 
the soil surface, in which case those higher areas could be classified as non-wetland and 
delineated as uplands during a follow-up visit. This would require monitoring the survey area for 
changes to wetland hydrology following drought release and earlier during the growing season.  
 
The USACE has final jurisdiction when determining whether a waterbody or wetland is a 
jurisdictional water of the United States. All of the aquatic resources (creeks, ponds, springs, and 
wetlands) within the survey area are, or are directly adjacent to, waters which flow downstream, 
eventually flowing into the Great Salt Lake. Therefore, it is likely that the USACE will classify 
all of the wetlands and surface waters within the survey area as jurisdictional.  
 
These results are preliminary, and the USACE has the final decision when determining whether a 
waterbody or wetland is a jurisdictional water of the United States.
