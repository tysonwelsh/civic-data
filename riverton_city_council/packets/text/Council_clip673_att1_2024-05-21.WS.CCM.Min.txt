39          Accessory Structures Discussion
40
41   Kevin Hicks, Interim City Manager, noted that the Council had recently emphasized the need for
42   greater focus on code enforcement, leading to questions about shipping containers specifically.
43   He explained that staff intended to provide clarity on how shipping containers are currently
44   treated under existing codes and ordinances.
45
46   Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, presented information about the current
47   regulations and enforcement concerning shipping containers. He reminded the Council that the
48   ordinance prohibits storage containers, with exceptions made for temporary use during moves.
49   Despite their popularity due to low cost and availability, shipping containers can be problematic
50   because they often appear industrial and unkempt. To address this, the City has adopted a
51   compromise allowing shipping containers to be repurposed as accessory structures, provided
     Page | 2 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     May 21, 2024




 1   they meet certain standards. These standards include matching the exterior materials and
 2   design of existing structures, with a stipulation that metal siding, except for aluminum, is not
 3   permitted. Containers below 200 square feet must be consistent in color and design with the
 4   main dwelling.
 5
 6   Mr. Lethbridge highlighted recent legislative changes which raised the threshold for requiring a
 7   building permit from 200 to 350 square feet for cargo containers, simplifying the compliance
 8   process. This change means most cargo containers no longer need a building permit, easing
 9   some regulatory burdens.
10
11   Councilmember McDougal asked how long the original code prohibiting storage containers has
12   been on the books. In response, Mr. Lethbridge clarified that the specific reference to cargo
13   containers in the City code was established in 2011. However, even before this date, there was
14   an ordinance prohibiting metal as an exterior material for structures, which implicitly included
15   cargo containers. Therefore, cargo containers were never legally permitted as exterior
16   structures, and this is pertinent to the question of whether existing containers are
17   "grandfathered" in. Since they were never allowed under the earlier code, containers that have
18   been on properties for years were never in compliance, even if they went unnoticed.
19
20   Councilmembers and staff further discussed the storage container issues. Mr. Lethbridge
21   confirmed his department is preparing material, including visual aids, to address the issue of
22   cargo containers. He plans to collaborate with code enforcement to ensure this information is
23   included in initial contacts with residents who have or are reported to have these containers.
24   Additionally, he is working with the communications team to make this information available on
25   the website and other platforms.
26
27   Ryan Carter, City Attorney, emphasized the need for better communication between code
28   enforcement officers and the planning and zoning department to guide residents towards
29   compliance solutions. He suggested that all staff should provide a consistent message
30   regarding this issue. He expressed optimism that as this transition progresses and longstanding
31   issues are resolved, the situation will improve.

38            Resolution No. 24-48 – Reaffiming the Policy of Riverton City, Utah, Regarding
39            the placement of Campaign Signs on Park Strips Located Adjacent to City-
40            Owned Property
41
42   Mr. Carter discussed the Utah Code 20A-17-103, which allows governmental entities to permit
43   the posting of campaign political signage on public property, provided that all candidates are
44   allowed equal opportunity. Riverton City's current ordinance under 18.150.090 restricts
45   political signage to private property only. However, the state code, which was enacted after
46   the city code, overrides this provision.
47
48   In 2017, the City Council decided to allow political signage on park strips in front of
49   governmental property during campaign seasons. Given the turnover in the Council and the
50   upcoming campaign season, it was proposed to reaffirm this practice through a formal
51   resolution.
     Page | 8 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     May 21, 2024




 1   The resolution would direct staff not to enforce the restriction of campaign signage to private
 2   property only, thus allowing signage on public property as per state law. Public Works staff
 3   requested clear guidance on handling these signs during maintenance activities, as they have
 4   encountered issues with maintaining park strips where campaign signs are placed.
 5
 6   The proposed solution was for staff to work around the signs without removing them, even if it
 7   meant occasional minor issues like dead spots in the lawn or excessive grass growth under
 8   the signs.
 9
10   Councilmember McDougal expressed opposition to allowing political signs on public parkways
11   and park strips, citing several issues. The concerns included interference with sprinklers and
12   mowing, as well as potential safety hazards for families and children playing in parks, who
13   might encounter damaged or knocked-over signs.
14
15   Councilmember McDougal emphasized the responsibility that comes with placing signs on
16   private property, where homeowners take care of the signs by removing and maintaining
17   them. In contrast, signs on public property tend to be neglected, leading to lingering, outdated
18   signage.
19
20   Councilmember Haymond agreed with Councilmember McDougal, sharing his recent
21   campaign experience of seeking permission from private property owners to place signs. He
22   emphasized the importance of involving the community in elections, suggesting that citizens
23   should actively support candidates by placing signs on their own properties.
24
25   Councilmember Pierucci expressed support for the resolution, emphasizing the importance of
26   protecting free speech rights. He acknowledged that while the government can set time and
27   place restrictions on speech, such powers should be used sparingly. He argued that the public
28   benefit of allowing candidates to communicate their messages outweighs the potential issues
29   of limiting signage to private property. He did not see a problem with campaign signs in park
30   strips or in front of parks, viewing it as a necessary part of the election process and
31   communication with residents.
32
33   Councilmember Buroker highlighted that while placing signs in parking strips might seem
34   productive, it is more respectful to obtain permission from property owners. This approach not
35   only fosters respect for private property but also ensures that all candidates follow the same
36   rules, creating a fair campaign environment.
37
38   Mayor Pro Tempore McCay expressed support for maintaining the current practice of allowing
39   campaign signs in park strips, which has been in place since 2017. She emphasized the
40   difficulty of changing this rule, especially during an ongoing election season, which includes
41   statewide and countywide elections.
42
43   Councilmember Pierucci MOVED that the City Council approve Resolution No. 24-48 –
44   Reaffiming the Policy of Riverton City, Utah, Regarding the placement of Campaign
45   Signs on Park Strips Located Adjacent to City-Owned Property. Mayor Pro Tempore
46   McCay SECONDED the motion. Mayor Pro Tempore McCay called for discussion on the
47   motion. Mayor Pro Tempore McCay called for a roll-call vote. The vote was as follows:
48   Buroker-no, Haymond-no, McCay-yes, McDougal-no, and Pierucci-yes. The motion FAILED

25   Councilmember Buroker expressed a desire to incorporate resident feedback into decisions
26   about recycling by conducting a survey. She emphasized that the recycling program should
27   operate as a self-sustaining enterprise fund. She concluded by stating that relying on the
28   general fund to sustain the recycling program is not beneficial for the residents or the City.
29
30   Councilmember Haymond expressed a preference for making a decision on recycling without
31   conducting a survey. He highlighted the importance of understanding the numbers and
32   complexities involved in the decision, which might not be fully grasped by all residents.
33
34   Councilmember McDougal expressed agreement with the idea of making the sanitation fund
35   an enterprise fund, running independently from the general budget. He highlighted the benefit
36   of this approach, noting that it would allow for clearer future planning and budgeting without
37   concerns about costs beyond the City's control.
38
39   Councilmember McDougal proposed gathering information from surrounding cities about their
40   recycling practices to better inform the Council's decision. He reiterated that the two key
41   issues are establishing the sanitation fund as an enterprise fund and determining how to
42   manage the resulting cost increases, whether through eliminating recycling, adjusting fees, or
43   other means.
44
45   Mayor Pro Tempore McCay expressed a preference for gathering feedback from residents
46   through a survey, emphasizing the ease of modern technology for conducting such surveys.
47   She shared anecdotal evidence from discussions with other Riverton residents, indicating a
48   preference for keeping recycling despite potential fee increases. She expressed concern
49   about the current contamination rate of 25.8%, which exceeds the goal of 15%, and
50   emphasized the need for better resident education on recycling practices. She proposed
51   setting a goal to reduce the contamination rate to 15% or less within three to six months, with
     Page | 10 Minutes – City Council Meeting

 1   a commitment to reevaluate the situation if the goal is not met.
 2
 3   Councilmember Pierucci emphasized the importance of engaging residents in decision-making
 4   processes and finding innovative ways to involve them. He was not opposed to sending out a
 5   survey, even if it might not be academically sound, to gauge public sentiment on the proposed
 6   changes to the recycling program.

 9   This subsidy is due to the inclusion of six months of curbside recycling service. It was also
10   clarified that the sanitation fund is already considered an enterprise fund. Therefore, the
11   discussion should focus on whether to make it fully self-sustaining without any subsidies from

19   Mr. Hicks conveyed that Waste Management emphasized the company's commitment to
20   partnering with the City to educate the public on recycling. Waste Management has various
21   tools and educational materials available and aims to work collaboratively with the City to
22   ensure residents understand what can and cannot be recycled.

25   recycling, even with a regional model. He shared his personal reluctance to take recyclables to

27   being transparent about the costs of recycling and allowing residents to decide if they want to
28   pay for it. Additionally, he stressed the importance of maintaining and even increasing the
29   City's savings, advocating for a robust savings account that could cover a full year's budget.
30
31   Councilmember McDougal shared his concerns about the City's financial situation, highlighting
32   that despite having $6 million in the budget, the City is also burdened with $30 million in debt,
33   incurring interest rates of three to five percent. He stressed the importance of reducing this
34   debt to save residents from paying interest.
35
36   Councilmember McDougal expressed skepticism about conducting a public survey on
37   recycling, noting the complexity of the issue and the difficulty in fully informing the public
38   through a survey. He pointed out that the perception of landfill disposal as inherently harmful

40   methane gas, which is used to generate power. He also mentioned that the recycling industry
41   is heavily influenced by national politics and federal subsidies, which can change with different
42   administrations. This political influence can lead to fluctuations in what materials are accepted
43   for recycling, further confusing residents.
44
45   Mayor Pro Tempore McCay suggested that the City's communication department could create
46   a visually appealing and informative chart to help residents understand the costs and benefits
47   of recycling. She highlighted that if recycling were eliminated, residents might end up needing
48   an additional garbage can, effectively increasing their monthly costs by $3.50. She advocated
49   for educating residents about the costs and benefits of recycling and involving them in the
50   decision-making process through surveys.

 1   Councilmembers continued discussing how best to educate residents about the decision to
 2   keep or eliminate recycling. Councilmembers decided to utilize emails and the City’s website
 3   and provide detailed numbers and data, thus enhancing transparency and enabling informed
 4   feedback.
 5
 6   Mayor Pro Tempore McCay proposed discussing the Youth Council's suggestion for a
 7   scholarship program for graduating seniors. She praised the Youth Council's idea of a sliding
 8   scale based on participation, number of years, and hours of service. She suggested setting a
 9   total scholarship fund amount, such as $5,000, which could then be distributed based on the
10   seniors' commitment and involvement.
11
12   Sheril Garn, Events and Operations Manager, proposed a system similar to that used for Miss
13   Riverton, where scholarship recipients would need to provide an invoice from their college or
14   educational institution to receive the funds. She suggested implementing a two-year limit for
15   using the scholarship.
16
17   Councilmembers further discussed the scholarship money and decided to allocate a set
18   amount of money each year, $5,000, to be distributed by the Youth Council advisors with a
19   maximum of $1500 per recipient. Scholarship money could be used for higher education or
20   trade schools.
