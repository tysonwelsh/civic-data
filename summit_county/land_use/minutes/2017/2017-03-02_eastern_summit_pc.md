1.  Discussion regarding fence requirements in Chapter 11-2-2 of the Eastern Summit 
County Development Code. – Ray Milliner, Principal Planner 
Planner Milliner stated the Staff Report contains a memorandum that Attorney Strachan 
wrote a few years ago concerning Eastern Summit County’s fencing regulations in Chapter 
2 of the Code. The State of Utah is a “fence in” state for livestock. The only fencing 
Eastern Summit County Planning Commission 
March 2, 2017 
Page 6 of 17 
 
 
 
regulations in Eastern Summit County’s Code are regarding livestock. “Fence in” means it is 
the obligation of the property owner or the livestock owner to fence the livestock in. The 
Utah State Code gives the County the ability to create its own ordinance, provided certain 
criteria and certain rules are met. The Eastern Summit County rules have traditionally been 
“fence out.” This means that it is the responsibility of the adjacent property owner to have 
fencing up to make sure that cattle do not get into their yard or property. In the Eastern 
Summit County Code the provisions are unclear. It is not specific as to who the responsible 
party is for paying for the fence. The Code is also not specific as to who the responsible 
party is for a major development and what constitutes a major development. Planner 
Milliner would like to know if the Commission would like the Planning Department to dig in 
to those existing regulations and clarify them; Also if the Commission would like to keep it 
as “fence out.” 
Commissioner Willoughby asked how long Eastern Summit County has been “fence out.” 
Planner Milliner answered that it has been essentially since the Code was written in 1996. 
Commissioner Sonntag stated that Promontory specifically requires “fence out.” 
Commissioner Sargent shared some background on this regulation. The reason this was 
put in the Development Code to begin with was because of the big surge of development in 
the mid to late 90’s. The Planning Commission felt it was an extra burden on adjacent 
agricultural operators to take on that responsibility of maintaining the cattle, particularly 
when new developments were coming in. There were several complaints from the owners 
of the new lots saying the cattle were coming on to their property. The lot owner did not 
necessarily want to spend the money to fence in the entire lot. The intent of this was to try 
Eastern Summit County Planning Commission 
March 2, 2017 
Page 7 of 17 
 
 
 
to share the burden. This gave the agricultural operator the opportunity to not have the 
burden one hundred percent and let the developer or the lot owner carry some of that 
burden. Commissioner Sargent does not believe it has worked very well. 
Planner Milliner stated the County does not have any teeth to go in and start charging 
people for fences. It can be a Condition of Approval, but the current rules do not work very 
well. The concern of the farmer who has new development come in adjacent to their 
property is an extra burden to build a large fence.  
Commissioner Willoughby asked if this would change open range. Who would do the 
fencing for open range? Planner Milliner answered the County is silent on that. Attorney 
Strachan stated State Code does not require a person to fence. It is smart to fence because if 
a livestock owner does not they would be liable if their livestock got out. A wise livestock 
owner would fence in so they would not be potentially liable for trespass and damages.

livestock would want to fence in for their own protection. Another issue is when new 
development comes in, some fences have been in for several years and they are adequate. 
Farmers and ranchers have to fix them all the time. When a new development comes in 
those fences are not quite adequate. To place the burden on a livestock owner because a 
new development comes in is not fair. The new development should fence in their 
development. The third issue is the open range. If a farmer does not want to fence and he
