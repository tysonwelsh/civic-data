 1            Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District Proposed Tax Increase
 2
 3   Sherrie Ohrn, Board of Trustees of Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District, provided details
 4   about the truth in taxation hearing which is scheduled for August 9 at 6 pm at the Jordan Valley
 5   Water Conservancy District Offices located at 8215 South 1300 West in West Jordan. She said
 6   the proposed tax increase for the District this year would be 9.3%. The District aims to keep the
 7   increase as low as possible while still fulfilling their obligations. She emphasized the importance
 8   of having taxing ability to maintain good bond rates and fund crucial infrastructure projects, such
 9   as water-related capital projects.
10
11   Ms. Ohrn said that to illustrate the impact of the tax increase, the District provided a flyer with
12   examples of annual tax increases for homes at different price points. For instance, a $400,000
13   home would see an increase of $6.38 annually, a $550,000 home an increase of $8.77, and a
14   $700,000 home an increase of $11.17. The flyer also outlined some of the District's upcoming
15   capital projects, including a treatment plant in Herriman worth $70 million, new storage
16   reservoirs totaling $40 million, a southwest aqueduct costing $35 million, groundwater
17   development at $10 million, and debt service amounting to $30 million.
18
19   Councilmember McCay asked who the bond benefits and what capital projects it funds. In
20   response, Ms. Ohrn highlighted the significance of the planned capital projects, which included
21   upgrades to the treatment plant, construction of storage reservoirs, aqueducts, and new wells.
22   She clarified that the bonds would be utilized to fund these infrastructure initiatives. The new
23   reservoirs and other projects are intended to serve the needs of the community in the coming
24   decades, providing necessary resources for continued growth and development. The bonds,
25   which have a long-term repayment period, will be assessed on future residents who will benefit
26   from the infrastructure improvements made possible through the bonds.
27
28   Councilmember McCay suggested that the Jordan Valley Water Commission explore the
29   possibility of charging the developers for the infrastructure improvements, rather than burdening
30   existing residents with the cost. She believes the benefits of these improvements would
31   primarily be reaped by future homeowners and generations, while the current residents might
32   not directly benefit from the development.
33
34   Ms. Ohrn explained that while the burden of the bond increase is shared among all residents,
35   including existing ones, new growth is expected to pay its share through property taxes and
36   other contributions. She said it was important to recognize that investing in infrastructure
37   benefits the entire community, not just new developments. The District's efforts in water supply,
38   flood control, and other essential services provide advantages to all residents, irrespective of
39   their individual contributions to the funding. She said she would share Councilmember McCay’s
40   concerns with the Board.
41
42   Councilmember McDougal said he shared the same concerns as Councilmember McCay and
43   believes the current tax increase might disproportionately impact infill cities with minimal
44   development. As an infill city, residents may feel like they are financing the growth of other cities
45   without receiving direct benefits from impact fees that other cities might impose. He said it was
46   important to have a balanced approach that considers the fair distribution of costs and benefits
47   among all cities involved. He asked if there was a possibility of future tax increases to cover the
48   costs associated with the Olympia Hills project or other potential developments.
49   Ms. Ohrn said the District’s capital facilities plan outlines various capital projects scheduled to
50   be implemented over the next several years. The plan shows the projects that will come online
51   and the services they will provide during the next decade and beyond. The Olympia Hills
     Page | 3 Minutes – City Council Meeting

 1   development is designed to have its own water tanks, piping, and related infrastructure. Funding
 2   for these components will be sourced through a public infrastructure district (PID) established
 3   for the development. Consequently, the developer for this project will bear this responsibility.

 5   Mayor Staggs emphasized the importance of investing in infrastructure without burdening
 6   taxpayers. He gave the example of the sewer board, where impact fees from new developments
 7   were utilized to maintain existing facilities and fund expansion projects without raising rates or
 8   taxes. He suggested that the Conservancy District might need to address the issue of impact
 9   fees and potentially shift the burden onto new developments for the expansion of treatment
10   plants and reservoirs.
11
12   Councilmember Pierucci asked about debt service and if there would be a tax cut at some point.
13
14   Shazelle Terry, Assistant General Manager for Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District,
15   clarified that the tax increase under discussion was specifically for the current service area and
16   did not apply to the Kennecott lands that were not yet annexed.
17
18   Regarding the debt service, Ms. Terry noted that the current period had significant growth,
19   especially in the southwest area. This led to the largest and most intensive capital development
20   phase the District had ever experienced, with a particular focus on constructing a substantial
21   pond.
22
23   Ms. Terry said the Capital Improvements Plan for the next decade highlighted that the current
24   and upcoming years would likely see the highest capital investment. The expectation was that
25   this infrastructure development would support the community for many years to come, and as a
26   result, costs should gradually decrease over time.

10   Cary Necaise, Public Works Director, said there was an agreement with a contractor to
11   complete the bank stabilization work. This work was crucial for the City to qualify for a $150,000
12   grant. He explained that the money for the grant was sourced from the previously discussed

25   opting for concrete solutions.
26
27   Mr. Necaise said they would be using natural materials while implementing measures to prevent
28   erosion. This project aims to maintain the natural look and aesthetics of the Jordan River while
29   addressing the stability and safety concerns effectively.
30
31   Councilmember Buroker acknowledged that while the delay on the stabilization project might
32   have increased the project's costs, it allowed for collaboration with Draper, who would be
33   working on the opposite side of the river. She emphasized the importance of understanding that
34   water flows and river dynamics are interconnected, and protecting one side without considering
35   the other could be counterproductive.

38            Resolution No. 23-59 - Approving an Amendment to the Riverton City Personnel
39            Policies and Procedures Manual
40
41   Councilmember McCay expressed her support for allowing military employees to have time off
42   but had some concerns about the proposed change. She noticed that the proposal required a
43   minimum of 14 calendar days' notice to the City before leaving for military service, but it did not
44   mention the total number of days allowed for military leave in a year.
45
46   In response, Ryan Carter, City Attorney, provided further explanation and context regarding the
47   proposed changes related to military service leave. He stated that the number of days an
48   employee is entitled to for military service leave is already covered by state statutes. Therefore,
49   he said it was not necessary to specify this number in the City ordinance. The reason behind
50   this decision was to avoid constantly updating the ordinance in case of any changes to state
51   statutes in the future.

22            Ordinance No. 23-13 -Text Amendment, Amendment to the Riverton City Code
23            Section 18. (20,25,30,35,40).050.9, Amending the total allowed lot coverage in
24            single-family residential zones, and amending Section 18.05.030 Definitions,
25            excluding pools from coverage calculations. Amendments proposed by Riverton
26            City.
27
28   Mr. Lethbridge said the current code sets a maximum lot coverage of 30%, which includes the
29   footprints of all structures, such as homes and accessory buildings. Swimming pools are also
30   counted as structures under the existing ordinance.
31
32   Mr. Lethbridge said the proposed amendment, which was discussed in a previous work session,
33   aims to increase the maximum lot coverage to 40%. This means allowing a slightly larger area
34   of each lot to be covered by structures, while excluding swimming pools from this calculation.
35   The amendment also ensures that pavement is not included in the 40% coverage calculation.
36
37   Mr. Lethbridge explained that the feedback from the Planning Commission and the public was
38   generally positive regarding the amendment. Residents expressed a desire for more flexibility in
39   terms of expanding their homes and accessory buildings, while also aiming to be more water-
40   wise by reducing irrigated or landscaped areas.        He said the Planning Commission
41   recommended approval of the amendment, and the staff also concurred with their
42   recommendation.
43

49   Section 18.05.030 Definitions, excluding pools from coverage calculations.

19            AI Guidelines Discussion - Councilmember Pierucci
20
21   Councilmember Pierucci raised the topic of generative AI and its increasing impact on society.
22   He noted that AI, including platforms like ChatGPT, has become more prominent in recent years
23   and is being used in various ways across different industries.
24
25   Councilmember Pierucci expressed concern that while the state and counties are starting to
26   address policies and regulations related to AI, cities have not yet taken significant steps in this
27   direction. He suggested that it is essential for cities to engage in discussions about how they
28   want AI to shape their communities and how they can interact with and utilize AI to serve their
29   residents better. He proposed that the City should start having conversations about creating
30   guidelines or policies to provide staff with clear boundaries and expectations regarding the use
31   of AI.
32
33   Mr. Carter said he thought it was important for Council and staff to engage in discussions on AI,
34   despite not being experts in the field. He expressed concerns about the policy's encouragement
35   of "responsible experimentation" with AI, as it could potentially lead to unintended
36   consequences. He suggested that the City should consider crafting its own AI policy based on
37   the Boston model or explore other options, such as conducting research, questionnaires for
38   employees, and obtaining training on AI usage. He also proposed the idea of seeking a
39   consultant through an RFP (Request for Proposal) to help develop an AI policy specifically
40   tailored for municipalities.
41
42   Mayor Staggs suggested that the League of Cities and Towns should be involved in providing
43   guidance on AI policies for municipalities. He emphasized the City's investment in the
44   organization and the need for the League to take a proactive approach in offering model
45   legislation or recommendations for dealing with AI-related issues.
46
47   Councilmember Buroker said she listened to a panel discussion organized by the Chamber of
48   Commerce and highlighted the challenges faced by the state in crafting a statewide IT policy.
49   She also mentioned the importance of being cautious about automating certain functions and
50   how AI could impact transitions and changes in the City's current computer systems. Based on
51   her current understanding and the rapidly changing nature of AI, she expressed reservations
     Page | 9 Minutes – City Council Meeting
     June 20, 2023




 1   about moving forward with any policy without further research and discussion.
 2
 3   Councilmember McDougal said that AI technology is in the early stages and agrees with Chief
 4   Barfuss's measured approach in seeking further information before making decisions. He also
 5   appreciated the Mayor's comment about the League of Cities and Towns taking the lead in
 6   addressing AI policies.
 7
 8   Councilmember Wells agreed with the cautious approach and emphasized the need to be
 9   careful about potential unintended consequences of implementing AI policies too early. He also
10   supported the idea of the League of Cities and Towns taking the lead on this matter, as they are
11   well-informed about developments in the state legislature and can provide guidance on any
12   potential policy changes related to AI.
13
14   Councilmember Pierucci emphasized that while they may not be looking to regulate AI usage
15   outside the City's context, he believes it's essential to have a policy that guides and encourages
16   responsible AI usage within the City by its employees. He believes that being proactive and
17   setting guidelines early will help prevent potential issues and ensure that AI is used responsibly,
18   with a focus on innovation while considering the implications on civil liberties and bias.
19
20   Councilmember McDougal shared the perspective that AI should be viewed as another tool,
21   similar to other software applications like Excel or WordPerfect. He believe that as long as
22   employees use AI responsibly and within the existing employment guidelines, it should not
23   encroach on civil liberties.
24
25   Councilmember McCay agreed that it's worth having additional discussions about AI and its
26   usage in the City. She suggested considering a policy that requires City employees to inform
27   their supervisors or department heads if they are using AI tools.
