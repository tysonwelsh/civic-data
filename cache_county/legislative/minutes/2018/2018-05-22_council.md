« Review/Approval of Restaurant/RAPZ Tax Committee Recommendations —
Executive Buttars noted the procedure is to present the recommendations of the

RAPZ/Restaurant Tax Committee at one meeting and return in a couple of weeks for
approval.

Council member Merrill asked Executive Buttars to explain the committee’s procedure.
Executive Buttars said the committee receives the printed instructions for ranking the
applications and ranks them on a 1-5 basis, with 5 being the highest. The applications
then go to the finance office for compilation for comparison with the dollars available in
each category. The committee meets again and reviews each individual project looking
at the total cost of the project and past awards given to the entity and priorities given by
applicants with more than one project. Population and attendance numbers are
considered and whether the project qualifies under state statute. Chairman Erickson
commented there are also some interlocal agreements for allocations for a specified
time on long-term projects as well.

Council member Worthen asked if Stacey Frisk, Cache Humane Society Director, could
be recognized to speak.

Frisk said their request was denied because it was submitted under the zoo category;
however, last year they were directed by the county to apply under that category.
Nowhere on the application did they choose to be in the zoo category — the committee
assigned the category. The Society has many education programs and sponsors the
only affordable spay/neuter clinic in the county. What now?

« Zootah RAPZ Request — Troy Cooper apologized for missing the RAPZ application
deadline. His assistant, Barbara Tew, had emergency medical issues around the time it
should have been submitted and Cooper failed to file the application. Cooper stressed
that RAPZ funding is essential for the zoo to function. Without RAPZ funding, the zoo
will be closed by July. Cooper asked if there is a way to still apply for funding.

Cooper stated the zoo had a 32% increase in gate admissions this past year with a 4%
increase in revenue overall after expenses. More than 50% of the schools visiting the
zoo come from outside the valley. Many organizations — summer camps, reunions, etc.
— also come to the zoo. The visitors last year were from 48 states and fourteen
countries. The educational programs touched over 30,000 individuals last year and the
zoo had over 67,000 attendees. He works closely with Utah State interns and has a
strong volunteer program with $192,000.00 worth of documented volunteer hours last
year.

In response to Chairman Erickson’s question about employees, Cooper said there are
two full-time employees and ten part time employees and an army of volunteers.

Council member Zilles said the zoo obviously qualifies for funding; the problem is what
can be done because the deadline was missed.

Council member Merrill asked what the policy is on a missed RAPZ application
deadline. Executive Buttars said the Council decides if a late application can be
considered.

Council member Tidwell said she considers the zoo as important as other entities
wanting funding. It draws people to the county and is good for county residents and
their families. She supports allowing consideration of their late application. Council
member Worthen agreed.

Council member White said his grandchildren go to the zoo more than the opera, but he
doesn't like having deadlines and then making exceptions.

The Council will review the situation and let Cooper know their decision in the next few
weeks.

CACHE COUNTY RESOLUTION 2018 - 12
A RESOLUTION RECOGNIZING THE DEVASTATING EFFECTS OF THE OPIOID
CRISIS AND SUPPORTING THE PURSUIT OF LEGAL ACTION AGAINST
MANUFACTURERS AND DISTRIBUTORS OF OPIOIDS

WHEREAS, the Centers for Disease Control has declared that the United States is experiencing

an opioid-induced “public health epidemic;” and

WHEREAS, 91 Americans die each day from opioid-related drug overdose; and

WHEREAS, opioid pain relievers, including oxycodone, hydrocodone, fentanyl, and

hydromorphone are responsible for three-fourths of all prescription drug overdose deaths; and

WHEREAS, nearly one in three Utah adults (32%) were prescribed an opioid pain medication in

2014; and

WHEREAS, Utah has the seventh-highest drug overdose rate in the United States with an
average of six people dying per week (one death nearly every day) and 24 deaths each month as
a result of overdosing on prescription opioids; and

WHEREAS, according to the State Department of Health, the rate of prescription opioids
dispensed in Utah from 2002 to 2015 grew by 29.4%, averaging nearly five opioid prescriptions

per patient in 2015; and

WHEREAS, since 2002, drug poisoning deaths per 100,000 in population have increased at an
alarming rate and prescription opioids have been responsible for more drug deaths in Utah than
all other drug categories, such as benzodiazepines, over-the-counter fnedications, or illicit drugs;

WHEREAS, drug poisoning deaths are a preventable public health problem that has outpaced

deaths from firearms, falls, and motor vehicle crashes in Utah since 2002; and

WHEREAS, Cache County is experiencing opioid-related deaths at the same pace as the rest of

Utah; and

WHEREAS, there are significant economic impacts caused by the opioid epidemic — $55 billion

in health and social costs, and $20 billion in emergency department and inpatient care; and

WHEREAS, counties often bear the brunt of these costs, paying both for the treatment and
incarceration of addicted persons and the deep social cost that the loss of a significant population

of productive community members has in the region; and

WHEREAS, the North American prescription opioid market exploded to a value of $12.4 billion

in 2015 with an expected annual growth rate of 4.6%; and

WHEREAS, prescription opioid manufacturers realized these profits as thousands of individuals

lost their lives, having fallen victim to prescription opioid addictions; and

WHEREAS, practices of manufacturers, distributors, and sellers of opioid painkillers have

contributed to the epidemic; and

WHEREAS, beginning in the 1990s, opioid manufacturers lied to both doctors and the public

WHEREAS, as a direct consequence of over-distribution, addiction has become commonplace,
with death often the ultimate consequence; hard-working Americans have become addicts, first
to the opioids pushed by the drug makers and distributors, and then to street drugs like heroin;

and

WHEREAS, drug companies and distributors spent millions of dollars on promotional activities
and materials that falsely deny or trivialize the risks of opioids while overstating the benefits of
using them for long-term chronic pain, including providing funding to prominent doctors,
medical societies, and patient advocacy groups to win their support for the drugs’ use for chronic
pain; and

WHEREAS, serious allegations have been raised that prescription opioid manufacturers have

systematically engaged in deceptive marketing practices and fraudulent cover-ups to advance the

sale of prescription opioids; and

WHEREAS, to date, over 250 lawsuits have been filed in federal court, with others spread
across the state courts, against opioid manufacturers seeking damages for the public cost of the

opioid crisis, including 16 states (Alaska, Arizona, Delaware, Illinois, Kentucky, Louisiana,

Mississippi, Missouri, New Hampshire, New Mexico, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, South

Carolina, West Virginia, and Washington), and dozens of counties across the country; and

WHEREAS, Cache County has the authority to bring suit on behalf of the public under a variety
of legal theories, including false advertising, abating a public nuisance, and violations of the

Utah Pattern of Unlawful Activity Act.

NOW THEREFORE BE IT RESOLVED that:
Section 1: Cache County shall work on a sustainable, comprehensive public health approach to

the opioid crisis in order to address preventable drug overdose deaths.

Section 2: Cache County shall pursue legal action against drug manufacturers and distributors of

opioids as recommended by the County Attorney.

Section 3: Cache County shall seek the maximum award for damages from prescription opioid
manufacturers to hold them accountable for the destruction and devastation they have inflicted

upon the citizens of Cache County.

Adopted by the County Council of Cache County, Utah, this 22 day of May, 2018.
