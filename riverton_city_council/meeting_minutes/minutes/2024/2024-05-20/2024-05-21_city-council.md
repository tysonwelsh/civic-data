       Accessory Structures Discussion

Kevin Hicks, Interim City Manager, noted that the Council had recently emphasized the need for
greater focus on code enforcement, leading to questions about shipping containers specifically.
He explained that staff intended to provide clarity on how shipping containers are currently
treated under existing codes and ordinances.

Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, presented information about the current
regulations and enforcement concerning shipping containers. He reminded the Council that the
ordinance prohibits storage containers, with exceptions made for temporary use during moves.
Despite their popularity due to low cost and availability, shipping containers can be problematic
because they often appear industrial and unkempt. To address this, the City has adopted a
compromise allowing shipping containers to be repurposed as accessory structures, provided
Page | 2 Minutes – City Council Meeting

they meet certain standards. These standards include matching the exterior materials and
design of existing structures, with a stipulation that metal siding, except for aluminum, is not
permitted. Containers below 200 square feet must be consistent in color and design with the
main dwelling.

Mr. Lethbridge highlighted recent legislative changes which raised the threshold for requiring a
building permit from 200 to 350 square feet for cargo containers, simplifying the compliance
process. This change means most cargo containers no longer need a building permit, easing
some regulatory burdens.

Councilmember McDougal asked how long the original code prohibiting storage containers has
been on the books. In response, Mr. Lethbridge clarified that the specific reference to cargo
containers in the City code was established in 2011. However, even before this date, there was
an ordinance prohibiting metal as an exterior material for structures, which implicitly included
cargo containers. Therefore, cargo containers were never legally permitted as exterior
structures, and this is pertinent to the question of whether existing containers are
"grandfathered" in. Since they were never allowed under the earlier code, containers that have
been on properties for years were never in compliance, even if they went unnoticed.

Councilmembers and staff further discussed the storage container issues. Mr. Lethbridge
confirmed his department is preparing material, including visual aids, to address the issue of
cargo containers. He plans to collaborate with code enforcement to ensure this information is
included in initial contacts with residents who have or are reported to have these containers.
Additionally, he is working with the communications team to make this information available on
the website and other platforms.

Ryan Carter, City Attorney, emphasized the need for better communication between code
enforcement officers and the planning and zoning department to guide residents towards
compliance solutions. He suggested that all staff should provide a consistent message
regarding this issue. He expressed optimism that as this transition progresses and longstanding
issues are resolved, the situation will improve.

         Budget Discussion

Mr. Hicks reminded everyone that the current budget under review includes a Staff column and
a Mayor's column, with the Mayor's column being tentatively approved. As the Council moves
into June and prepares for public hearings, the Mayor’s column will be presented to the public
and is set to be approved by the Council on June 18, unless any changes are proposed. He
emphasized the importance of streamlining the process by discussing and voting on each
proposed item individually.

Councilmember McCay highlighted the importance of the Utah League of Cities and Town’s
(ULCT) lobbying functions and educational support for the City. The ULCT has been effective in
gathering information from partner cities, which has been critical in legislative discussions,
particularly regarding housing density and zoning rights. She suggested reinstating the City's
membership fee which is $34,065.96 for the current year, representing a 2.6% increase from the
previous year. Councilmembers Buroker, Pierucci, McDougal, and Haymond were supportive of
putting this cost back into the budget.

Councilmember Pierucci asked for a discussion about sales tax revenue. Mr. Hicks recalled
how economists had predicted significant drops in sales tax revenue during the pandemic,

which did not materialize as expected. Instead, the City saw strong sales tax revenue due to
residents focusing on home improvements and increased online shopping, resulting in banner
years for the City.

For the previous fiscal year, the budget was based on trends showing an average of 9-10%
increases year over year. However, recent trends indicate that while sales tax revenue isn't
declining, the rate of increase has slowed. This shift has prompted a more conservative
approach for the upcoming fiscal year's budget (FY 25), moving away from the aggressive
projections used for the current year (FY 24). He acknowledged that the previous year's
projections may have been too optimistic and emphasized the importance of ensuring the
revenue estimates are not overly ambitious.

Councilmember McDougal highlighted the unique circumstances surrounding Riverton City's
recent revenue streams, emphasizing the significant influx of funds received during the COVID-
19 pandemic and through other grants. These funds have been instrumental in addressing past
financial gaps. However, he cautioned the Council about the need to adapt spending to match
more realistic tax revenue projections in the future.

Acknowledging the completion of major projects like the Village Center and Costco,
Councilmember McDougal noted that Riverton may not experience another surge in sales tax
revenue within its city boundaries. He commended the Council's proactive approach to using
funds to pay down debt but stressed the importance of transitioning to a more sustainable
financial model.

Councilmember Pierucci proposed allocating $25,000 from Fund 450, the capital improvement
fund, to install a bocce ball court in Riverton City. The location would be decided at a later date.
He highlighted the accessibility and appeal of bocce ball as demonstrated during their
attendance at a Special Olympics tournament. The proposed court would include benches,
trees, and a pathway, with sprinklers to ensure maintenance. He noted that Fund 450 currently
has a remaining balance of $45,111, suggesting that discussions with staff about future park
amenities, such as bocce ball courts, could enhance existing park services for residents.
Councilmember McCay and Buroker were supportive of this request.

Councilmember McDougal expressed dissent regarding the bocce ball courts. His concerns
were primarily about the process and the need for long-term planning. He emphasized the
importance of using planning meetings, such as those held in January, to strategically plan and
prioritize city amenities. He suggested that the proposed $25,000 could be better utilized to
address more pressing needs, such as crosswalks for the new trails. He also pointed out that
there is an existing park project for which a plan has not yet been presented, indicating that this
could be an opportunity to include the bocce ball court as part of the overall park design.

Councilmember Haymond acknowledged the fun and accessibility of bocce ball but expressed
uncertainty about the allocation of funds without a comprehensive plan. He highlighted concerns
about the budget, noting that many funds are decreasing by significant amounts.

In response, Mr. Hicks explained that the projected beginning cash balance for the general fund
(Fund 100) was approximately $8 million, expected to decrease to about $6.1 million by the end
of the fiscal year. The reduction was attributed to the use of fund balance, where savings from
the general fund were being used for operational support and interfund transfers.

         Resolution No. 24-48 – Reaffiming the Policy of Riverton City, Utah, Regarding
         the placement of Campaign Signs on Park Strips Located Adjacent to City-
         Owned Property

Mr. Carter discussed the Utah Code 20A-17-103, which allows governmental entities to permit
the posting of campaign political signage on public property, provided that all candidates are
allowed equal opportunity. Riverton City's current ordinance under 18.150.090 restricts
political signage to private property only. However, the state code, which was enacted after
the city code, overrides this provision.

In 2017, the City Council decided to allow political signage on park strips in front of
governmental property during campaign seasons. Given the turnover in the Council and the
upcoming campaign season, it was proposed to reaffirm this practice through a formal
resolution.
Page | 8 Minutes – City Council Meeting
May 21, 2024




The resolution would direct staff not to enforce the restriction of campaign signage to private
property only, thus allowing signage on public property as per state law. Public Works staff
requested clear guidance on handling these signs during maintenance activities, as they have
encountered issues with maintaining park strips where campaign signs are placed.

The proposed solution was for staff to work around the signs without removing them, even if it
meant occasional minor issues like dead spots in the lawn or excessive grass growth under
the signs.

Councilmember McDougal expressed opposition to allowing political signs on public parkways
and park strips, citing several issues. The concerns included interference with sprinklers and
mowing, as well as potential safety hazards for families and children playing in parks, who
might encounter damaged or knocked-over signs.

Councilmember McDougal emphasized the responsibility that comes with placing signs on
private property, where homeowners take care of the signs by removing and maintaining
them. In contrast, signs on public property tend to be neglected, leading to lingering, outdated
signage.

Councilmember Haymond agreed with Councilmember McDougal, sharing his recent
campaign experience of seeking permission from private property owners to place signs. He
emphasized the importance of involving the community in elections, suggesting that citizens
should actively support candidates by placing signs on their own properties.

Councilmember Pierucci expressed support for the resolution, emphasizing the importance of
protecting free speech rights. He acknowledged that while the government can set time and
place restrictions on speech, such powers should be used sparingly. He argued that the public
benefit of allowing candidates to communicate their messages outweighs the potential issues
of limiting signage to private property. He did not see a problem with campaign signs in park
strips or in front of parks, viewing it as a necessary part of the election process and
communication with residents.

Councilmember Buroker highlighted that while placing signs in parking strips might seem
productive, it is more respectful to obtain permission from property owners. This approach not
only fosters respect for private property but also ensures that all candidates follow the same
rules, creating a fair campaign environment.

Mayor Pro Tempore McCay expressed support for maintaining the current practice of allowing
campaign signs in park strips, which has been in place since 2017. She emphasized the
difficulty of changing this rule, especially during an ongoing election season, which includes
statewide and countywide elections.

Councilmember Pierucci MOVED that the City Council approve Resolution No. 24-48 –
Reaffiming the Policy of Riverton City, Utah, Regarding the placement of Campaign

         Budget Discussion

Councilmember Buroker expressed a desire to incorporate resident feedback into decisions
about recycling by conducting a survey. She emphasized that the recycling program should
operate as a self-sustaining enterprise fund. She concluded by stating that relying on the
general fund to sustain the recycling program is not beneficial for the residents or the City.

Councilmember Haymond expressed a preference for making a decision on recycling without
conducting a survey. He highlighted the importance of understanding the numbers and
complexities involved in the decision, which might not be fully grasped by all residents.

Councilmember McDougal expressed agreement with the idea of making the sanitation fund
an enterprise fund, running independently from the general budget. He highlighted the benefit
of this approach, noting that it would allow for clearer future planning and budgeting without
concerns about costs beyond the City's control.

Councilmember McDougal proposed gathering information from surrounding cities about their
recycling practices to better inform the Council's decision. He reiterated that the two key
issues are establishing the sanitation fund as an enterprise fund and determining how to
manage the resulting cost increases, whether through eliminating recycling, adjusting fees, or
other means.

Mayor Pro Tempore McCay expressed a preference for gathering feedback from residents
through a survey, emphasizing the ease of modern technology for conducting such surveys.
She shared anecdotal evidence from discussions with other Riverton residents, indicating a
preference for keeping recycling despite potential fee increases. She expressed concern
about the current contamination rate of 25.8%, which exceeds the goal of 15%, and
emphasized the need for better resident education on recycling practices. She proposed
setting a goal to reduce the contamination rate to 15% or less within three to six months, with
Page | 10 Minutes – City Council Meeting
May 21, 2024




a commitment to reevaluate the situation if the goal is not met.

Councilmember Pierucci emphasized the importance of engaging residents in decision-making
processes and finding innovative ways to involve them. He was not opposed to sending out a
survey, even if it might not be academically sound, to gauge public sentiment on the proposed
changes to the recycling program.

Mr. Hicks noted that the Mayor's budget still includes a small subsidy for the sanitation fund.
This subsidy is due to the inclusion of six months of curbside recycling service. It was also
clarified that the sanitation fund is already considered an enterprise fund. Therefore, the
discussion should focus on whether to make it fully self-sustaining without any subsidies from
other funds.

Councilmember Pierucci expressed support for the goal of making the sanitation fund fully
self-sustaining while maintaining recycling. He favored eliminating curbside recycling in favor
of a regional model but stressed the necessity of engaging in more active discussions with the
public on this topic and other significant issues.

Mr. Hicks conveyed that Waste Management emphasized the company's commitment to
partnering with the City to educate the public on recycling. Waste Management has various
tools and educational materials available and aims to work collaboratively with the City to
ensure residents understand what can and cannot be recycled.

Councilmember Haymond expressed concern about the public's reaction to ending curbside
recycling, even with a regional model. He shared his personal reluctance to take recyclables to
a regional site, suggesting people might opt to dispose of them as trash instead. He proposed
being transparent about the costs of recycling and allowing residents to decide if they want to
pay for it. Additionally, he stressed the importance of maintaining and even increasing the
City's savings, advocating for a robust savings account that could cover a full year's budget.

Councilmember McDougal shared his concerns about the City's financial situation, highlighting
that despite having $6 million in the budget, the City is also burdened with $30 million in debt,
incurring interest rates of three to five percent. He stressed the importance of reducing this
debt to save residents from paying interest.

Councilmember McDougal expressed skepticism about conducting a public survey on
recycling, noting the complexity of the issue and the difficulty in fully informing the public
through a survey. He pointed out that the perception of landfill disposal as inherently harmful
to the environment is not entirely accurate, as many materials decompose and produce
methane gas, which is used to generate power. He also mentioned that the recycling industry
is heavily influenced by national politics and federal subsidies, which can change with different
administrations. This political influence can lead to fluctuations in what materials are accepted
for recycling, further confusing residents.

Mayor Pro Tempore McCay suggested that the City's communication department could create
a visually appealing and informative chart to help residents understand the costs and benefits
of recycling. She highlighted that if recycling were eliminated, residents might end up needing
an additional garbage can, effectively increasing their monthly costs by $3.50. She advocated
for educating residents about the costs and benefits of recycling and involving them in the
decision-making process through surveys.
