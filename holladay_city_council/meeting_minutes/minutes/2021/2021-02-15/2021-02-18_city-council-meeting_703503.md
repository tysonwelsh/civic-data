VII.    Consideration of Ordinance 2021-01 Amending the Zoning Map for Property Located at
        4545, 4549, and 4551 South Highland Drive from R-2-10 (Duplex/Townhome) Zone to RM
        (Residential Multi-Family).
The Planning Commission vote was 5-to-2 and there were differing opinions on whether the rezone
was appropriate. The desire was to ensure that the applicant as well as the neighbors who weighed
in on the application understood that Council Members were giving the matter their full attention.

Council Member Petersen agreed with the Mayor and did not consider this to be a black and white
issue. She took recommendations from the Planning Commission seriously but in general, was very
cautious about rezones. She was a firm believer in capturing unclaimed density first and foremost
before coming to the Council for a rezone request. The property is in the R-2-10 Zone with three
single-family homes on it. Her recollection was that it was a 0.95-acre property, which would allow
seven or eight units. She was not concerned about the height or traffic. She preferred to start with
the unclaimed density before considering a rezone that would allow up to 14 units on the 0.95 acres.
There was another area with RM zoning nearby, however, the subject property was primarily
surrounded by R-2-10. She did not believe there had been a compelling argument that this property
needed to be rezoned in order for density to be claimed. She had spent a lot of time considering the
issue but was not persuaded to grant the rezone.

Mayor Dahle did not feel it was a clear-cut request and stated that there was language in the General
Plan and the Highland Drive Master Plan that supported capturing the density that was already
entitled. He Council Member Petersen’s position since the applicants had an opportunity to double
the units that are currently entitled on the parcel. He saw no reason to develop 14 units on less than
one acre in the middle of neighborhoods that are all zoned R-2-10.

Council Member Fotheringham interpreted the General Plan and the Highland Drive Master Plan
differently. He also took what existed across the street, which was highly commercial, into
consideration. He agreed that development along Highland Drive was different than a rezone inside
of a neighborhood as it was on a major arterial and had commercial across the street. Council Member

        b.      Legislative Update.
Mr. Godfrey shared legislative updates with the City Council. He reported that the main bills the City
was tracking related to billboards and Accessory Dwelling Units (“ADU”). The ADU bill was HB
82. It made an internal ADU a permitted use in any residential zone. The negotiated solution would
require that a city approve internal ADUs within 75% of the area of its primary residential zones.

Another bill that the City was tracking was SB 61, which was related to billboards. S.B. 61 focused
on the conversion of existing billboards to digital billboards. The original form of the bill would
allow for the conversion of any existing billboard to digital. The form of the bill that was currently
being discussed would create a provision that stated: “if there was not a zone in the City that allowed
on-premise signs that were digital changing copy or mechanical changing copy, billboards with digital
changing copy, or mechanical changing copy would not need to be permitted”. Discussions were
ongoing.

Council Member Durham wondered if there was language regarding on-premise digital signs. He
wanted to know if it was limited to businesses or if it also included schools and churches. Mr. Godfrey
explained that the latest version of the bill created an allowance where the on-premise digital sign
must be a certain size before it would trigger the billboard conversion. City Council Members may
be asked to make their voice heard about how damaging digital billboards could be to the city by
attending meetings or contacting local representatives and legislators.

The last bill that the City was tracking was H.B. 98. The bill related to inspection and plan review
timelines and would prohibit cities from imposing design standards on developments. Mr. Godfrey
reported that the two elements of the bill (the inspection and plan review timelines and the design
standard element) had been separated. Mr. Godfrey discussed the timelines for inspection and plan
review. The bill stated that if the city was unable to meet the three-day timeline, the permit holder
had a right to ask the City to hire an outside inspector or to hire their own. As for the plan review, if
the City was unable to meet the first 14-day or 21-day timeline and the permit holder asked for a plan
review, there was an additional 14 days or 21 days. If the review was not done within the second
timeframe, a property owner would have the right to bring in a stamped plan from an engineer or
architect. Mr. Godfrey commented that on paper, those changes seemed significant. However, he
wasn’t sure that they would make much of a difference in practice.

couple of years ago, residents had shared dissatisfaction with the multi-sport tile. City staff had been

additional options were explored. Ms. Smith noted that the existing pickleball tiles could be removed
and an acrylic court applied over the concrete. That project would cost an estimated $15,000 with a
lifespan of approximately 7 to 10 years.

Council Member Petersen felt it was important to do our due diligence to pursue a different option
but wanted to know if this was the most reasonable solution. Pickleball courts often have an asphalt

Fotheringham did not want there to be a repeat of the same problem. He suggested that some residents
try out a similar type of court and share feedback.

       a.      Discussion on Previous Public Hearings.
               •      Text Amendments
Planning Manager, Jon Teerlink discussed the text amendment related to development near
waterways. The amendment would provide guidance to property owners when they proposed
development within proximity to waterways and canals. Currently established ordinances required
that anything proposed within 100 feet of a waterway or within 20 feet of a canal must be reviewed
and approved by the Planning Commission. The text amendment would codify a set of standards that
any resident could look to for guidance. The proposed draft amendment recommended by the
Planning Commission established two scenarios - development near a natural waterway and
development near a manmade waterway. It also established a set of activities and land uses that could
and could not be done within certain distances.

Council Member Fotheringham asked about enforcement issues. He wondered what would happen
with a neighbor who already had a lot of development in a waterway and a new homeowner who
wanted to do something similar but was not allowed. Mr. Teerlink acknowledged that there could be
difficulties and enforcement was a challenge.

Mr. Teerlink discussed the text amendment related to the Holladay Village Zone. The language has
been changed to alter “should” to “shall” in many cases. The Planning Commission also updated
lighting standards to become more pedestrian-oriented. Mr. Teerlink explained that the amendments
included technical updates and clarifications.

Mr. Teerlink discussed the text amendment related to the regulation of tobacco sales. He noted that
the City does not allow specialty tobacco retailers, but a minor amendment had been made to update
the minimum age requirement for purchase from 19 years old to 21 years old.

        b.     Grants Update.

approximately 71%. Most grant programs have a success rate of approximately 50%, which meant
that the City was well above average. Most of the grants that the City was able to secure had a low

Smith shared slides about the top five grant awards received, specific grant-funded projects as well

continue to be important to the City.

that there were two tools on the website: iWorkQ Citizen Request Application and Report a Traffic
