 1   actual impact but acknowledging the value in terms of service provided—specifically the number
 2   of cans residents receive and how much they pay for that service. While supportive of a fee

10   Staff presented three possible sanitation fee structures in response to the request for a tiered
11   approach targeting heavier users. The first model proposes a $1 increase to the base rate (from
12   $13 to $14), a $3 hike for homes with one additional garbage can (raising the total increase to
13   $4 for them), and a jump from $7 to $12.50 for households with more than one extra garbage
14   can. The additional recycling can fee would increase to $2.50. This model would nearly break
15   even with only a minimal General Fund transfer or none at all, using rounding in the fund. The
16   second model—originally recommended—applied a flat $2.50 increase to all can types, bringing
17   the base rate to $15.50 and resulting in a $120,000 fund balance contribution. The third model
18   showed a true break-even scenario with a uniform $2.10 increase (base fee to $15.10,
19   additional garbage to $9.10, and additional recycle to $7.10), without contributing to fund
20   balance.
21
22   Mayor Staggs expressed support for separating out the cost of the first garbage and recycle
23   cans to improve transparency for residents, suggesting that the current bundled fee obscures
24   the true cost of recycling, which is nearly double the collection cost of garbage. He
25   recommended charging a distinct, visible amount for recycling within the base fee—e.g., $10 for
26   garbage and $5 for recycling—to better inform residents, especially ahead of future opt-in or
27   opt-out decisions.
28
29   Staff clarified that while they can split the fee, they can’t accommodate requests to remove the
30   recycling charge under the current contract, but agreed that doing so could initiate important
31   dialogue.

33   Councilmember Pierucci expressed full support for fee transparency and welcomed public input
34   on the direction of the City’s recycling and sanitation services. He agreed with the Mayor's call
35   to separate and clearly identify the costs of garbage and recycling services, emphasizing that
36   such transparency is essential for informed public dialogue. He also acknowledged that under
37   the proposal to eliminate curbside recycling, residents would lose a can while still paying the
38   same base fee—highlighting the importance of weighing service loss against cost stability. He
39   favored a gradual, more sustainable approach that considers residents’ financial constraints
40   while building toward long-term viability.
41
42   The Council discussed various sanitation fee structures, with general agreement on moving
43   toward a self-sustaining model while allowing for potential cancellations and the need for more
44   data. Most Councilmembers supported a tiered approach where those using more cans pay
45   more, and some favored breaking out recycling fees for transparency—though concerns were
46   raised that this might prompt residents to request removal of recycling services. Staff was asked

 8            Ordinance No. 25-12 – Amending the Commercial Architectural Standards in
 9            Section 18.65.060, 18.70.060, 18.75.050, 18.80.060, 18.85.060, 18.95.040,
10            18.100.060, and 18.215.030 of the Riverton City Code
11
12   Jason Lethbridge, Development Services Director, provided a summary of recent changes to
13   the architectural standards ordinance based on prior Council feedback. The requirement for
14   consideration of all building sides was clarified from "equal consideration" to "consistent
15   architectural consideration" to prevent overly strict standards for rear facades, while still
16   ensuring cohesive use of materials. The previous limitations on the amount of glass were
17   removed; glass is now generally permitted as a material, with some discretionary interpretation
18   by the City. For precast concrete, new exceptions allow for less detailed treatment on side and
19   rear walls, again at the City's discretion. Additionally, within a designated downtown area—
20   between 1830 W and 1300 W, from Peterson’s Market to the Senior Center—brick will now be a
21   required material, though no set percentage is mandated. Instead, its use will be assessed for
22   visual cohesion with surrounding buildings.
23
24   Councilmember Buroker explained her reasoning for advocating brick as a required material in
25   what she proposed be named the “Civic Center” area, rather than simply downtown. She shared
26   that the Peterson family, who developed the prominent clock tower building on 12600 South,
27   had expressed frustration that they were held to a higher architectural standard than newer
28   developments in the area. To address that and promote fairness and consistency, she
29   supported requiring brick for future buildings in that zone. She also expressed support for the
30   revised four-sided architecture language, noting it strikes a fair balance. Lastly, she requested
31   clarification on the exceptions to precast concrete treatment requirements.
32
33   In response, Mr. Lethbridge explained that while precast concrete walls still require treatment
34   (such as brick veneer or form patterns), the new exceptions apply only in certain zones
35   (commercial regional and PCC) and allow for discretion on side and rear walls that are not
36   visible from the street. This was done to prevent unnecessary costs in areas like loading docks
37   while still maintaining visual quality on public-facing sides.
38
39   Councilmember McCay expressed support for the recent architectural code changes overall,
40   particularly the removal of the limitation on glass. She proposed updating the language in
41   section 6A to read: “All mechanical equipment shall be located in the back or screened so as to
42   shield visibility.” The intent was to clarify that equipment placed at the back of a building and not
43   visible does not also need to be screened. She also voiced opposition to the new requirement
44   for brick as a mandatory material in the downtown area. She argued that modern architecture
45   includes a wide variety of appealing materials and felt that requiring brick would unnecessarily
46   limit architectural creativity. She recommended removing the brick requirement entirely.
47
48   Mayor Staggs clarified the language regarding mechanical equipment placement. The intent of
49   the current code is that if equipment is located at the back of a building and not visible, the
50   building itself acts as a screen, and no additional screening is required.
