HB 82 — Single Family Housing Modifications:
This is the “ADU” bill. It requires the city to allow internal accessory dwelling units (“IADU”)
as a permitted use in primarily residential zones. It preempts most design regulations,
such as unit size, lot size, and frontage, but does allow cities to impose parking standards
and occupancy requirements. The bill also establishes an enforcement process specific
to IADUs and modifies building code requirements.

HB 98 — Local Government Building Regulations:
This bill has two major components: a change to building permit reviews/inspections and
preemption of certain zoning ordinance requirements.
For building permits and inspections, developers may hire a 3% party inspector if the city
takes too long to complete inspections. In that event, the developer must notify the city
that they are using a 3" party inspector and the 3 party inspector is authorized to issue
certificates of occupancy after all inspections are completed. Additionally, the bill defines
what items a city can require for building permit submittals and waives the permit
requirement for certain residential buildings damaged by a natural disaster.
On the zoning ordinance side, this bill prohibits a city from regulating building design
elements for 1- and 2-family houses or townhomes unless done so as part of planned unit
development in exchange for a benefit to the developer. Building design elements
includes:
* Exterior color and material,
* Roof design,
* Nonstructural architectural ornamentation,
« Location and design of windows and doors, including garage doors,
* Number, type, and interior layout of rooms,
* Minimum size,
* Rear yard landscaping requirements,

One of the more challenging elements in this bill is the types of units that are affected.
. The preemption changes in the bill apply to “one to two family dwelling or townhome.”

This term is not defined and has caused some confusion. A strict reading of this clause

would cause it to affect all townhome developments, excluding those in planned unit

development, because every townhome is a one-family townhome, regardless of the

number of units attached. A less strict reading jrould limit application of the clause to only

those buildings that have 1 or 2 units in them. The difference in interpretation will affect

how many zones are affected by the building design element preemption: the looser

reading will affect fewer zones while the stricter reading will affect more.

One approach to resolve the issue is to revise all references to “townhome” to

“multifamily” uses. The city can still imposy building design element regulations on

multifamily uses, so this change would preserve greater housing options. Alternatively,

all townhome uses could be removed from city code, reducing the number of uses and

project types available.

HB 409 — Municipal and County Land Use Development Revisions:
This bill affects several areas in state code affecting planning and zoning efforts, including
planning commissions, development agreements, conditional use permits, subdivisions,
lot line adjustments, and residential street standards.
For planning commissions, new annual training requirements are added. Commissioners
are required to complete 4 hours of training each year, with at least 1 hour on LUDMA. 1
hour can be credited by attending at least 12 planning commission meetings in a calendar
year. The remaining time can be obtained from staff trainings, conferences, seminars,
etc. All new commissioners must receive the first hour of LUDMA training before sitting
for their first meeting.
The new development agreement (“DA”) language refines standards and clarifies
procedures. DAs continue to be an option for cities, but a city cannot require a DA as the
only option to develop land. Further, a DA that deviates from the established ordinance
must go through the same process as a text amendment before it can be approved.
Conditional use permits must now use “objective” standards. Additionally, “substantial
evidence” now has a meaning (evidence that is “beyond a scintilla and a reasonable mind
would accept as adequate to support a conclusion.”) These changes have a significant
impact on conditional use permit standards in city code, as many of our current standards
are not as objective as they should be. Staff is considering consolidating use
requirements throughout the ordinance to minimize repetition and administrative burden
caused by code amendments.
Subdivision and lot line adjustment changes are included that reduce the burden for
developers seeking approvals. Additional exceptions are added to the subdivision
process, allowing property owners to divide land if the recorded document states the
division (1) is in anticipation of future land use approvals on the parcel(s), (2) does not
confer any land use approvals, and (3) has not been approved by the city. The lot line
adjustment language describes processes by which property owners can complete lot
line adjustments without city input. Cities may require review and approval of lot line
adjustments affecting lots with houses on them; otherwise, approval cannot be required.
Finally, a new provision has been added that prohibits the city from imposing new zoning
ordinance provisions to a subdivision for 10 years after the plat is recorded. This will 1
require us to figure out how to track approval dates and zoning ordinance versions better
than we have in the past to minimize the administrative burden.
