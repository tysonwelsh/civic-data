7. PUBLIC HEARING, CONSIDERATION, AND RECOMMENDATION on a Proposed

Text Amendment to Chapter 11.220.030 to add a definition for “Gas Station Electronic

Display Sign” and to Chapter 11.220.070.A to allow electronic display signs for gas

stations with a minimum display interval of three (3) seconds. (Application 2026-10).

Applicant, Yesco. Staff Presenter, Ellen Oakman.
Associate City Planner, Ellen Oakman, presented the Staff Report and reported that the Text
Amendment was requested by Yesco on behalf of Maverik, Inc. to allow electronic display signs
specifically for gas station monument signs. The proposed new definition and sign category generally
mirrored existing language for static electronic numeric display signs, with the primary difference
being the allowance of a three-second display interval rather than the 12-hour interval allowed for
static signs. The proposed definition was as follows:

Bluffdale City Code §11.220.030: Definitions
GAS STATION ELECTRONIC DISPLAY SIGNS:
An electronic message center sign that:

e Is incorporated into a monument sign and does not occupy more than 50% of the entire sign

face;

e Changes display no more frequently than once every three seconds;

e Does not scroll, flash, blink, emit sound, or include animated copy or graphics.
The proposed gas station electronic display signs would be limited to gas station monument signs and
have a minimum three-second display interval. Electronic display signs use the NIT brightness
standards to measure how bright the display appears. By contrast, electronic message centers have a
minimum eight-second display interval and are measured using foot-candle brightness standards.
In response to a question raised by Commissioner Flynn, Ms. Oakman confirmed that static signs
require a 12-hour display interval. Electronic message centers are largely prohibited in the City, but
allow an eight-second interval. She spoke with the Engineering Department regarding safety
standards, and they indicated that they had no concerns with the three-second interval for this sign
category.
Commissioner Swanson asked for examples of static electronic signs. Ms. Oakman reported that
banks or other businesses utilize that type of sign. In response to a follow-up question, Community
and Economic Development Director, Grant Crowell, stated that digital clocks are not static, but there
may be one legal nonconforming clock sign in the City. Bluffdale had worked over the years to
clarify its policy on electronic message centers and their full animation. Gas station signs were first
manual, then static electronic, and the proposal was in response to the next step in their evolution. It ;
was important to avoid content regulation.
Skyler Walser spoke on behalf of the applicant and indicated that three seconds is longer than it may
seem. Maverik had implemented a companywide change with lower pricing for Nitro rewards
program members, and they want to display the different pricing. He then presented a video of the

* Parcels between two and five acres in size are limited to a density of 1.5 dwelling units per
acre.

In response to a question raised by Commissioner Swanson, Mr. Crowell reported that the General
Plan Map includes a Very Low Density Residential (“VLDR”) designation for a density of up to one
unit per acre, which is prevalent on the west side of the City. The Low Density Residential (“LDR”)
designation allows up to four units per acre, which includes the existing R-1-10 Zones. The text of
the General Plan provides the City’s recommended intent for each designation, and City Ordinance
indicates that zoning should conform with the General Plan. If the new Zoning Table were approved,
the issue would need to be addressed at some point. Unless the VLDR designation was redefined,
the new R2 and R3 Zones should only be applied in LDR areas.
Commissioner Flynn asked if the footnotes included in text would be better served in the General
Plan. Mr. Crowell reported that their purpose was to serve as criteria to determine if an application
was valid, and the concept of applicability was better suited to the General Plan.
Ms. Tubbs reviewed the text descriptions of General Plan categories. WLDR equates to less than one
dwelling unit per acre, or the current R-1-43/proposed R1 Zone. LDR allows one to four dwelling
units per acre, or the current R-1-10/proposed R4 Zone and infill overlays.
Commissioner Flynn remarked that the new residential zones will actually help the City. Smaller lot
developments currently required a Special District, and the new zoning would clearly define the
requirements. Mr. Crowell confirmed that Special Districts are very complex. The footnotes provide
the criteria for application, but do not guarantee that the application will be favorably received. An
applicant could currently apply for R-1-10 zoning anywhere in the City, but the likelihood of approval
may be very low. The City did not yet know whether there would be a discernible pattern to create a
General Plan amendment, and he appreciated the uncertainty that created.

Residential Text Amendment

Dear Planning Commission Members,

I am writing to strongly oppose the proposed residential text amendments that would allow

technical classification — it directly influences financial decisions, long-term planning, and
expectations about neighborhood stability. When residents invest in property based on
established zoning, there is a reasonable expectation that those standards will not be continually
reduced over time.

originally anticipated. That change was deeply unpopular, and many residents felt blindsided.

that undermine the original vision for this area.

is necessary to meet broader goals. However, over time, these cumulative changes significantly
alter the character of established neighborhoods. At some point, the City must stand by its long-
term planning commitments and protect the expectations of current residents.

This area was never intended to evolve into high-density residential by gradual amendment.
Increasing density yet again will affect traffic, infrastructure strain, stormwater management, and
overall neighborhood cohesion. More importantly, it erodes trust in the planning process.

I respectfully urge the Commission to reject this amendment and preserve compatibility with
existing lot sizes and neighborhood character. Stability and predictability in zoning decisions are
critical to maintaining community confidence and protecting property investments.
