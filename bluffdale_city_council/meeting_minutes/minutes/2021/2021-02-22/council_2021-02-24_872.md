         a.     Discussion Related to Transportation Access Planning, Staff Presenter,
                Michael Fazio.

City Engineer, Michael Fazio reported that the corridor along 2700 West is becoming a hot spot
for development. Several developers have expressed interest in the area and as a result, he
requested that Ryan Hales from Hales Engineering assist in studying the corridor to better
understand how to organize development and access. Mr. Hales reported on the issue of access
management.

Mr. Hales explained that access management balances the mobility of traffic with the need for
access, which is a significant benefit for any development. The concept of access management
using the example of a freeway has increased mobility and faster speeds, yet limits access. As you
go down the scale to local streets, there is increasing access with reduced mobility or speeds.

Mr. Hales explained that access management also looks at signal spacing and access spacing. The
more conflict points on a given roadway, the more potential for collisions. A good access
management policy minimizes this vehicular conflict, which improves safety.

Mayor Timothy commented that every time the City has added a signal within the Utah Department
of Transportation ("UDOT") system, they spend a lot of money to connect the signal to UDOT's
system. He asked about the importance of signal spacing if the signals are timed. Mr. Hales
explained that when there is equidistant spacing between signals, there should be a better
progression through the signal system. If the signals are closer than they would normally want,
the communication between the two signals is not as efficient. They time the signals so that when
a light turns green at one signal, it begins to dissipate traffic in time for the approaching traffic to
go through the green light. He acknowledged that the coordination of the signals is not always
accomplished. He used the example at 14600 South, and 14800 South, which are four-way
intersections.

Mr. Fazio added that there is a flaw in the system because it waits until the light is triggered rather
than having mid-block detection, which would be ideal. He explained that UDOT is invested in
dead-end detection. Mr. Hales added that the current system would be more efficient if there were
four-way intersections along this corridor with equidistant spacing because it is easier to time the
lights to keep the flow of traffic moving.

Mr. Hales discussed the example of University Avenue in Provo. The signals are timed so that
traffic flows through a 30-second bandwidth. This means that if a vehicle starts from a red light
and maintains the correct speed, it will not hit another red light along that corridor. The lights
were timed to control the speeds along that corridor. Mr. Hales stated that for the most part, UDOT
does a good job of coordinating signals but in some cases, it needs work to make it function even
better.

Mr. Hales explained that they want to strike a balance between flow and progression along the
corridor, while at the same time providing access for commercial properties. He used an example
of poor access management on State Street in Orem and highlighted the multiple curb cuts along
that corridor.

Mr. Hales next presented an example of good access management in Saratoga Springs. In trying
to move a large amount of traffic through a corridor, an efficient access management program
helps accomplish that while maintaining the investment in the roadway for a longer period of time.
The more curb cuts along a corridor, the more it slows down vehicles, which results in more friction
that clogs the corridor.

Mr. Hales described negative and positive offsets. A negative offset in a three-lane condition has
overlapping left turns that create a high potential for vehicle crashes. A positive offset allows left
turns with enough distance between to not overlap. The intent is to avoid negative offsets and add
more positive offsets. The best alignments involve intersections that line up with each other.

Mr. Hales reported that in Bluffdale, 2700 West is a major collector corridor as identified on the
Transportation Master Plan. The spacing is designated with a minimal signal spacing of 1320 feet,
minimal street spacing of 660 feet, and commercial access spacing of 3 30 feet. He highlighted the
latter two numbers.

He explained that UDOT has its own access management standards. In the case of Bangerter
Highway and 2700 West there is a future planned· interchange, so UDOT wants to ensure that the
cross-streets flow well into the interchange. It was noted by Mayor Timothy that they want to
ensure that the proposed interchange is not just a flyover. Mr. Hales indicated that access
management guidelines are mandated by State law and bind the developer. If the Access
Management Plan deviates from State law, they are required to obtain a written variance.

Mr. Hales explained that there is a corner clearance requirement on the radius of interchange access
ramps delineating how close to the interchange the first access point can be installed. If the
clearance requirement is not met, the developer is required to go through a lengthy process to
establish how they can offset the negative impacts of the close proximity.

He reiterated that UDOT's standards are similar to those included in Bluffdale's Transportation
Master Plan. UDOT's standards require a minimum interchange to crossroad access spacing of
500 feet. With respect to the plan for 2700 West, 13900 South is located 680 feet south of the
interchange. The intersections are planned as full access intersections with the other access points
being restricted. Here, there is access for the existing buildings that is very close to the proposed
interchange, and UDOT may suggest a raised median through the area due to the close proximity
of the existing access points.

Mr. Hales showed what the access points would look like in this area, assuming a full interchange.
They have also contemplated the potential for a roundabout in this location. He noted that they
are presenting options and alternatives to consider as a City, recognizing the requirements that
UDOT might place on the access points near this proposed interchange.

Mayor Timothy remarked that it makes sense to have a plan so that when developers come in, they
already know what the plan entails. He used the example of connecting commercial areas with a
single access point. Mr. Hales agreed and noted that sometimes a City does not have that
oppo1iunity. He stressed the importance of looking at each development as it comes in and
evaluating whether there are cross-access easements that could be implemented to allow the flow
of traffic. He recognized the difficult decisions involved in balancing the desire for economic
development with good access management.

It was noted that the City Code requires connectivity be addressed by developers. Mr. Hales
suggested that the City Attorney be consulted to determine whether the City could mandate cross-
access for commercial development. The Council discussed whether mandating access, especially
for existing developments, constitutes a taking. Mayor Timothy suggested that they take steps to
ensure that funds are available for connectivity to ensure that these areas are developed properly.

4.     Presentation and Update on UTA's Microtransit Pilot and the Future of
       FrontRunner, Presenter, Carlton Christensen, UTA Board.

Carlton Christensen, Utah Transit Authority ("UTA") Board Chair reported that the prior year
began with a lot of optimism, including a planned celebration of their 50th anniversary as an agency
providing service along the Wasatch Front. Due to the pandemic, many of their plans were
canceled. In November 2019 they began an on-demand service provided by Via, a third-party
microtransit service provider. It allowed for service in many areas that historically did not have
sufficient transit service. The service met and exceeded their projected demands. In early March
2020, they were very good numbers just before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Mr. Christensen explained that coverage for the service is primarily intended to allow someone to
get to a connection point within the transit system. It also provides intercommunity transportation
within the geo-coded area. They have seen improved customer service as a result of the program.
Mr. Christensen reported that the technology provided by Via allows a user to request a service
that arrives within a 15-minute time frame. Prior to the pandemic, the service was contemplated
to include shared rides, but as a result of the pandemic, they reduced the number of passengers.

Due to COVID-19, they experienced a significant decline in ridership. They went from around
180,000 passengers per day to 42,000. Those numbers have steadily increased in recent months,
and on some routes, they have higher service than they did last March. The service also helped
identify many of their key routes where essential workers are dependent on transit services.

Mr. Christensen reported that since COVID-19, they have improved their cleaning and safety
procedures. They suspended many of their marketing efforts so the service may not have been as
well-known last year. He presented statistics on the rate of increase from inception of the program
to early March 2020. They noticed that from March 2020 on, the Wheelchair Accessible Vehicles
("WAV") rides were essential and allowed many individuals who previously did not qualify for
paratransit service to have accessible rides to their destinations.

Mr. Christensen presented the March 2020 numbers and stated that it was important to factor in
that prior to this service there was no transit service in these locations. Within a few months, it
was determined to be a good way for residents to access the UTA system. Mr. Christensen
presented a map of the geo-coded areas where riders can access the service. It picks up riders
within a short distance of where the ride was requested. He also presented a map depicting
pinpointed destinations in one day's activity. The map showed a high concentration of use at
transit stations, with secondary locations at many local businesses.

Mr. Christensen stated that originally the pilot program was scheduled to last one year. Given the
pandemic, they extended the pilot program through August 2021. The UTA Board recently
adopted a five-year service plan that includes microtransit service not only in Bluffdale but other
areas within the six-county region that is serviced by UTA. While they are calling this program a
pilot program, he did not see UTA pulling away from it. They are currently in the procurement
process of seeing how they might utilize this service in other areas that do not lend themselves to
traditional transit service. He reported that while Bluffdale does not have any flex routes, some of
these routes in other communities might be suspended sometime between this spring and August.
They plan to transition those riders to the microtransit service.

Mr. Christensen reported that they have integrated their electronic fare medium to this service and
employers or students at one of the partner institutions are already integrated into the app. They
are also trying to connect this service with out-of-area paratransit services. In response to an
inquiry from Mayor Timothy, Mr. Christensen stated that UTA will evaluate whether to bring this
microtransit service in-house versus continuing to utilize a third-party provider. For the short term,
they will utilize the third-party provider. He noted that the third-party provider adds a lot of
flexibility in tenns of scheduling and drivers. Additionally, the technology associated with the
service is proprietary to third-party providers.

Mr. Christensen explained that the app allows users to rate their experience and they are seeing
ratings in the 4.8 to 4.9 out of 5.0 range. The average response time is 11 to 13 minutes. He noted
that the vendor they currently use provides this service worldwide and one of their main customer
service facilities is located in Lehi. Mr. Christensen stated that the Governor has recently proposed
a $350 million investment into FrontRunner. He reported that FrontRunner is the backbone of

UTA 's system, but they are limited because it is primarily a single-track system. As a result, the

Mr. Christensen noted that with double-tracking, they are looking at more frequent service, adding

include improvement of air quality, enhancing safety at crossings, and serving as the backbone for

plan and coordinating some of the business needs around the enhancement. They have contracted
with a world-renowned company that perfonns operation modeling that will help guide UTA to

the timeframe for these activities, which includes the goal of beginning construction in late 2022.

In response to an inquiry, Mr. Christensen stated that the cost of the project as mandated by the
Legislature would be $350 million, with the total project costing $450 million. He added that UTA
will pursue federal funding and other potential funding sources to bridge the gap. Legislative
leadership strongly supports the project so they remain cautiously optimistic that the funding will
be approved as requested.

Council Member Kallas asked if additional stations and electrification is included in the $350
million project scope. Mr. Christensen responded that the project scope does not include
electrification, but they are looking at other types of trains, and diesel-generated electricity is
increasingly a more viable option. He added that in the future, the project could involve the
addition of stations. He noted that they cannot add stations until the double-tracking system is in
place. UTA will not double track the entire route but will to the degree they can.

Mayor Timothy recalled the presentation made to the Wasatch Front Regional Council and
addressed the upgrade to the engines, which was approved because electrification was predicted
far into the future. Mr. Christensen confirmed that electrification is still further down the line but
explained that they are in the process of overhauling the engines to be fuel-injected, more efficient,
and cleaner burning. Both electrified and diesel-generated cars can run on the same system. They
can still utilize current trains and potentially new trains on the same system. He concluded by
stating that there is a lot oflife remaining in the current trains, especially with the engine overhauls.

require the Council to approve or deny any other project. The City Council possesses broad
discretion pursuant to Utah case law and Code.
