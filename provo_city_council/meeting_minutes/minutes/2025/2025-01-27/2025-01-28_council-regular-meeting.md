1         A resolution authorizing the acceptance of a loan from the state infrastructure bank for the
          Provo airport. (24-003) 0:12:55

     Motion:      An implied motion to approve Resolution 2025-1, as currently constituted, has been
                  made by council rule.

Brian Torgersen, Airport Director, presented. He provided an update on the airport expansion project,
focusing on the engineered fill process and funding needed for final pavement construction. The fill has
settled as expected, making the area ready for construction. However, while $16.9 million in funding has
been awarded from the Mountainland Association of Governments (MAG), those funds will not be
available until 2028. To proceed without delay, the city is securing an interim loan of $14.5 million from
the State Infrastructure Bank. The difference between the loan amount and the MAG award accounts
for interest costs, which will be repaid using the MAG funds. The project is set to begin as soon as
weather conditions permit, with work commencing when the ground is no longer frozen. U-DOT has
offered the option of a single balloon payment for the loan, aligning with the expansion timeline. The
loan carries a favorable interest rate of 3.08%, making it a beneficial financing option.

2         An ordinance updating and clarifying standards related to noise. (25-009) 0:19:12

     Motion:      An implied motion to approve Ordinance 2025-8, as currently constituted, has been
                  made by council rule.

Michael Sanders, Council Policy Analyst, presented. He provided an update on revisions to the proposed
noise ordinance, following direction from the Council at the December 17 work meeting. The key
changes include exemptions for stadium noise, with Subsection B exempting noise from official inter-
school athletic competitions at sports venues on educational institution property, and Subsection C
allowing other stadium activities to be exempt between 7 AM and 10 PM if aligned with the stadium's
purpose, with events past 10 PM requiring a permit from the mayor’s office. Additional exemptions
were also introduced, including noise from snow and ice removal equipment (Subsection G) and
landscaping equipment operating between 7 AM and 10 PM (Subsection H). Other adjustments include
the removal of a previously included 10-minute sound break for indoor noise exceeding 105 decibels.
Additionally, all references to ambient noise have been temporarily removed due to a lack of
confirmation from the police department on the feasibility of measuring it with phone-based apps; if
confirmed, these references may be reinstated through a future amendment. The ordinance was
presented to the Neighborhood District One Board, where concerns were raised about the lack of a
notification system for special exemptions and the mayor’s authority to grant exemptions.

Councilor Whipple questioned the need for a landscaping equipment exemption, noting that some
equipment, such as leaf blowers, can operate quietly. She asked why an exemption would be necessary
if the work could be completed without excessive noise.

Brian Jones, City Attorney, responded that the landscaping equipment exemption is a policy decision for
the Council to consider. He explained that it was not originally suggested by the committee but was
included as part of BYU’s proposal due to the volume of landscaping work they conduct. He clarified that
the Council has the authority to modify or remove the exemption if desired.

Councilor MacKay expressed concern about the hours allowed for landscaping equipment, noting that 7
AM may be too early as children are still sleeping. She suggested that 8 AM would be more reasonable
and questioned why the exemption extends until 10 PM, advocating for a smaller time window.

Councilor Christensen agreed with concerns about the noise of landscaping equipment, stating that 10
PM is too late and the hours allowed should be reduced. He acknowledged that while he appreciates his
neighbors, he does not enjoy the noise from their equipment.

Councilor Bogdin asked for clarification on why base noise is excluded from the ordinance, noting that
many concerns raised through Open City Hall seemed to be related to this issue.

Mr. Sanders explained that measuring base noise is challenging due to the need for specialized
equipment, and there are no clear standards for determining what constitutes too much base. After
reviewing 31 noise ordinances, only one, from Orem, mentioned base noise, and Orem reported never
having used that provision for enforcement. While base noise can be disruptive, the ordinance allows for
violations to be based on evidence or testimony of disturbance. If someone can prove that base noise is
causing a reasonable disturbance, it could still be considered a violation of the ordinance.

Councilor Bogdin asked how a Mayoral exemption is obtained.

Mr. Sanders explained that the mayoral exemptions in the ordinance require a written permit issued by
the mayor or their designee. However, the code does not specify the details of the process, making it an
administrative matter. He asked Mr. Henderson if he had any comments on the current process for
issuing these permits.

Scott Henderson, CAO, explained that requests for noise ordinance exemptions often go through the
special event process. When such requests are made, they are forwarded to the mayor’s office to review
the specifics and make a decision.

Councilor Bogdin asked for clarification on the section of the exemptions that addresses concerns about
loud music being played while cleaning the stadium. She wanted to confirm that the noise ordinance
prevents this from happening.

Mr. Sanders clarified that cleaning the stadium would not be a valid reason to play loud music through
the PA system, as the noise ordinance only allows such activities during official inter-school athletic
competitions (Subsection B). For non-competition activities like cleanup, Subsection C specifies that the
PA system cannot be used past 10 PM without a permit from the mayor. He further explained that if
music were to be played before 7 AM or after 10 PM, a permit would be required.

Chair Garrett asked Mr. Henderson if the mayor’s office could clarify in the permit process that
exemptions for post-game activities or early morning noise (such as music) would be sensitive to the
concerns raised by neighbors. He emphasized the need to address these issues when granting
exemptions.

Mr. Henderson expressed appreciation for the changes made to provide clarity. He acknowledged that if
there were a request to extend the normal time for loud music during cleanup, the mayor’s office would
likely have concerns and would probably not approve such a request.

Chair Garrett opened public comment.

4         An ordinance amending Provo City codes regarding building height and unit size
          regulations in the General Downtown (DT1) zone. Citywide application. (PLOTA20240308)
          1:10:06

     Motion:      An implied motion to approve Ordinance 2025-10, as currently constituted, has been
                  made by council rule.

Aaron Ardmore, Planning Supervisor, presented. He said the item was discussed in the study session,
which involves adjusting building height regulations in the downtown area. Currently, the DT1 zone
allows a height of 100 feet, and the DT2 zone allows 108 feet. Developers in the DT1 zone have been
seeking opportunities to increase this height. The proposal ties the height increase to the amount of for-
sale housing in a project, reflecting a goal of the City Council and administration. The plan allows for
incremental increases in height based on the percentage of for-sale units. Additionally, it proposes
extending the reduced minimum average unit size (from 800 to 600 square feet) on Center Street to also
apply to University Avenue within the Downtown zone. This change aims to provide more flexibility for
high-density projects. The Planning Commission recommended approval, supporting the gradual
increase and the benefits to the city. The applicant was also available for questions.

Councilor Bogdin asked Mr. Ardmore if the requirement for owner occupancy for the first year of
ownership had been changed, specifically whether it was sufficient to ensure the requirement would be
recurring. Mr. Ardmore responded that the proposed language still stood as is, and Councilor Bogdin
clarified that any changes would need to be included in a motion.

Councilor Bogdin asked the applicant why the percentage of for-sale housing starts at 10% for up to 120
feet in height, suggesting that it could start at 20% since the additional height allows for a 20% increase.

Jeff Knighton, applicant, explained that the proposed plan was based on discussions with Aaron and Bill
Peperone, and they were comfortable with it. They did not specifically discuss whether the percentage
of for-sale housing should be 10% or 20%, but the proposal was designed to allow more flexibility by
increasing height to 120 feet. He mentioned that the reasoning for this height increase was related to
construction types and costs, as buildings above seven stories require different construction methods.
The idea of offering 10-20% for-sale housing was seen as a good way to balance housing needs with
building flexibility.

Councilor Bogdin asked Mr. Knighton about the type of for-sale housing envisioned for the project, and
he responded that it would likely include ground-level units like brownstone walk-ups or two-story
townhomes, along with potential condominiums on higher floors. When asked about mixing apartments
and condominiums in the same building, he confirmed that it could work, though the owner-occupancy
requirements complicate funding, particularly with lenders. He explained that the requirements could
make lending more difficult, especially for first-time homebuyers using FHA or VA loans, as these loans
often require specific ratios that might not align with the proposed units. Councilor Bogdin inquired
about the estimated number of units for the project, with Mr. Knighton estimating around 450 to 500
units for the entire block, with 10-20% of the units in the taller buildings being for-sale units. He clarified
that the 10-20% figure referred to the portion of units in buildings exceeding the 100-foot height, not
the entire project.

Councilor Christensen asked if the developer was comfortable with including language that would
require owner occupancy for the first year, recorded as part of the deed. This would mean that if
someone bought the property and sold it to another person, the new owner would also have to live at
the property for the first year.

Mr. Knighton expressed concerns about the owner-occupancy language, as it could limit lending options,
which are already challenging for for-sale units. He mentioned that adding such language might make it
more difficult to secure financing for the project, potentially hindering the creation and sale of the units.
He encouraged further discussion with the city and mentioned that there are ongoing efforts to improve
incentives for condominium projects, though he was unsure if the proposed language would ultimately
be beneficial.

Councilor Christensen emphasized the importance of balancing rental and homeownership in the city's
development. While recognizing the need for rental units, he argued that the city cannot allow
developers to solely rely on funding that favors rentals. He stated that homeownership is crucial and
must be prioritized, suggesting that without meaningful homeownership provisions in the deed—
particularly for the first year—he would not support the proposal. He expressed concern that, without
this provision, institutional investors could dominate the market, potentially undermining local citizens'
access to homeownership.

Councilor MacKay and Councilor Whipple discussed the implications of homeowner occupancy
requirements for FHA-backed loans. Councilor MacKay noted that after speaking with bankers and
developers, the concern was that lenders want the option for some units to be rented out, ensuring
mortgage payments are secured in case of job changes or relocation. Councilor Whipple confirmed that
the proposed percentages would likely not interfere with FHA loans, and they were working on language
to address the homeowner occupancy requirement. They both agreed that while they would like to add
further provisions, the current language should be sufficient for FHA loan compatibility.

Councilor Whipple referenced a concern raised in a previous meeting regarding how to enforce the
restriction on renting out units during the first year of ownership. She asked Brian to explain this issue
further.

Mr. Jones explained that the key issue is enforcing the restriction on renting the property during the first
year of ownership. He suggested that the concept should be framed as a "rental prohibition" rather than
an "owner occupancy requirement" because it is easier to enforce a ban on renting than to enforce
owner occupancy. He proposed that deed restrictions would prevent renting for the first year after
ownership changes. Additionally, he suggested adding clarifying language to the city code to ensure
rental licenses are not issued if they violate the deed restrictions, allowing for prosecution if the
property is rented during that time. This approach, he believes, would be more enforceable than trying
to prove whether the owner is living in the house.

Councilor Whipple asked if the deed restriction requirement could be included in the written agreement
with the city for the for-sale housing, instead of needing to add it to the city code. She clarified that the
city has the authority to impose conditions, like the deed restriction, when agreeing to such proposals.

Mr. Jones responded that the proposed language could be amended to specify that individual owner
occupancy is required for the first year of ownership, or after each change in ownership, in a residential
project. He suggested this slight amendment to ensure clarity and prevent future disputes about vested
rights and noted that this change would align with the enforcement mechanism and agreements.

     Motion:      Councilor Whipple made motion to amend proposed language ownership after each
                  change in ownership. Seconded by Councilor Christensen.

With no discussion on the motion, Chair Garrett called for a vote.

     Vote:        The motion passed 7:0 with Councilors Bogdin, Christensen, Garrett, Handley, Hoban,
                  MacKay, and Whipple in favor.
