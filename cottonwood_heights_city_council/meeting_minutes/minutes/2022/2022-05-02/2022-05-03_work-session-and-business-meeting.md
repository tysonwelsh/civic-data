3.      SALT LAKE CITY PUBLIC UTILITIES ADVISORY COMMITTEE REPORT.

Salt Lake City Public Utilities Advisory Committee Member, Ted Boyer was present to share the
Advisory Committee Report. He reported that Cottonwood Heights receives water from Salt Lake
City and there is a mechanism in place to ensure that the City has a voice in affairs related to the
utilities. That was the reason for the Salt Lake City Public Utilities Advisory Committee.
Mr. Boyer stated that he has served on the Committee for seven years. The Committee consists of
nine members, with three residing outside of the Salt Lake City limits, but within service districts
that receive water from Salt Lake City. That included Cottonwood Heights, the City of Holladay,
and Millcreek. The Advisory Committee could make recommendations to the Mayor of Salt Lake
City as well as the City Council and Public Utilities Department. The duties were to review capital
improvements for water and sewer as well as review maintenance and operations. Rate
adjustments were recommended, as appropriate, and there could also be recommendations related
to Legislation.

Mr. Boyer reported that his term on the Salt Lake City Public Utilities Advisory Committee will
end in January 2023. The Mayor and City Council would need to make a recommendation to the
Public Utilities Department and the Mayor of Salt Lake City. There was no rush because there is
a provision in the law that states that a sitting member of the Committee can continue to serve until
a replacement is appointed. In terms of possible replacements, he believed it would be appropriate
to appoint someone with experience in water, water rights, water resources, budgeting, or rate
setting. Alternatively, the replacement could have legal, accounting, or business experience.
Mr. Boyer was recommended by former Cottonwood Heights Mayor Kelvyn Cullimore. He was
then appointed by former Salt Lake City Mayor Ralph Becker. Mr. Boyer shared information
about his experience and referenced some of the work he had done in the past.

Mr. Boyer spoke to the Director of Salt Lake City Public Utilities and the water outlook was
expected to be similar to last year. The moisture content of the soil is higher, but drought
conditions continue. The recommendation for the coming year was Stage 2 Water Conservation.
No stringent rules or restrictions would be imposed. Mr. Boyer reported that he had drastically
cut back on his personal water usage. Mayor Weichers noted that Cottonwood Heights’ water
comes from Deer Creek Reservoir. It was his understanding that Deer Creek filled up to a fairly
normal level last year. Mr. Boyer clarified that it had not quite filled up to normal levels. He
overviewed some of the nearby water sources. The proposed water rates were shared. Water rates
increased in the last few years and the proposal this year was an increase of 15%. In terms of
dollars per month per customer, the increase was not as intense as it sounded.

Council Member Birrell wondered if Cottonwood Heights should think about a new metering
system that would allow residents to be more mindful about water use. It was noted that in the
current budget year, money was earmarked for the replacement of meters. Users could log in to
check usage on a weekly or monthly basis and make adjustments accordingly. Mr. Boyer stated

an enjoyable experience and he was able to meet a lot of interesting people.

Mr. Brace believed that residents of Cottonwood Heights deserve a reliable fiber network. In

it was possible to fast-track the first customer served. Google Fiber could be running within eight
months. This would bring competition to the market in terms of speed, price, and customer service.
Google Fiber would deliver best-in-class internet. Ms. Church shared a graph from PC Magazine,
an Independent Reader’s Choice Award magazine. Google Fiber was described as the gold
standard amongst ISPs. Google Fiber had a 20-point advantage over the next ISP on the list, with
a net promoter score above 50.

Ms. Church explained that the American Customer Satisfaction Index ("ACSI") was another
industry standard survey that went out. Google Fiber was ranked first across 22 categories. Speed
was one factor that was important to consider but other reasons for the high score related to free
call center support (open all day every day with calls answered within 30 seconds or less), service
trucks available within 24 hours, and the transparent pricing structure. The 1 Gig service was
launched 10 years ago, and Google Fiber had held firm at $70 per month for that product offering.
On the residential side, a multi-gigabyte service was introduced, which was 2 Gig. As household
demands continued to grow, there are capabilities within the network infrastructure to increase to
10 Gigabits per second without any changes to the construction. Only the electronics on the
endpoints would need to be altered. Google Fiber would continue to invest in product
development. Mr. Brace added that both residential plans come with a terabyte of Cloud storage.

It was noted that not all internet and pricing are equal. With Google Fiber, there would be
consistent communication with residents. For instance, before construction begins in a
neighborhood, door hangers with information would be shared. The marketing was all done by
Google Fiber as was the installation, ongoing repair, maintenance, and customer service. There
are no long-term contracts and there are no provider change fees. Ms. Church reported that there
is no introductory pricing that escalates over time and no surcharges imposed for modems or Wi-
Fi routers. The $70 price point encompasses everything. Additionally, there are no construction,
no installation, or activation fees.

Mr. Brace discussed digital equity. He explained that Google Fiber intends to close the digital
divide. More residents than ever rely on computers in the home for education, employment, and
entertainment. Google Fiber wants to be a community partner by developing community

The process was overviewed, which began with a design and engineering phase. That usually
takes four to six months. However, the design and engineering were already complete for
Cottonwood Heights. After that, the construction phase could start, which normally took between
18 to 24 months depending on various factors. Four cities were currently operational, which
included Provo, Salt Lake City, South Salt Lake, and the City of Holladay. Ms. Church explained
that construction is never truly complete in a city because there is always growth, private
easements, and areas that were previously unbuildable that can be utilized.

Ms. Church explained that there was a toolkit of proven deployment methods. This included both
traditional underground boring and shallow trenching. The goal was to use these tools to minimize
disruption to the community, existing utilities, and infrastructure, and deliver an end-product that
was resilient and reliable. When designing and deploying, four main principles were considered:

    •   Lifetime:
            o The plan should have an expected lifetime of no less than 20 years. That factored
               in proactive and scheduled maintenance on a routine basis to ensure the quality of
               the sealant and that the trench remained in a good state. This was done at no cost.
    •    Survivability:
            o The network needed to be able to survive routine road maintenance events and not
               increase any of the city resources or risks. The shallow trenching profile was 8-12
               inches deep and that was enough to clear any mill and pave initiatives and routine
               road maintenance. Large overhauls would require a locally based Google Fiber
               team to work with the city to proactively relocate ahead of the projects.
    •   Constructability:
            o The intention was for Google Fiber to be easily constructible and meet all of the
               city permit requirements. The in-house expertise was combined with a portfolio of
               partners to design, construct, and operate the network. With that came a robust
               quality of program during and after construction.
    •   Repairability:
            o Google Fiber held itself to high standards. When there were multi-customer
               outages, the repairs were addressed in four hours. When there were single-
               customer outages, the repairs were addressed in eight hours.

Finance and Administrative Services Director, Scott Jurges reported that the item before the
Council was a Tentative Budget. It would be made available for residents to review and comment

noted that a few items in the tentative budget were estimates, such as the property tax rate. The
final property tax rate and final revenue amount would not be ready until June 10, 2022.

Changes were made to the Tentative Budget since the Council last reviewed the document during
a Work Session two weeks ago. Those changes were highlighted in yellow. Once the Tentative
Budget was adopted, it would be on the Cottonwood Heights website and shared via social media.
Hard copies of the Tentative Budget could also be printed and shared. Mr. Jurges reported that
the Tentative Budget was developed through many meetings. For instance, the Council Retreat
was held in January 2022. There were also discussions with different Department Directors, the
Benefits and Compensation subcommittee, and the Budget subcommittee.

Mr. Jurges reported that the City has five different funds, but the General Fund was the main fund
and most of the revenue flows through it before being transferred elsewhere. In the General Fund,
the expected ending Fund Balance for the current year (June 30, 2022) was $8,513,270, which was
an increase of $584,974 from the beginning Fund Balance and was $2,665,000 better than the
current adopted budget. There were several reasons for that, such as higher sales tax revenues.
The $8,513,270 represented 33.1% of the 2021/2022 budgeted revenues. The City was required
by State Statute to hold a minimum of 5% and an additional 1% by City Ordinance. Mr. Jurges
noted that this was a total of 6% or $1,541,592. In addition, there was a reserve for Compensated
Absences that was $595,179 each year. That would leave approximately $6,376,499 available for
appropriation at the beginning of the new 2022/2023 budget year.

The Revenues Section of the General Fund was overviewed. Mr. Jurges reported that Property
Taxes were budgeted at $8,203,450 and that included an estimated new growth of $50,000. On
June 10, 2022, the final new growth revenue numbers would be submitted by the County Auditor.
The current certified tax rate was 0.001740 and that would be updated by the County Auditor.
Mr. Jurges explained that Sales Tax Revenue had been budgeted at $7.2 million, but last year, the
City received $7,894,215 in sales tax revenue. In the current year, it was anticipated that the City
would receive $8.5 million. Moving forward, the City would estimate $8.8 million, which would
be a $300,000 increase. He felt that was still a conservative estimate.

Additional revenues were discussed, such as the County Option Highway Sales Tax. It was
budgeted at $650,000. Energy Use Tax was built into the budget at $2.2 million, which was
$250,000 more than the current budget. The Fee in Lieu of Property Tax (Vehicles) was built into
the budget at $425,000 and the Franchise Taxes (Cable TV) was built into the budget at $300,000,
