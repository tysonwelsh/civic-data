3.      A presentation regarding the FY2023 Budget- Administrative Services (Facilities,
        Information Systems and Justice Court) (22-016) (0:20:48)

John Borget, Administrative Services Director, introduced the item. ReAnnun Newton, Justice
Court Administrator, presented. Revenues for the justice court have trended downwards for the
last five years. Various pandemic restrictions have limited some of the operations and tools they
could use. Councilor Rachel Whipple asked whether virtual meetings would allow them to save
money on transport. Ms. Newton said they are limited to 2 hours per week for virtual hearings
with inmates—due to this time limit, there is a bit of a backlog. Councilor George Handley asked
about their revenue forecast. Ms. Newton said they are higher this year than last at this point in
the 2022 fiscal year; they have already budgeted for transport, so when the usual process resumes
they should be in an okay position budget-wise.

Dick Blackham, Facilities Division Director, presented. He addressed the facilities operations,
the new city hall, and the new airport terminal as they relate to facilities. He highlighted in
particular the budget for the new city hall. Increased square footage in the new facility will also
result in increased facilities costs. They will go through a new RFP process when the new
building is almost finished in order to allow applicants to view the space and types of materials
and finishes. Mr. Blackham reviewed the line items of the facilities budget and the major
changes to costs between the current facility and the new building. Councilor George Handley
noted that it is a 3x increase in size from the old building and only about double the costs, which
seems to illustrate some of the efficiency of the new building. Councilor Katrice MacKay asked
if the building is opening in May 2022, whether they would need funds budgeted for utility costs
in the current fiscal year. Mr. Borget indicated that current utilities are part of the construction
cost; they anticipate moving into the building at the end of June, so they should be fine operating
with the current budget for the current fiscal year.

help the Council better respond to residents’ questions about the actions of state officials and
how this affects Provo. Mr. Decker reviewed some of the basic information about Provo’s water
system and water storage. Culinary water systems are pressurized systems; in contrast,
wastewater systems are a gravity flow system and often parts of the pipe are empty. Water

Mr. Decker shared information about 16 wells operated in Provo and the spring collection boxes.
He shared a slide illustrating the water levels in one particular well, which showed that the static
water level has decreased over the last 30 years. Most places in Utah County show the aquifer on

to plan for long and extensive droughts.

Mr. Decker reviewed Provo’s water rights, including surface storage in Deer Creek and
Jordanelle Reservoirs and water rights on the Provo River (which requires culinary treatment).
Mr. Decker referenced a 1921 decree in which the city received most of its Provo River water
rights. He shared a graph that illustrated Provo’s water rights compared to water consumption. In
2000, the state set a 25% water conservation goal for the State. Provo residents have been doing
very well with water conservation, even without these numbers not having been adjusted for
population—water consumption is declining even as the population is growing.

Councilor Shannon Ellsworth asked what accounted for the reduction in water use last year. Mr.
Decker explained that residents were asked to use less water; there was not an official mandate.
Residents have typically responded well when they are asked to conserve water—about 400
million gallons were conserved in Provo in 2021. The main concern comes from the availability
of water rights at the times they are needed. Mr. Decker shared some projections on snowpack as
well to illustrate the impacts to spring water sources. He also highlighted some of the differences
between the treatment of surface water versus ground water.

Mr. Decker distributed a copy of a 1915 Provo River Commissioners’ report on the river. Mr.
Decker noted the geography referenced in the report—“Provo Valley” refers to Wasatch County.
In 1915, water was used 6 times before it reached Utah Lake—it was used twice in each valley
with flood irrigation and groundwater wells. Several of those uses have been eliminated since
flood irrigation is no longer a common practice. Presently, water is only used three times
between the headwaters of Provo River to when it reaches Utah Lake. Mr. Decker noted that this
was significant—the number of times we use water is an important data point in conversations
about water conservation. Utilization of surface water storage has reduced the number of times
we can use water and it also impacts groundwater. If Provo is worried about Utah Lake and
Great Salt Lake drying up, Mr. Decker thought it vital to pay attention to groundwater systems,
which play a contributing role in how water reaches those bodies of water. Surface water is
typically insufficient as a source because storage capacities are relatively small.

Mr. Decker reviewed the last five water years and noted that the extra water in 2017 and 2019
was not stored but went to Utah Lake due to insufficient storage capacity in reservoirs. He

7.      A presentation from the Foothills Protection Committee regarding the Provo
        Foothills Trail Plan. (22-024) (3:25:49)

Doug Robins, Parks and Recreation Assistant Director, presented. He noted the geography of the
Provo area, with the river, lake, canyons, valley, and urban parks. Trails are a linking element to
these expansive natural areas. Provo and surrounding communities are a very active population;
Mr. Robins highlighted the community and economic benefits of having these trails. Provo has
year-round opportunities with good spaces for snowshoeing, cross-country skiing, and mountain
biking. Poor planning results in unsustainable trails that are hard to maintain, don’t follow proper
contours, and can be unsafe. As more people travel in the backcountry, it requires a higher level
of planning and management to accommodate that growth in an appropriate manner.

The Utah Valley Trail Alliance helped develop the Provo Foothills Trail Master Plan. Mr.
Robins thanked Brandon Plewe, a cartographer and BYU professor, who helped spearhead this
trail planning effort. This plan is intended to be incorporated into the Forest Service’s area trails
plan and has also involved their collaboration. The plan objectives were sustainability, open to
the widest variety of users, interconnected with surrounding areas, and within legal parameters.
The next step is to adopt the plan, which will provide clear direction, secure public access,
enhance continuity, and support the Forest Service’s planning documentation requirements. After
funding the plan, official survey work can be conducted. The Provo Parks and Recreation
Department plans to include and coordinate capital improvements at city trail facilities to interact
with the Forest Service trails and to continue their partnership with the Forest Service. After the
plan is adopted and funded, then the next step is to execute the plan—this includes identifying
trail routing, abandoning and restoring unauthorized and unsustainable pathways, construction
phasing, and USFS planning and environmental documentation.

Mr. Robins noted this plan was not final; it memorializes the current situation and has involved
citizen input for inclusion in the Forest Service plan. Although the plan involves a lot of property
that is not part of the City’s trail system, it represents community support for the plan which will
interface with Provo’s trails.

Councilor George Handley has been involved with those conversations and planning efforts for a
long time. He thanked Mr. Robins and invited Mr. Plewe to comment if he would like. Mr.
Handley asked if they have worked with private landowners. Mr. Robins indicated that they
worked with private landowners to involve them in the public education process; they also
wanted to understand the concerns of landowners. Mr. Plewe reiterated that most of the land is
Forest Service property, but there are some significant areas that are in the city limits or on
property owned by the City that would be the primary responsibility of the city. This is a

9.      A discussion regarding the hiring process and committee for selecting a Municipal
        Council Executive Director. (22-027) (4:27:00)

John Magness, Interim Council Executive Director, presented. He outlined two processes for
selecting a new Council Executive Director, which each involve different time commitments
from Councilors. He noted that all the Councilors could be involved in either process; it would
