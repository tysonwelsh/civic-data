At the October 5, 2021 Council meeting, Community Development Director Mike
DeSimone addressed the Council regarding the proposed code amendments.

RECOMMENDATION
Staff recommended that the Planning Commission recommend approval to the
Municipal Council of the proposed amendments to Chapters 17.08, 17.11, 17.37, and
17.62 of the Land Development Code.

REQUEST
This is a proposed amendment to the Land Development Code (LDC) permitting
Community Agriculture and Urban Agriculture throughout the City. More specifically,
the proposed language defines the minimum standards for keeping chickens, domestic
fowl, bees, miniature, and larger livestock on residential properties. This amendment also

                                                                                  5 | Page
             Logan Municipal Council Minutes ~ Logan, Utah ~ October 19, 2021
clarifies the distinction in types of Agriculture, namely the differences between
Community Agriculture and general Agriculture.
Moreover, the purpose is to enable residents to keep animals and bees on their property
while minimizing the negative impacts on adjoining properties.

This amendment is in response to inquiries we regularly receive about keeping chickens
on residential lots and a lack of clear guidance in the City's current code structure.

BACKGROUND
There is a disconnect between the City's animal regulations (Chapter 6A) and the Land
Development Code on keeping animals or bees.

Chapter 17.37: Additional Development Standards

Domestic Fowl, Beekeeping, Livestock

Urban Agriculture - Purpose:

Establish minimum standards for keeping domestic fowl (chickens), bees, &
 livestock in residential areas (Urban Agriculture). Clarify the distinction in types of
“Agriculture” that are appropriate for Logan – community gardens, community-supported
gardens, market gardens, commercial/industrial agriculture. Minimize negative impacts
of keeping fowl, bees, & livestock on adjoining Properties. Attempt to balance the “rights
& desires” of all landowners – those owning animals and those living next do.

Logan Municipal Code 6A.06.030 - Limits on Animals for Family Food Production
states:

The type and number of animals for family food production that may be kept is limited
and governed exclusively by the city's land-use ordinances and regulations set forth in the
city ordinance. It is presumed that if a type of animal is not expressly allowed as either a
permitted or conditional use in a specific land use zone as determined by city ordinances
then that animal is deemed prohibited.

PROHIBITED: Animals for family food production Definition of Animals for Family
Food Production includes Chickens, Bees, livestock, etc. Governed by City’s Land Use
Ordinances – Land Development Code - If not Expressly Permitted in a specific zone,
then Expressly Prohibited

Land Development Code Chapter 17.62: Definitions states:

"Agriculture" means the production, keeping, or maintenance, for sale, lease or personal
use, of plants and animals useful to society, including and not limited to: forages and sod
crops; grains and seed crops; dairy animals and dairy products; poultry and poultry
products; livestock, including beef, cattle, sheep, swine, horses, ponies, mules, or goats
or any mutations or hybrids thereof, including the breeding and grazing of any or all such
animals; bees and apiary products; fur animals; trees and forest products; fruits of all
kinds, including grapes, nuts, and berries; vegetables; nursery, floral, ornamental, and
greenhouse products; or lands devoted to a soil conservation or forestry management
program. Agriculture is broadly defined - both industrial-scale Ag (commercial feedlot
– 100 cows) and residential-scale Ag (4 chickens). The definition includes animals
"traditionally" kept for utility purposes –chickens, bees, livestock

Chapter 6A of the Logan Municipal Code defers the keeping of animals for family food
production to the Land Development Code. The definition of agriculture in the LDC is
relatively broad as it includes a wide range of ag uses from large-scale commercial
operations down to individually scaled agriculture. Under the current use tables,
agriculture is a permitted use in the NR-2, NR-4, RC, GW, CS, IP, and AP zones. Under
this definition and with our current use charts, hypothetically, a property owner could put
in a commercial dairy in the Cliffside area yet a property owner living in the NR-6 zone
cannot raise chickens for family food production.

Contrary to some of the comments you received for the September 9, 2021 public
hearing, the purpose of these regulations is to clarify where and how various types of
agriculture uses should be permitted throughout the City.

The first element of this proposal is the proposed changes to the applicable definitions in
17.62: Definitions where the definition of Agriculture is modified to referenced
commercial, and a definition of Community Agriculture and Urban Agriculture are
included to reference smaller-scaled agricultural related activities for food coops,
personal use, etc.

"Agriculture" means the production, keeping, or maintenance, for commercial purposes,
of plants and animals useful to society, including and not limited to: forages and sod
crops; grains and seed crops; dairy animals and dairy products; poultry and poultry
products; livestock, including beef, cattle, sheep, swine, horses, ponies, mules, or goats or
any mutations or hybrids thereof, including the breeding and grazing of any or all such
animals; bees and apiary products; fur animals; trees and forest products; fruits of all
kinds, including grapes, nuts, and berries; vegetables; nursery, floral, ornamental, and
greenhouse products; or lands devoted to a soil conservation or forestry management
program.

Community Agriculture means crop production, horticulture, floriculture, or viticulture,
and which may include facilities for the sale of produce, plants, and/or flowers produced
or grown on-site. The phrase "agriculture, community" is lower in scale than
"agriculture" and more restricted in its operations. Community Agriculture includes
composting (of agricultural byproducts produced on-site for use on-site) and seasonal
produce stands as accessory uses. Community Agriculture is separated into three
categories:

A. Community Gardens are gardens that are maintained and cultivated by non-profit
entities or groups of individuals who engage in the activity for their own benefit;
B. Small community-supported Farms are farms that are less than two acres in area, in
which produce, plants, and/or flowers are grown and harvested on behalf of a community
of members ("shareholders") who pledge financial support in advance of the growing
season; and
C. Market Gardens are gardens up to one-half acre in the area in which produce, plants,
and/or flowers are grown for off-site distribution and sale.

Urban Agriculture means the production of food within an urban area on a scale that is
for personal use and consumption. Urban agriculture can also include animal husbandry
(e.g., breeding and raising livestock), beekeeping, aquaculture (e.g., fish farming),
aquaponics (e.g., integrating fish farming and agriculture), and non-food products such as
producing seeds, cultivating seedlings, and growing flowers.

The change to the definition of agriculture moves it in the direction of referencing
commercial agriculture while the others are new definitions recognizing a smaller scale,
more urban agriculture for various groups or individual use. There is also a difference in
scale between community agriculture and urban agriculture as community ag is centered
around a larger involvement by more people in raising plants or food whereas urban ag is
generally a private individual raising plants, animals, and vegetables for their own
consumption with some secondary distribution. There are currently a number of
community gardens in Logan providing food for residents unable to grow it at their own
residence. Both types of agriculture are important and encourage residents to raise their
own food provided impacts to adjacent owners are minimized.

The third element is the proposed language in 17.37 regulating types of livestock and
their operational standards. In preparing the revised language, I surveyed a number of
jurisdictions for precedent examples on regulating chickens, animals & bees including
Brigham City, Murray, Providence, North Logan, Roy, American Fork, Millcreek, Cedar
City, Farmington, Clearfield, Springville, Spanish Fork, Draper, Smithfield, Provo,
Orem, West Valley and Bountiful.

Generally, the common threads in most approaches include a statement of purpose, what
zones they are permitted, numbers/densities, location on the property, types of
enclosures, setbacks from property lines and structures, sanitation, roosters, slaughtering,
and permitting. The revised draft language being presented to the Planning Commission
on 9/23/21 addresses these areas of concern except for permitting. We do not want to
require a permit to keep animals. Regarding beekeeping, I also reached out to the local
individuals who commented on the first draft and expressed their levels of experience and
expertise in beekeeping. Their comments have been incorporated into the revised draft.

Proposed changes to Land Development Code - summarized below and attached.

17.37.080 (chickens) - standards for permitting domestic fowl on residential lots based on
lot sizes, existing residential development, lot requirements (setbacks), chicken coop/run
requirements, and nuisance elements. The one area that remains a point of discussion is
determining the appropriate number of chickens to be located on a residential property.
The average among most similarly sized jurisdictions is six (6) chickens per residential
lot. Some of the smaller cities permit greater numbers while some of the larger cities
permit fewer numbers. I also modified the language permitting roosters, peacocks, or
similar animals on larger lots to be prohibited on residential lots.

17.37.090 (beekeeping) - standards for permitting beekeeping on residential lots based on
lot sizes, registration with the State, lot requirements, existing residential development,
and setbacks from adjoining parcels.

17.37.100 (miniature livestock)- standards for permitting miniature livestock on
residential lots based on lot sizes and minimum lot area available for animals, health and

sanitation requirements, zoning, residential development, and setbacks from adjoining
parcels.

17.37.110 (large livestock) - standards for permitting large livestock (horses, cows, goats,
sheep) on residential lots based on lot sizes and minimum lot area available for animals,
health and sanitation requirements, zoning, residential development, and setbacks from
adjoining parcels.

The fourth element is the proposed language in 17.37 addressing community agriculture
and the uses associated with it.

17.37.120 (Community Agriculture)- operational standards for community gardens,
community-supported gardens, and market gardens to help minimize or reduce potential
impacts to neighboring properties.

STAFF RECOMMENDATION AND SUMMARY
The proposed changes to the Land Development Code are in response to requests we
have received from residents regarding raising chickens in residential areas. We have also
responded to issues or complaints regarding livestock with limited standards to fall back
on. There are many areas of Logan that are suitable for raising many types of animals or
livestock and the proposed language provides minimum operational standards to help
minimize nuisance problems with adjacent property owners. Generally, complaints come
down to how well an owner maintains their property and manages their animals by
controlling noise, odors, feed, manure, and rodents. Staff would recommend that the
Planning Commission forwarded a recommendation of approval to the Council for their
consideration.

GENERAL PLAN
The proposed amendments are minor and are consistent with the General Plan.
