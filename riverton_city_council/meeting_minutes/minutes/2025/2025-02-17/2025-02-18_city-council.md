       Short-Term Rental Discussion

Ryan Carter, City Attorney, discussed potential changes to Riverton’s short-term rental
regulations, prompted by concerns from residents. He referenced Murray City’s recently
adopted ordinance and asked the Council to consider whether they wanted to adopt a similar
approach to Murray’s or maintain the existing structure.

Councilmember McDougal asked Mr. Carter to clarify how long property owners must live on-
site. Mr. Carter said that property owners must live on-site for at least 185 nights per year if
they operate a short-term rental. Additionally, rentals are limited to 150 nights per year. If a
property is solely used for short-term rentals without an owner living there, it is in violation of
City code, and enforcement action would be taken upon receiving evidence.

While Councilmember McDougal supported the ability for homeowners to generate extra
income through short-term rentals, he also wanted to ensure that neighborhoods did not turn

Councilmember Buroker emphasized that the most critical aspect of the short-term rental
ordinance is its enforceability. If issues arise and the City can effectively address them, then the
ordinance is functioning as intended. As long as the residency requirement of 185 nights per
year remains in place, the ordinance provides a clear standard for enforcement.

After the discussion, Councilmembers were not interested in changing the current ordinance.


         City Noise Ordinance and Common Code Enforcement Issues

Chief Shane Taylor explained that law enforcement and code enforcement frequently address
nuisance and noise ordinance violations, with common issues including golf cart violations and
off-road vehicle infractions, such as dirt bikes and ATVs. Other prevalent code violations involve
operating without a business license, animals running at large, weed control and property
maintenance, improper storage of personal property, and unlawful parking, which remains a
daily enforcement issue.

Mr. Carter explained that a resident challenged the City’s noise ordinance, which prohibits motor
vehicles within 800 feet of a dwelling, after code enforcement responded to complaints about his
children riding mini bikes on an oval track in his backyard. The City maintains that mini bikes
generate significant noise and could be considered a nuisance if used continuously. Even
without the 800-foot rule, the general noise prohibition would still apply, requiring proof of
sustained disturbance. The resident received warnings, but enforcement is limited without clear
evidence. While minor exceptions to the ordinance could be considered, they would not change
this case.


         4200 West Pond

Cary Necaise, Public Works Director, explained that the 4200 West pond project explores three
options for expanding water storage and potential public amenities. The existing pond holds 31
acre-feet, and the proposed options include: (1) expanding storage by 21 acre-feet with minimal
changes; (2) adding a second pond with a walking trail and landscaping; and (3) including a
fishing feature, which would require deeper excavation and a lift station. Costs range from
$1.1M for basic expansion to $1.4M for a landscaped fishing pond. Impact fees are expected to
cover costs within 1.5 years. While amenities enhance public use, they reduce storage capacity
and add maintenance costs.

Councilmember Buroker said that the primary objective of the 4200 West pond project was to
increase water retention capacity. She raised concerns about potential privacy issues for
neighboring homes if a trail were built at an elevated level. Additionally, access from the nearby
park would require crossing the canal, adding logistical challenges. While making the space
more visually appealing is desirable, she expressed reluctance to sacrifice significant water
storage capacity for amenities.

Councilmember McDougal reiterated that the primary goal of the 4200 West pond project was
water retention, and also expressed reluctance to reduce capacity for additional amenities. He
questioned the need for another fishing pond, given that one already exists in the City. Another
key concern was the long-term maintenance of new amenities, as multiple parks are already

Councilmember McCay suggested modifying the first option to retain the additional water
storage while incorporating some landscaping, benches, and trees. To address privacy
concerns, she proposed shifting the northern pond closer to the property line and placing

Mayor Staggs inquired about the potential impact of using TRCC funds on the project’s funding
structure, specifically whether adding amenities would limit the use of secondary water impact
fees. Mr. Hicks clarified that secondary impact fees could only be used for increasing pond
storage capacity, not for amenities. However, TRCC funds and park impact fees could
potentially cover beautification efforts, such as landscaping and recreational features. He
estimated that the excavation and expansion costs—approximately $1 million—could be funded
through impact fees, while the additional enhancements could be covered by other sources.

Councilmember Haymond asked about the estimated maintenance costs for the proposed pond
and potential liability concerns if it were to fail. Mr. Necaise explained that the state conducts
annual dam inspections to ensure compliance with safety regulations. He noted that any
modifications, including moving the pond closer to residences, would require state approval.
The inspection process ensures that structural integrity is maintained, reducing the risk of
failure.

Councilmember Pierucci inquired whether the estimated cost included a bridge over the canal
for access. Mr. Necaise explained that a bridge was factored into the estimate but was unsure
whether it covered both the canal crossing and the overpass. He noted the cost for a bridge was
$75,000 but needed clarification on whether this was for one or both crossings.

Mayor Staggs emphasized that functionality should be the top priority, noting that the goal of the
secondary water ponds is to maintain at least a couple of days' worth of flow for users. He also
pointed out that secondary water quality has been a long-standing concern among residents
and acknowledged the City's efforts to improve it through filtration, reducing micron levels at
pump stations, and increasing pressurization.


         Business License Code Updates

Mr. Carter presented an overview of proposed updates to the business license code,
emphasizing that the goal was to modernize and streamline the existing regulations. Key
changes included requiring all forms to be submitted online rather than using City-provided
paper forms. Additionally, businesses would now need to provide EIN numbers and tax ID
numbers to verify proper state registration, addressing past issues where businesses operated
without state approval. Another significant change involved removing unnecessary or outdated
provisions. The issuance and renewal of business licenses were also clarified, ensuring that
renewal dates align with the original issuance date, maintaining the City's current practice. A
lengthy table detailing different fees for various business types was also eliminated, as it was no
longer in use; instead, business license fees will follow the City’s fee schedule, which currently
sets them at zero.

Mayor/Council Reports

Councilmember Haymond reported on a sewer board meeting discussing PFAS, industrial
waterproofing chemicals under EPA review. Currently measured in parts per trillion, potential
regulations could require costly upgrades to the sewer treatment plant, converting it into a full-
scale water treatment facility at an estimated $180 million. This could increase sewer bills from
$33 to $150 per month. The board is monitoring developments, hoping further research leads to
balanced regulations.

plan in coordination with Riverton City, with a public comment period expected in early March.

highlighted a newsletter article on the Old Dome Meeting Hall, which is gaining recognition for
quality art exhibits, crediting Vicky Wartman for her efforts in curating outstanding shows.
Additionally, she mentioned the grand opening of the Burger King on 12600 South and 1830
West, praising its design as a successful compromise that blends historic Riverton aesthetics

Discussion/Action Items

         Water Rate Tiers

Mr. Hicks set the stage for discussing water rate adjustments by referring to previous
conversations in strategic planning meetings. He mentioned that the Council had given
feedback on the possibility of adding new rate tiers and possibly applying more dramatic
increases for larger water users. He expressed the intention to move forward with some minor
fee adjustments in the upcoming meeting and then pursue a more comprehensive study with a
consultant to refine the rates.

Mr. Geer walked the Council through four potential water rate options. His presentation is
include in the packet.

Councilmember Haymond raised a question about residents who never use secondary water
but still pay for it, particularly those who only use culinary water for lawn irrigation. Mr. Hicks
explained that a disconnection process is in place for those individuals, allowing them to be
charged a non-user rate of $4.50 if they prove they have fully disconnected from the secondary
water system.

Councilmember Haymond favored option three, which includes a $1.50 increase in the base
rate while maintaining tier adjustments. This approach would ensure the water enterprise fund
remains financially stable, with the option to readjust after the water study is completed.

Councilmember Pierucci expressed support for the fee structure because it charges based on
actual water usage, incentivizing more mindful consumption, unlike property taxes, which don't
reflect individual usage and also don't apply to schools and nonprofits. While he is generally
supportive of Option 3, he prefers a rate study to fully justify any significant changes, especially
given the likelihood of a property tax increase for water.

Councilmember Buroker asked if the current water usage fees were not enough to cover the
actual water costs. In response, Mayor Staggs explained that several factors have contributed
to this issue, such as the delay in the Greenwell coming online, a significant increase in water
usage (from 4,800 acre-feet to 6,200 acre-feet), and the resulting higher costs due to the Jordan
Valley Conservancy District's block two rates, which double the price per acre-foot when
consumption exceeds certain thresholds.

Councilmember Buroker said that she was leaning towards Option 3.

Councilmember McDougal expressed concern about raising fees for those using 10,000 gallons
or less, as they are already within reasonable consumption levels. He supported Option 2,
which shifts the cost burden to higher-use consumers, and stated that if an increase for lower-

         Truck Route Discussion

Mr. Hicks sought direction from the Council regarding the City's truck route code, specifically
whether to maintain the current code or make changes. A map of approved truck routes was
presented, highlighting that the City only owned half of 13800 South, with Bluffdale owning the
other half. Bluffdale officials had indicated they did not support designating their portion as a
truck route.

Councilmembers discussed the connectivity of 13800 South, noting that there was no direct
access for eastbound trucks to connect to Bangerter Highway unless they traveled through
other routes. The majority of Councilmembers agreed that 13800 South should not be a truck
route and staff agreed to bring this change back to the next City Council meeting.
